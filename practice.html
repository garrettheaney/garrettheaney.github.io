正Shōbōgenzō

The Treasury of the True Dharma Eye. A fascicle-by-fascicle outline. Each entry gives a synopsis, the central claim, and paraphrased passages.

Part one: the seventy-five-fascicle collection

Arranged in the order of Dōgen’s own seventy-five-fascicle compilation.

01現GenjōkōanActualizing the Fundamental Point

Central claimAwakening is not a possession; it is practice without a terminus.

Composed in 1233 as a letter to a lay disciple, Genjōkōan is Dōgen’s most widely read work. Its opening sets out two perspectives: from one, delusion and awakening, practice, birth and death, and buddhas and ordinary beings are distinct; from the other, none of these has a fixed self-nature. The way transcends both, yet flowers fall despite our attachment and weeds grow despite our aversion.

The central reversal follows. To advance the self and verify the myriad things through it is delusion; to let the myriad things advance and verify the self is awakening. Dōgen illustrates the point with images rather than syllogisms: a passenger who mistakes the motion of the boat for that of the shore, firewood that does not turn into ash but occupies its own before and after, and the moon reflected whole in a dewdrop without wetting or breaking it.

The closing passages warn against complacency. Fish and birds go as far as their element allows, but one that sought the limit of water or sky would lose its footing. In the final anecdote a monk fanning himself is told that the nature of wind is permanent and omnipresent and is asked why he fans; the master’s reply is to continue fanning. Realization is thereby identified with practice itself, not with a state that practice produces.

Paraphrased passages
To study the way is to study the self; to study the self is to forget the self; to forget the self is to be verified by the myriad things.
When the dharma has not yet matured in body and mind, one supposes it already sufficient; when it has, one perceives what is lacking.

Discernment. The second passage supplies a test for any claim of understanding: the sense of completion is a reason for suspicion.

02般Maka hannya haramitsuThe Great Perfection of Wisdom

Central claimWisdom is not a doctrine about emptiness but the activity of the whole person, responsive to every circumstance.

The fascicle comments on the opening of the Heart Sūtra, in which the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, practicing deep prajñāpāramitā, sees that the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness) are empty. The conventional reading treats emptiness as a verdict passed on the aggregates. Dōgen reverses this: the five aggregates are themselves prajñā, and clear seeing is a form of practice rather than a conclusion reached afterward.

Dōgen thereby opposes the scholastic tendency to treat the sūtra as a teaching to be comprehended and cataloged. Wisdom is not located in the intellect; the whole body-mind is prajñā, as is the world in which it acts. Emptiness does not signify nonexistence but the condition that allows each thing to be fully what it is.

The central image is taken from a verse of his teacher Rujing: the whole body is like a wind bell hanging in empty space, ringing prajñā for all alike, whichever direction the wind blows. The bell does not discriminate among winds; it is suspended, open, and responsive.

Paraphrased passages
The whole body is like a bell hung in empty space; whatever wind comes, it rings forth prajñā.
The five aggregates are not obstacles to wisdom; they are wisdom in activity.

Discernment. Dōgen rereads a canonical text against the habit of treating it as an idea. Understanding is demonstrated in response to circumstances, not in the quality of one’s explanation.

03性BusshōBuddha-Nature

Central claimBuddha-nature is neither possessed nor lacked; it is the whole of existence, including impermanence.

Busshō is among the longest fascicles. Dōgen takes the Nirvāṇa Sūtra statement that “all sentient beings have buddha-nature” and reads it differently from the usual sense of an inherent essence possessed by each being. Rendered as “all beings, the whole of being, is buddha-nature,” the sentence adds nothing to beings; their existence is itself the nature.

He supports the reading with classical cases. When the Fifth Ancestor told Huineng, a newcomer from the south, that southerners lack buddha-nature, Huineng replied that although persons may be northern or southern, buddha-nature has no north or south. In the case of Zhaozhou’s dog, the different answers given on different occasions are treated as living responses rather than doctrinal positions.

The most frequently cited passage identifies impermanence itself as buddha-nature, following Huineng. Dōgen thereby rejects the picture of an unchanging soul beneath the flux of phenomena: grasses, trees, and change are not obstacles to the nature but its expression. He adds that buddha-nature must be understood through the conditions of time, a link to Uji: it is not awaited but occupied.

Paraphrased passages
Buddha-nature is neither having nor lacking; it is the whole of being.
Impermanence is not opposed to buddha-nature; impermanence is buddha-nature.

Discernment. Dōgen guards against the oldest misreading, the idea of a permanent inner essence, by locating realization in the actual, changing present rather than in a private possession.

04學Shinjin gakudōLearning the Way with Body and Mind

Central claimThe way must be studied with body and mind together; neither alone is sufficient.

Dōgen distinguishes two aspects of study, body and mind, and denies that they can be separated. Study with the mind begins in the aspiration to practice and is renewed by it. Study with the body consists of sitting, posture, and the physical forms of training. Neither is optional, and neither is the real practice concealed behind the other.

He repeatedly cautions against equating “mind” with the discriminating, thinking faculty. In his usage mind includes the world, so that studying mind is not an inward withdrawal; conversely, the body is not an obstacle to be overcome but the place where practice occurs.

The fascicle thus excludes two familiar positions: a purely mental spirituality and a purely physical technique. Dōgen’s tone is uncompromising. Study of the way is not an activity to be fitted in after other concerns are settled but the organizing principle of the practitioner’s life.

Paraphrased passages
To study the way with the mind is not to follow the chatter of thought; to study it with the body is not to disregard the mind.
Body and mind are not two roads; the one road is walked with both.

Discernment. Dōgen rules out both purely mental and purely physical conceptions of practice; authentic practice involves the whole person.

05即Sokushin zebutsuThe Very Mind Is Buddha

Central claim“The very mind is Buddha” is misleading if read as a private inner self; it designates the entire activity of practice.

The slogan sokushin zebutsu is common in Zen, and Dōgen treats it as hazardous when read carelessly. One misreading holds that the ordinary thinking mind already is Buddha and requires no training; another posits an eternal spirit within the body that survives death. Dōgen rejects both, citing Nanyang Huizhong’s criticism of the doctrine of a permanent mind within a perishing body as a heterodox view.

In its place he proposes that mind be understood as the whole course of practice: arousing the aspiration, training, realizing the way, and entering nirvana. Each stage is “the very mind,” and each is Buddha. The phrase does not refer to something inside the practitioner but to the entire activity of buddhas practicing.

The fascicle is argumentative in tone, a defense of a phrase against its own popularity. Dōgen holds that the slogan is true in enactment, not as a belief; a teaching that assures its hearers of their completeness while requiring nothing of them falls under his critique.

Paraphrased passages
The very mind is not an object within the head; it is the whole of arousing the aspiration, practicing, realizing the way, and entering nirvana.
Belief in an eternal inner spirit is not the buddha way.

Discernment. A sound doctrine can become a false one when it is converted into a reassuring slogan.

06威Gyōbutsu igiThe Dignified Conduct of the Practicing Buddha

Central claimAwakening is enacted in concrete, dignified conduct; there is no buddha apart from practice.

The title refers to the deportment or dignified activity (igi) of the practicing buddha. Dōgen’s thesis, recurrent in his writing, is that there is no buddha who has completed practice and left it behind. Buddhas are those who practice, and practice is not preparation for something else; it is what buddhahood consists of.

The emphasis on igi reflects monastic training, in which the forms of walking, sitting, standing, eating, and bowing are not ornamental but are the physical shape of realization. A buddha does not hover above the body: the manner of standing and of lifting a bowl is part of the teaching.

Dōgen also opposes the picture of awakening as a single decisive event followed by anticlimax. Any such event is, on his account, an occurrence within practice rather than its conclusion, and the dignity of continuing practice is his subject.

Paraphrased passages
Buddhas do not cease practicing; practice is what a buddha is doing.
Walking, sitting, and standing are not decoration but the place where the teaching takes form.

Discernment. Conduct, not assertion, is the relevant evidence: how a person performs ordinary tasks shows the character of their practice.

07珠Ikka myōjuOne Bright Pearl

Central claimThe whole world is one bright pearl, and no explanation is required for it to be so.

The fascicle is built on a brief exchange. Xuansha states that the whole universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl and asks what use there is in understanding it. On the following day he asks a monk how he now understands it; when the monk repeats Xuansha’s own formulation, he is told that he is living in a ghost cave in a black mountain.

Dōgen does not read the exchange as a trick. It illustrates the difference between holding a concept and being the thing. The monk reproduces the words correctly but has converted the pearl into an idea; Xuansha’s rebuke is not that the words are wrong but that they are lifeless.

For Dōgen the bright pearl is not a metaphor for totality but each thing in its entirety, with nothing concealed. A pearl need not be understood to be bright. The fascicle indicates that a correct statement and a realized one differ, and that the difference lies not in vocabulary but in whether a person speaks from the pearl or about it.

Paraphrased passages
The whole universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl; what use is understanding?
To repeat correct words from the wrong standpoint is to dwell in a cave of ghosts.

Discernment. A correct answer, merely repeated, can be lifeless; the fascicle distinguishes real understanding from competent imitation.

08心Shin fukatokuThe Mind Cannot Be Got

Central claimMind cannot be grasped in the past, present, or future; when grasping ceases, mind is simply what is occurring.

The title is drawn from the Diamond Sūtra: past mind cannot be obtained, present mind cannot be obtained, future mind cannot be obtained. The fascicle turns on the story of Deshan, a scholar renowned for his command of that sūtra, who traveled south with his commentaries on his back. Stopping to buy a snack (tenshin, a pun on “refreshing the mind”) from an old woman selling cakes, he is asked which mind he intends to refresh, since the sūtra he knows so well says that none can be obtained.

Deshan cannot answer, and the old woman directs him to a teacher; tradition holds that he later burned his commentaries. Dōgen takes the statement seriously and applies it practically. Mind is not an object that can be held up for inspection, since it has changed by the time one points to it. The wish to capture a state of mind, a good meditation or a moment of clarity, is itself a form of grasping, and relinquishing it is not the loss of mind but its unobstructed operation.

The episode also exhibits the limits of textual mastery: a scholar who knows the text thoroughly is stopped by a vendor who applies it directly.

Paraphrased passages
Past mind cannot be obtained; present mind cannot be obtained; future mind cannot be obtained.
Which mind will you refresh? The author of the commentary has no answer.

Discernment. Scholarly knowledge and lived understanding are tested against each other, and the scholar must acknowledge the gap.

09古KobutsushinThe Old Buddha Mind

Central claimThe mind of the ancient buddhas is not a concealed inner state; it is fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles.

Presented to the assembly in 1243, the fascicle begins with a monk asking National Teacher Nanyang Huizhong about the mind of the ancient buddhas. The answer is blunt: fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles. A questioner expecting something refined and interior receives a list of ordinary objects.

Dōgen accepts the answer at face value and extends it. If the ancient buddha mind were hidden within, it would be a mystery to be unlocked; instead it is what is plainly present. The wall is not a symbol of mind but mind in operation, and tile and pebble are not inferior to awareness but are the form it takes.

He qualifies the word “ancient” as well. It does not mean antique; it denotes completeness, to which no later improvement can add. Being fully present, it cannot be preserved in a book or inherited like property, and each person must encounter it directly.

Paraphrased passages
What is the ancient buddha mind? Fences, walls, tiles, pebbles.
The wall is not a symbol of mind; the wall, doing what a wall does, is mind.

Discernment. Dōgen declines the glamorous conception of awakening in favor of ordinary things, which is the opposite of performance.

10悟DaigoGreat Awakening

Central claimGreat awakening is not a single completed event; awakening continues within awakening, and practice continues after it.

Dōgen examines the term “great awakening” with suspicion toward anyone who treats it as a finish line. Awakening, on his account, is not a peak experience that is attained and retained, nor does it have a fixed form that could be indicated. The awakened person does not stand outside delusion as a spectator.

A principal thread of the fascicle is that awakening and delusion are not simple opposites. One may awaken within delusion and be deluded within awakening. This is paradoxical in form but corresponds to the reports of serious practitioners: a moment of clarity does not guarantee a clear day.

He also criticizes the use of the term as a status. A person who presents themselves as having had a great awakening, and as therefore superior to others, should on Dōgen’s approach be trusted less, not more. The authentic condition is unobtrusive, continues in ordinary activity, and requires no audience.

Paraphrased passages
Great awakening is not finished; there is awakening upon awakening.
An awakened person who stands above the world has misunderstood what awakening is.

Discernment. The fascicle’s strongest warning concerns status: presenting awakening as a credential is a sign of error.

11坐Zazen giPrinciples of Zazen

Central claimSit upright, think non-thinking, and let sitting itself constitute the practice.

Zazengi (Principles of Zazen) is the practical core of Dōgen’s teaching, a set of concrete instructions with little rhetoric. One sits in a quiet room on a thick mat and cushion, setting aside involvements and allowing body and mind to settle. The posture is full or half lotus, with the hands placed one in the other, thumbs lightly touching. The spine is straight, the ears aligned with the shoulders and the nose with the navel, the tongue against the palate, the lips and teeth closed, the eyes open, and the breathing quiet through the nose.

After the body is arranged, the mind is addressed through a well-known exchange. What does one think while sitting motionless? One thinks of not-thinking. How does one think of not-thinking? By non-thinking (hishiryō). This, Dōgen says, is the essential art of zazen.

Dōgen’s wider teaching on zazen holds that it is not meditation used as a means to some further end but the practice and realization of awakening in a single act. Sitting does not lead to awakening; it is awakening taking this particular form. The text also instructs the practitioner to rise slowly and to carry the settled quality of sitting into the rest of the day.

Paraphrased passages
Think of not-thinking. How? Non-thinking. This is the essential art of zazen.
Zazen is not a practice undertaken to gain awakening; it is the gate of great ease and joy.

Discernment. Dōgen specifies precise form because he distrusts a practice that consists only of an attitude.

12箴Zazen shinThe Lancet of Zazen

Central claimNon-thinking is not a blank state; it is free activity, like that of a fish in water or a bird in the sky.

A lancet is a small blade used to lance a boil, and the title signals that the text is meant to cut cleanly through confusion about sitting. The fascicle is short, dense, and partly poetic. It engages an earlier poem by the Chinese teacher Hongzhi and concludes with a poem of Dōgen’s own.

At its center is the exchange of Yaoshan, who, asked what he thinks of while sitting so steadily, answers that he thinks of not-thinking, and, asked how, answers “beyond thinking.” Dōgen devotes most of the text to this. Non-thinking is neither dullness nor a trance in which thought has been switched off, nor is it thinking about a particular object; it is a wakefulness in which thoughts pass without being grasped.

The imagery is physical. A fish swims, and however far it goes there is no end to the water; a bird flies, and however far it goes there is no end to the sky. The sitter is like both, not traveling toward a goal but moving completely within an element. The text warns against two errors: regarding sitting as a technique that produces a result, and regarding it as mere passivity.

Paraphrased passages
Thinking of not-thinking: how? Beyond thinking.
The fish swims and there is no end to the water; the bird flies and there is no end to the sky.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses both a technique that promises results and a passivity that avoids effort; the difference can be tested only in practice.

13海Kaiin zanmaiThe Ocean Seal Samadhi

Central claimIn each moment of settled practice the whole world appears at once and in full depth.

The title refers to a Mahāyāna image of a samādhi (deep concentration) in which all things are reflected as a calm ocean reflects sky, shore, and passing cloud. “Seal” carries the sense of a stamp, the single impression that fits all things.

Dōgen ties this teaching directly to ordinary sitting. The samādhi is not a special state remote from daily practice; the activity that keeps the spine upright and the mind quiet holds the whole world in view, because the world is not outside the one who sits.

He also uses the image to correct the assumption that the ocean is a single vast entity of which each wave is a small, separate part. On his reading the ocean is not more real than the wave: each wave is the entire ocean at that place and time. A single moment of practice is therefore not a fragment of some larger enlightenment to come but the whole. The fascicle proceeds by accumulation and echo and is best read slowly.

Paraphrased passages
The ocean is not greater than the wave; each wave is the whole ocean, just where it is.
A moment of settled practice is not a part of enlightenment but the whole of it.

Discernment. The seal image indicates that what is genuine bears the same impression everywhere and needs no special stage or setting.

14華KūgeSky Flowers

Central claimWhat appears illusory is also the actual flowering of things; appearance should be neither dismissed nor clung to.

“Sky flowers” are the spots seen floating in clear air by a person with diseased eyes, a traditional Buddhist image of delusion. They resemble flowers though no flowers are there, and the image usually serves to show that deluded perception produces unreal objects.

Dōgen takes up the image and refuses the easy conclusion. If sky flowers are mere illusions to be dismissed, then the person who dismisses them has posited a real, flowerless sky behind appearance and is clinging to that. He asks his listeners to look more carefully: the flowers bloom and fall in the sky, and the whole world is this activity of blooming and falling.

This is not an argument for credulity toward whatever is perceived. It is an argument against a facile skepticism that takes pride in seeing through everything. Dōgen requires accurate seeing, including recognition that appearance has a mode of being that is not simply an error.

Paraphrased passages
The flowers in the sky bloom and fall; to call them mere illusion is only another form of clinging.
See through appearance without turning one’s seeing into a new appearance.

Discernment. Cynicism is a counterfeit of discernment; Dōgen requires accurate seeing, not reflexive disbelief.

15光KōmyōIlluminating Wisdom

Central claimIllumination is not an inner glow but the actual world seen without obstruction.

The fascicle builds on a saying of Yunmen: everyone has their own light, but when they try to see it, they find only darkness. Asked what this light is, Yunmen answered with the monks’ hall, the kitchen, and the gate. The reply is deliberately flat.

Dōgen’s reading is that illumination, in the Zen sense, is not a luminous inner experience. One who sits hoping to see a light is looking for the wrong thing, which is why the looking finds only darkness. The light is the ordinary world, shown plainly and available at every moment because it is not hidden. The obstruction is the effort to find it elsewhere.

The fascicle thus carries an implicit caution against popular conceptions of spiritual light, energy, or auras. Dōgen does not deny that vivid experiences occur; he denies that they are the subject of the teaching, and he treats their pursuit as the darkness.

Paraphrased passages
Everyone has their own light; when they look for it, they cannot see it. What is it? The hall, the kitchen, the gate.
To wait for a glow is to look in the dark.

Discernment. The fascicle corrects the marketing of inner light: illumination is the ordinary world seen clearly.

16持GyōjiContinuous Practice

Central claimPractice is not a personal project; it is the unbroken activity of the whole lineage, continuing through the individual.

Gyōji (Continuous Practice), the longest of the fascicles on practice, is usually printed in two parts. Dōgen surveys the lives of the ancestors to show what continuous practice looked like for each. They appear not as heroes who achieved a breakthrough and then coasted but as people who persisted day after day, often in obscurity.

The best-known episode concerns Baizhang, who continued to work in the fields with the monks in extreme old age. When the monks hid his tools out of concern, he refused to eat, saying that a day without work is a day without eating. For Dōgen the point concerns not agriculture but the refusal to treat practice as optional once one is senior or tired.

He maintains that continuous practice cannot be assigned to the self. Its merit belongs to the whole world; one does not practice for personal credit, and its beginning and end are not visible. The text also sets an implicit standard: authentic practice does not announce itself.

Paraphrased passages
Continuous practice is not for oneself; it is the practice of the whole world passing through one.
A day without work is a day without eating.

Discernment. The criterion is persistence without display: quiet, unbroken, unadvertised practice is what Dōgen presents as genuine.

17恁InmoThus, What Is It?

Central claimThe real cannot be named as an object, yet it is not absent; practice and realization cannot be defiled by grasping.

The fascicle turns on one of the most important exchanges in Zen. The Sixth Ancestor, Huineng, asks the newcomer Nanyue, “What is it that thus comes?” Nanyue cannot answer and remains with the question for eight years. When he finally speaks, he says that to call it anything like a thing would miss the mark.

Huineng then asks whether there is practice and realization. Nanyue replies that it is not that there is none, but that they cannot be defiled. Huineng answers that this very non-defilement is what the buddhas protect and hold in mind.

Dōgen reads each line slowly. The question “What is it?” does not seek an answer to be filed but keeps the questioner in the actual; the eight years show that Nanyue did not produce a clever reply but lived with the question until it became part of him. Practice and realization are not absent, which would make Zen a negation, nor can they be seized and owned, since the seizing is the defilement. What the ancestors protect is the freedom of practice from the habit of making it an object or an achievement.

Paraphrased passages
What is it that thus comes? To say it is like a thing misses the mark.
It is not that there is no practice and realization; it is only that they cannot be defiled.

Discernment. Naming and grasping are the contaminants, and genuine practice remains free of both.

18觀KannonAvalokiteshvara

Central claimCompassion is a whole-body, unpremeditated response; and an honest teacher says when an answer is only partly adequate.

The fascicle concerns the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, whose images often show a thousand hands, each with an eye. Dōgen draws on an exchange between two dharma brothers, Yunyan and Daowu. Yunyan asks what the greatly compassionate bodhisattva does with so many hands and eyes. Daowu replies that it is like someone reaching behind them in the night for a pillow.

Yunyan says that he understands, and when asked what he understands, answers that the whole body is hands and eyes. Daowu says that this is well put but only eight-tenths of the matter. Pressed to say more, he states that the whole body is hands and eyes throughout.

The image of reaching for a pillow in the dark indicates that compassion involves no planning, deliberation, or consultation of rules; it is the body responding to a need it knows without thinking. Equally important is the second half of the exchange, in which Daowu declines to praise a good answer and presses for the remainder. The passage models honest assessment between persons who respect one another.

Paraphrased passages
It is like reaching behind oneself in the night for the pillow.
Well said, but only eight-tenths.

Discernment. Honest evaluation is itself a kindness; Daowu does not flatter a good answer, and Dōgen records the exchange with approval.

19鏡KokyōThe Old Mirror

Central claimA mirror shows each thing exactly as it is; realization is this kind of clarity rather than a fixed possession.

The fascicle uses the traditional image of the mirror to discuss the nature of awareness and comments on several classical sayings. One holds that when a foreigner stands before the mirror, a foreigner appears, and when a Chinese person stands before it, a Chinese person appears. The mirror neither selects nor prefers; it shows what is there.

Dōgen extends this. The mirror is not a mind that stores images, nor are the images impressions within it; mirror and reflected object constitute a single event. The “old” mirror is old in having no beginning, the clarity that has always been present.

He also criticizes readings that make the mirror a passive device whose surface requires polishing. In the Chan tradition a poem on polishing the mirror to remove dust was set against another declaring that there is no mirror to polish. Dōgen sides with neither, pointing instead to practice that is neither polishing in order to arrive nor a dismissal of polishing. Clarity, on this reading, is a manner of meeting things, not a state to be guarded.

Paraphrased passages
When a foreigner comes, a foreigner appears; when a Chinese comes, a Chinese appears.
The mirror is not a storehouse of images; mirror and images are one event.

Discernment. Clear seeing means not bending the picture toward what one prefers to see.

20時UjiBeing-Time

Central claimOne is not in time; one is time. Each moment is complete and constitutes the whole of existence.

Uji is among the best-known fascicles and the most philosophically adventurous. Dōgen takes the ordinary Japanese expression uji, “sometimes,” and divides it into its components: being (u) and time (ji). All being is time, and all time is being; a mountain, a pot, and a person are each a moment of time.

He begins with a classical verse about the forms a person may take at different times: sometimes on a mountain peak, sometimes at the bottom of the sea, sometimes with three heads and eight arms, sometimes a sixteen-foot golden Buddha. Dōgen reads this not as a sequence of transformations but as a series of complete beings, each whole in its own moment. His most developed image is that of firewood and ash. Firewood does not become ash; firewood is firewood, with its own before and after, and ash is ash, with its own before and after.

The teaching dissolves the picture of time as a stream bearing one from past toward future. The past is not behind one awaiting revisitation; it is the way the present moment is. In doing whatever one does now, one enacts the whole of time. For Dōgen this undermines any practice that treats its own activity as a means to a later state.

Paraphrased passages
Time does not fly past; each being is a moment of time, complete in itself.
Firewood does not become ash; firewood abides in its own before and after, and ash in its own.

Discernment. The present moment is the decisive test of authenticity; Dōgen rejects any practice that defers its meaning to a later time.

21授JukiConferring Predictions

Central claimGenuine confirmation is given in person and lived out; it cannot be claimed on one’s own authority.

In Buddhist tradition a prediction (vyākaraṇa) is the moment a buddha assures a practitioner of eventual awakening. It appears throughout the sūtras, especially in the Lotus Sūtra, where groups of disciples are told what kind of buddhas they will become. Dōgen asks what this ritual of assurance means within Zen transmission.

His answer moves it from the future to the present. A prediction is not a forecast but a recognition, made in the midst of practice by someone able to see it, that the person is already within the way. The teacher confirms; the student does not appoint themselves.

The emphasis on who confirms is characteristic. For Dōgen the lineage is a chain of persons who verified one another. A claim to awakening unsupported by a teacher, a lineage, or anyone who has observed the claimant’s practice is therefore not a prediction but a story told to oneself. The fascicle belongs to the part of his work concerned with trust: genuine recognition is external and humbling, and caution is warranted toward anyone, oneself included, who bestows it without verification.

Paraphrased passages
A prediction is not a forecast of the future; it is a recognition given now by one able to see.
One cannot certify oneself.

Discernment. The operative theme is verification: Dōgen ties awakening to recognition by a qualified teacher, not to private assertion.

22全ZenkiTotal Dynamism

Central claimLife and death are each the whole function; each is to be met completely, and neither fled.

Zenki denotes the full activity of a thing, the whole working of what is occurring. Dōgen applies it to the most basic subject, life and death. Life is not a span of time that death will later cut off, nor is death what follows life; each is a complete and independent function of the whole.

His image is a person riding a boat. The rider raises the sails, works the oar, and steers with the pole, yet the boat carries the rider; without the boat there is no rider, and the rider makes the boat a boat. Life is similar: one lives, and life lives one, and one cannot step outside to observe it. Birth does not come from somewhere, and death does not go anywhere; in birth one is entirely birth, and in death entirely death.

Dōgen does not offer this as consolation. His claim is that there is no second self standing apart to be threatened, and he asks that life no longer be treated as a project liable to be spoiled, since each moment is already complete.

Paraphrased passages
Life is like riding in a boat: one works the sails and the oar, yet the boat carries one.
In life, be entirely life; in death, be entirely death.

Discernment. Dōgen declines comfort that is not true and offers instead a harder and more accurate way to regard what frightens us.

23月TsukiThe Moon

Central claimThe moon is not a symbol standing in for the Buddha; it is the whole of the Buddha, present wherever it appears.

Dōgen takes the traditional Buddhist image of the moon, which signifies the awakened nature, and, characteristically, declines to treat it as a metaphor. The moon is not like the Buddha; in this fascicle it is the Buddha’s body, appearing in sky and water, whole in each place.

The old image of the moon reflected in many waters has a standard weakness: it suggests one real moon far away and many small copies. Dōgen blocks this reading. The moon in a puddle is the whole moon, and so is the moon in a dewdrop; each appearance is complete and none is lesser. The same logic governs his treatment of ocean and wave.

He also draws on sayings in which a teacher asked about the moon replies with something practical or plain. The teaching lies not in a clever interpretation but in how one responds to what is actually present. The fascicle is short on argument and proceeds by repeating a few images.

Paraphrased passages
The moon in a dewdrop is the whole moon, not a portion of it.
There is not one true moon and many copies.

Discernment. The moon is whole wherever it appears, so no one may claim a larger or more real share of the teaching.

24餠GabyōPainted Cakes

Central claimThere is no real world behind the painted one; only a painted cake can satisfy hunger.

The fascicle begins from a saying attributed to the Chinese teacher Xiangyan: a painted rice cake does not satisfy hunger. In much Buddhist teaching this served as a warning that words, pictures, and doctrines are only depictions of the real. Dōgen inverts the saying.

His claim is that there is no genuine cake elsewhere of which the painted one is a poor copy. Everything encountered is, in a certain sense, painted, that is, formed, composed, and presented: buddhas and ancestors, mountains and rivers, sūtras, and the self alike belong to one painted world. To judge the painting worthless and the real to lie elsewhere is not to have seen through anything but to have invented another painting.

Dōgen therefore says that only a painted cake can satisfy hunger. The remedy for hunger lies within the world of appearances, in the full-attention use of what is present, including words, images, and teachings. The passage is significant for the status of art, text, and craft in Zen: they are the actual medium in which the dharma appears.

Paraphrased passages
A painted cake cannot satisfy hunger, says the old teacher; Dōgen answers that only a painted cake can.
Do not seek a real world behind the painted one.

Discernment. Contempt for forms is a counterfeit of insight; Dōgen assigns craft, art, and language a place as the actual medium of practice.

25谿Keisei sanshokuSounds of the Valley, Forms of the Mountain

Central claimAwakening may be occasioned by anything, but a single experience is not its end; practice and humility must continue.

Written in 1240, the fascicle opens with a poem by Su Dongpo, a poet and official who was a layman rather than a monk. After studying with a Chan teacher, he heard a valley stream during the night and wrote that its sound was the long, broad tongue of the Buddha and the form of the mountain his pure body. Through the night, he said, he heard eighty-four thousand verses and wondered how he might explain them to others the next day.

Dōgen treats the poem as a genuine expression of realization and makes three points. First, awakening does not belong only to monks; a layman’s poem can show clear seeing. Second, sound and form are not metaphors: the stream is the teaching and the mountain the body. Third, and most important, a vivid event must not be taken for a conclusion. He discusses Xiangyan, who awakened on hearing a pebble strike bamboo, and Lingyun, who awakened on seeing peach blossoms, and holds that what matters is that each continued to practice. An isolated experience proves nothing.

The fascicle closes with an unusually personal note on humility: even a capable practitioner should be cautious and persevere.

Paraphrased passages
The sound of the valley stream is the Buddha’s long broad tongue; the form of the mountain is his pure body.
Do not mistake one vivid moment for the end of the road.

Discernment. Dōgen honors what is real wherever it appears, in a layman and in a poem, yet refuses to let a single experience become a credential.

26向BukkōjōjiWhat Is Beyond the Buddha

Central claimThere is no resting place called Buddha; realization itself continues.

The title renders a Chan expression meaning roughly “going beyond the Buddha” or “the matter above buddhahood.” The phrase is provocative because it seems to posit a level higher than awakening. Dōgen uses it to speak of continued practice.

If awakening is understood as a final state at which work ceases, buddhahood has been made into a seat on which one settles. Dōgen’s position is that the truly awakened do not rest there but take a further step, and another, within the way. Going beyond the Buddha does not mean ascending to a higher location; it means refusing to remain fixed in any attainment.

This accords with his insistence that practice and realization are one. If realization were a finish line, practice would end upon crossing it; instead, realization is a manner of continuing. The notion that one has finished, in any field, is for Dōgen the real danger, since it terminates learning.

Paraphrased passages
Do not make a seat of buddhahood and settle into it.
To go beyond the Buddha is to keep walking, not to climb.

Discernment. Authentic practice does not turn its achievements into a throne; resting on attainment is how practice dies.

27夢Muchū setsumuExpounding a Dream Within a Dream

Central claimThe teaching is given within this world, which is itself dream-like; solid ground should not be sought outside it.

The phrase describes buddhas expounding the dharma in a dream and in a dream within a dream. In Buddhist sources the world is often compared to a dream, usually to indicate that it is less solid than it appears. Dōgen declines to posit a waking reality outside the dream where things are finally firm.

The standard picture runs as follows. We are dreaming, that is, living amid unstable appearances; a buddha announces that all of this is a dream; we nod and suppose we have understood. Dōgen’s response is that this is merely one dream explaining another, for there is no outside from which the dream could be described.

The consequence is that the teaching is not a message from beyond but is spoken from within the same world of confusion and appearance that its hearers inhabit. The buddha is in the dream with us, not above it, and practice occurs where we are. One need not await a perfectly clear view before acting.

Paraphrased passages
Even the awakened teach from within the dream; there is no outside from which to explain it.
One dream explaining another is not yet seeing.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses a vantage point above the world; genuine teaching is conducted in the midst of it.

28髓Raihai tokuzuiGetting the Marrow by Doing Obeisance

Central claimThe dharma is to be honored wherever it is found and whoever carries it; rank, age, and sex are irrelevant.

The title alludes to the story of Bodhidharma, who asked four disciples to express their understanding. After the last, Huike, bowed three times and stood in his place, Bodhidharma said that Huike had obtained his marrow. Dōgen takes the bowing seriously: obeisance is not servility but the way a teaching is received from one who has it.

The core of the fascicle concerns whom one should bow to. Dōgen states that when a person has realized the dharma, one should bow to them regardless of appearance, age, or station, and he names the cases explicitly: a young child, a woman, a person despised by the world. He is sharply critical of the exclusion of women from sacred precincts and from instruction, and he ridicules those who place their own rank above the dharma. The position was unusual for its time, though his views on women elsewhere in his writings remain a subject of scholarly debate.

The underlying principle is that authority derives from realization, not title. A renowned abbot who has not realized the dharma has no claim to deference, whereas a seven-year-old girl who has realized it does. The passage is among Dōgen’s clearest statements on authenticity: attention should rest on what a person actually possesses, not on what they are called.

Paraphrased passages
If a person has the dharma, one should bow to them, woman or man, old or young.
To bow to a title is not to bow to the dharma.

Discernment. Respect what is real wherever it appears, and do not defer to what is merely titled.

29山Sansui kyōThe Mountains and Waters Sutra

Central claimMountains and waters are the teaching itself, walking and flowing in plain view; their activity must be learned to be seen.

The fascicle opens with a line often quoted out of context: the mountains are constantly walking. Dōgen builds on a verse by Furong Daokai, who said that the blue mountains are always walking and that a stone woman gives birth to a child in the night, without attempting to explain the verse away.

His claim is that mountains do not merely stand awaiting admiration; they are always doing what mountains do, which includes walking. We fail to see this because we assume that walking requires legs and a destination, and Dōgen suggests that doubt about the mountains’ walking arises from not understanding one’s own.

The fascicle also considers water. Different beings see water differently: a fish sees a palace, a heavenly being sees jewels, a hungry ghost sees fire, and a human sees water. Dōgen is not asserting that reality is merely perspectival; he observes how much of what is called real depends on one’s situation, and he asks that this be held lightly while the world is still taken seriously. He ends by encouraging training in the mountains, which are not a retreat from the world but the sūtra itself, written in the landscape.

Paraphrased passages
The blue mountains are constantly walking.
To doubt that the mountains walk is not yet to know one’s own walking.

Discernment. Dōgen directs attention to what is actually present and refuses to let a familiar assumption substitute for looking.

30看KankinReading Sutras

Central claimThe teachings are to be read with the whole body and without contempt, neither worshipped as words nor dismissed.

This short fascicle concerns how sūtras should be read. A common attitude in Zen at the time held that scriptures were merely words and that real practice lay outside them. Dōgen resists this, though not in the manner of a scholar.

He relates a story of Yaoshan, a master who forbade his monks to read sūtras yet read them himself. Asked why, he replied that he was merely trying to cover his eyes. Dōgen does not treat this as hypocrisy but as a remark on the kind of reading that matters: reading not to gather opinions but to see the world through the teaching, so that the page becomes transparent.

The true sūtra, he says, is not confined to the scroll. The entire universe is a sūtra, and the scroll is part of it. To read well is to read with the whole body, and to be read in turn. Dōgen asks that neither the written text nor the unwritten world be omitted.

Paraphrased passages
Reading a sūtra is done not only with the eyes but with the whole body.
The whole world is a sūtra, and the scroll is one part of it.

Discernment. Dōgen rejects both the worship and the dismissal of texts: genuine reading changes the reader.

31莫Shoaku makusaNot Doing Evil

Central claimRefraining from harm is not an external rule; it is how an awakened life naturally moves.

The fascicle takes up an exchange between the poet-official Bai Juyi and the teacher Niaoke, who lived in a tree. Bai Juyi asked what the great meaning of the Buddha’s teaching was. Niaoke replied that it is to do no evil and to practice all good. Bai Juyi said that even a three-year-old child could say that. Niaoke answered that a three-year-old can say it, but an eighty-year-old cannot do it.

Dōgen’s reading is the center of the fascicle. The traditional verse is usually taken as an instruction: do no evil, do good. Dōgen reads the grammar differently, so that “not doing evil” becomes a description. Where awakening is actually occurring, evil is not done, since it is not what that way of being does. The precept is not a fence around the way but how the way appears when it moves.

He guards against complacency by noting that the old man cannot do it: the words are easy and the practice is difficult. A person who claims realization and then causes harm has not realized. The fascicle is central to Dōgen’s view of ethics and authenticity, admitting no gap between understanding and conduct as observed over time.

Paraphrased passages
A three-year-old can say it; an eighty-year-old cannot do it.
Not doing evil is not a command from outside; it is how awakening acts.

Discernment. Conduct is the proof: if understanding permits harm, it was not understanding.

32衣Den eTransmitting the Robe

Central claimThe robe represents an actual, verified transmission; it is neither a costume nor a trophy.

In Zen history the robe (kesa) is passed from teacher to student as a sign that the teaching has been handed on. The legend extends back to the first ancestor, Mahākāśyapa, said to have received the robe from the Buddha, and in the story of the Sixth Ancestor the robe is a physical object with real consequences.

Dōgen treats the robe as an expression of what it signifies. Transmission of the robe is not a ceremony added to the teaching, nor a certificate; it is the way the teaching continues between persons. The robe is the dharma in a form that can be held.

The fascicle belongs among the writings that insist on the authenticity of lineage. Dōgen had traveled to China precisely to find a teacher whose transmission was genuine, and he returned with credentials of his own. The principle is that practice should be linked to a real line of persons who recognized one another, not to a self-declared status. A genuine transmission can be traced, is given by someone who possesses it, and carries obligations.

Paraphrased passages
The robe is not a costume; it is how the teaching is passed from hand to hand.
A robe without a lineage behind it is only cloth.

Discernment. Verify the lineage; Dōgen crossed the sea for exactly this reason.

33得DōtokuAble to Speak

Central claimTo express the way is not to repeat sayings but to speak or act from one’s own realization.

Dōtoku means “being able to say,” the expression of the way. In Chan literature a teacher often presses a student to say something about their understanding, and the reply discloses its depth. Dōgen makes this idea central.

His main claim is that dōtoku is not identical with speaking. Words are one form of it, but so are silence, a gesture, or the manner of performing a task. What matters is that the expression is alive and arises from realization rather than from memory. A teacher’s phrase is not available for borrowing: one who repeats what an ancestor said has expressed nothing.

He criticizes both extremes. One who covers a lack of understanding with a flood of clever phrases does not express the way, and neither does one who adopts silence as a pose. Both avoid the risk of actually being seen. Genuine expression cannot be delegated to a template or to another’s phrases and must be tested in the open.

Paraphrased passages
To repeat an ancestor’s words is not to express the way.
Silence adopted as a pose is another way of not speaking.

Discernment. Borrowed eloquence and performed silence are both counterfeits; real expression is risky and one’s own.

34教BukkyōThe Buddha’s Teaching

Central claimThe sūtras should not be set against the ancestors; the Buddha’s teaching and the mind that transmits it are one.

A frequent claim in Zen is that it is a special transmission outside the scriptures, independent of words. Read loosely, this can become contempt for the Buddha’s teachings and a belief that Zen requires no study. Dōgen addresses the claim directly.

He argues that the Buddha’s teaching, as found in the sūtras, and the mind of the ancestors, passed from teacher to student, are not two different things. The ancestors who traveled from India to China brought the same dharma that is in the sūtras. To say that sūtras and ancestors point in different directions is to misunderstand both.

The position matters because it makes Zen continuous with Buddhism as a whole rather than a clever departure from it. A genuine lineage need not disown the teachings in order to appear distinctive; it stands in the same line and should be able to read the old texts with respect and understanding. The fascicle thus supplies a criterion for assessing any modern teaching: whether the teacher dismisses the traditional sources or reads them with intelligence.

Paraphrased passages
The sūtras and the mind of the ancestors are not two different dharmas.
Zen is not an escape from the Buddha’s teaching.

Discernment. A teaching that must disown its sources in order to appear special is suspect; Dōgen insists on continuity.

35神JinzūSpiritual Powers

Central claimThe genuine marvel is ordinary activity performed completely; powers are not proof of awakening.

Traditional Buddhism lists a set of extraordinary abilities, such as clairvoyance, thought-reading, and recollection of past lives, associated with advanced meditation. For many these powers define spiritual attainment. Dōgen’s fascicle responds to that assumption.

He quotes the layman Pang, who wrote that the marvelous power and wondrous activity of an awakened person is simply drawing water and carrying firewood. Dōgen treats this verse as the point of the whole teaching: the real power is ordinary action performed wholeheartedly, with nothing held back and nothing left over.

He does not deny that unusual abilities have been reported but treats them as secondary and potentially misleading. A person may possess uncanny skills and remain confused, and another may be entirely clear without any. Taking powers as evidence of realization is looking in the wrong place. By this standard a teacher who points to displays, special states, or a reputation for miracles offers the wrong goods; the test is whether the marvel is found in how the person lives and works every day.

Paraphrased passages
Marvelous power and wondrous activity: drawing water and carrying firewood.
A power not found in ordinary action is not the buddhas’ power.

Discernment. Displays and special abilities are not proof; Dōgen returns attention to what is ordinary and verifiable.

36羅ArakanThe Arhat

Central claimOther forms of the dharma are not to be disparaged; precision about what a term means is a form of honesty.

In some Mahāyāna circles the arhat, the realized person of the early teachings, was treated as inferior to the bodhisattva. Zen writers sometimes joined in, using “arhat” for one who has attained a narrow, self-regarding peace. Dōgen disagrees and says so in this fascicle.

His argument is historical and doctrinal. The ancestors of the Zen line include arhats, and the title means a worthy one, a person who is truly liberated. To use the word as an insult is to misunderstand both the early tradition and Mahāyāna. He argues that an arhat is a buddha, in the sense that the realized state is the same under a different name.

The fascicle is short and fairly dry but revealing of his method. He does not accept a convenient caricature of another’s teaching even when it serves his own side; he checks the term against the sources and corrects the habit. The implication is that easy contempt for another tradition, or for an earlier stage of one’s own, usually indicates weak knowledge.

Paraphrased passages
An arhat is a worthy one, and the ancestors include arhats.
Do not use a respected title as an insult.

Discernment. Accuracy in naming what one criticizes is part of discernment; caricature is lazy.

37春ShunjūSpring and Autumn

Central claimEach season is to be met fully; no place free of cold and heat exists.

The fascicle turns on a well-known exchange about cold and heat, which in the Chan tradition stand for the pressures of ordinary life. A monk asks Dongshan how to avoid cold and heat. Dongshan asks why he does not go where there is no cold or heat. The monk asks where that is. Dongshan replies that when it is cold, one should let the cold kill one, and when it is hot, let the heat kill one.

The reply is blunt but not cruel. The way to deal with a hard condition is not to escape it but to meet it so completely that one no longer stands aside asking how to get out. The suffering added by resistance is a supplement to the cold; the cold alone, fully met, is only cold.

Dōgen links this to the changing seasons of the title. Spring and autumn are not metaphors but the actual rhythm in which practice occurs, and one who waits for suitable weather to begin never begins. Conditions are seldom ideal; Dongshan’s answer closes that exit and cautions against teachings that promise a place free of difficulty.

Paraphrased passages
When it is cold, let the cold kill you; when it is hot, let the heat kill you.
There is no place without cold and heat; stop looking for one.

Discernment. A teaching that promises a place without difficulty warrants wariness; Dōgen points the other way.

38葛KattōTwining Vines

Central claimThe relation of teacher and student is a tangle of transmission in which no part is inferior.

Kattō means the vines of wisteria or kudzu that wind around one another. In Zen the word has often signified entanglement in words, an obstacle to be cut through. Dōgen reverses the image and treats the vines as the very medium of transmission: the tangle of teacher and student, question and answer, word and word is how the dharma passes between persons.

He builds the fascicle around Bodhidharma’s test of four disciples, who are said to have received his skin, flesh, bones, and marrow, the last being Huike. Many have read this as a ranking from shallow to deep. Dōgen rejects the ranking: each of the four expressed the whole of Bodhidharma’s teaching, and none expressed less. Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow are not levels but aspects of the whole person.

The point bears on hierarchy in practice. A genuine teaching does not need a ladder of attainment to be authentic, and the notion that some students possess deeper understanding than others is often the first step toward a cult of rank. The fascicle can be read as a warning against any group that uses grades of insight to maintain order.

Paraphrased passages
Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow are not levels; each is the whole teaching.
The tangle of teacher and student is the transmission itself.

Discernment. A ladder of attainments is a warning sign; Dōgen refuses to rank understanding into higher and lower.

39嗣ShishoSuccession Record

Central claimA lineage should be documented, verifiable, and honest; forgery and vague claims are betrayals.

The shisho is the document recording the succession of dharma from teacher to student. It lists the line of ancestors, usually back to Śākyamuni, and ends with the name of the recipient, signed and sealed by the teacher. In Dōgen’s day it was the central proof that a person had received transmission.

Dōgen describes the document and explains its importance, having seen examples in China. He details the form with care: how the names are written, the language used, and how the final name is added. He treats the papers as the concrete expression of an unbroken relationship, not as mere paperwork.

He is also concerned with abuse of the process. A transmission that is bought, invented, or handed out casually damages the entire line, since the document is only as honest as those who write it, and he warns against monks who claim lineages they have not received. The fascicle is one of his plainest discussions of authenticity, and its practical implication is that a real succession can withstand inquiry: who gave transmission, what they were like, and whether they remain available to be asked.

Paraphrased passages
The succession record is the concrete trace of an unbroken line of persons.
A transmission that is invented or bought breaks the whole line.

Discernment. Dōgen treats traceable lineage as a basic test and regards forgery as a betrayal of everyone in the chain.

40柏HakujushiThe Cypress Tree

Central claimThe point is not hidden behind the tree; the cypress in the courtyard is the answer.

The famous case: a monk asks Zhaozhou why the first ancestor came from the west, that is, what the point of Zen is. Zhaozhou answers, “The cypress tree in the courtyard.” The monk objects that the teacher should not answer by pointing to an object. Zhaozhou says he is not pointing to an object. The monk asks again, and Zhaozhou gives the same answer.

Many readers take the cypress as a device for blocking an abstract question by directing attention to something concrete. Dōgen takes it more seriously. He quotes the story and comments on how the monk misunderstood: the monk assumed the tree was a symbol of something else and wanted to know what lay beneath. Zhaozhou’s denial that he points to an object means he is not using the tree as a sign. The tree is the matter itself.

Dōgen also criticizes those who treat the case as a cunning shortcut, or who claim to grasp its meaning without any comparable experience. The exchange is a demonstration to be met, not a riddle to be solved. The truth is usually not behind the thing one is looking at; it is the thing.

Paraphrased passages
Why did the first ancestor come from the west? The cypress tree in the courtyard.
Do not turn the tree into a symbol; it is the answer.

Discernment. A clever interpretation is often a means of avoiding the plain fact, to which Dōgen keeps returning.

41三Sangai yuishinThe Three Worlds Are Only Mind

Central claimMind is not a private screen within the head; it is the whole world as encountered.

A traditional Buddhist phrase states that the three worlds, of desire, form, and formlessness, are only mind. Read quickly, it sounds like idealism, as if the world were a projection of a personal mind that could be altered at will. Dōgen explicitly rejects that reading.

For him mind is not a thing inside a skull producing images. Mind is the entire field of what appears: trees, walls, people, and all. To say that the three worlds are only mind is to say that the world is not external to the one who experiences it, not that it is less real. It is as solid as ever, and the experiencer is not a separate observer.

He connects this with earlier fascicles on the ancient buddha mind and on “the very mind is Buddha,” which make the same point from different directions: the teaching concerns the unity of self and world in practice, not an interior fact. The implication is that the quality of attention one brings to tasks and to other people is not a private matter. The world is not a dream to be controlled but a field in which responsibility is complete.

Paraphrased passages
The three worlds are only mind, and mind is not inside one; it is the world.
Idealism is not the teaching; the teaching is the unity of self and world.

Discernment. Dōgen guards against a familiar misreading that converts a profound teaching into wishful thinking.

42説Sesshin sesshōExpounding Mind, Expounding Nature

Central claimDiscourse about mind and nature must be tested against actual practice; otherwise it is only talk.

The title refers to the teaching of mind and nature, a favorite topic in Chan debates. The question was whether the essence of the Buddha’s teaching lay in a mind or nature underlying all appearances. Some teachers claimed to expound mind and nature as the core of their method, and others dismissed the subject as an intellectual trap.

Dōgen takes a distinctive middle course. He accepts that the Buddha’s way concerns mind and nature but insists on what the words mean. Mind is the whole working of the world, in the manner of the ancient buddha mind; nature is the actuality of things as they are, not an essence behind them.

He criticizes the habit of presenting mind and nature as a doctrine that can be held and defended, since that converts an experience into a position. The way to expound mind and nature is to practice so as to show them, not to lecture about them. Fluent description is not evidence; the question is whether the talk corresponds to how the person lives and works.

Paraphrased passages
To expound mind and nature is to practice them, not to lecture about them.
A doctrine of mind is not mind.

Discernment. Fluent talk about profound matters is not evidence; it must be checked against how the person actually lives.

43相Shohō jissōThe True Reality of All Dharmas

Central claimReality is not concealed behind appearances; each thing, as it is, is the real form.

The title comes from a line in the Lotus Sūtra: only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas. Dōgen uses this famous sentence as a lens for considering what is real.

The usual reading posits a reality behind the shapes of things that only awakened beings can perceive. Dōgen reverses it: the real form of things is not a hidden essence but the way things appear, as they are, just now. A flower blooming is true reality; a wall standing is true reality. There is no further layer to be found.

The clause about a buddha together with a buddha is significant. Realization is not a private possession but occurs between persons, in a relationship of recognition and testing, and one cannot certify one’s own insight without someone able to see it. The way to verify that one has seen something is thus not inward certainty but recognition by another who has seen it too, and what is seen is not a secret but the plain world.

Paraphrased passages
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
The real form is not behind the appearance; it is the appearance, as it is.

Discernment. Realization is mutual; without someone who can see what one sees, certainty is only the feeling of being sure.

44佛ButsudōThe Way of the Buddha

Central claimThe Buddha’s way is distinct; it should be neither blended into a vague unity of all teachings nor rebranded.

This is a pointed and somewhat polemical fascicle. In Dōgen’s time a popular idea in East Asia held that Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism were three expressions of one truth and that their differences were superficial. Some Chan teachers promoted the view, which appealed because it seemed tolerant.

Dōgen rejects it firmly. His argument is that the Buddha’s way has its own content, which is not interchangeable with the others. To say that all three teach the same thing is to show that one does not understand any of them well enough to see where they differ; this is not breadth but vagueness.

He also criticizes the name “Zen school” and the habit of treating it as a separate sect that has surpassed the Buddha’s teaching. For him the way is simply the Buddha’s way, transmitted by the line of ancestors, and a new label implying a fresh start only separates it from its roots. The result is an early statement of authenticity as fidelity: Dōgen wants the actual tradition, with its edges, not a synthesis of whatever works.

Paraphrased passages
To say that the three teachings are one is to understand none of them.
The way is the Buddha’s way, not a new school.

Discernment. A comfortable blend is not depth; Dōgen wants the real tradition with its actual boundaries.

45密MitsugoIntimate Words

Central claimWhat is intimate is not hidden; it is plain and direct, and recognized only by one who is actually in it.

Mitsugo literally means “secret language,” and in Chan stories a teacher’s remark may be called an intimate word between teacher and student that others cannot follow. Dōgen uses the concept to ask what is meant by a secret in a tradition that claims to conceal nothing.

His answer is that the intimacy is real but is not a matter of concealment. A word is secret not because it is withheld but because it can be received only by someone standing in the right place. A plain remark can pass over the heads of everyone in the room who is still looking for an answer and land immediately with the one who is not.

The text draws on traditional cases in which observers dismiss a teacher’s words while the student plainly receives them. Dōgen does not explain these away or treat them as occult; they exemplify genuine encounter, in which saying and hearing are one event. In his account nothing is withheld, and what seems hidden is what is plainly shown but not yet attended to. This corrects the mystique of hidden knowledge cultivated by some spiritual groups.

Paraphrased passages
The secret is not withheld; it is plainly shown, and recognized only by one who is there.
An intimate word needs no mystery, only a listener.

Discernment. A teaching built on hidden knowledge is suspect; Dōgen’s secret is the plain thing no one is looking at.

46情Mujō seppōThe Insentient Preach the Dharma

Central claimThe whole world is teaching; one must listen with the eyes and the whole body, not only the ears.

The fascicle centers on an exchange between Dongshan and his teacher Yunyan. Dongshan asks who can hear the preaching of the insentient, meaning walls, trees, stones, and the rest of the non-living world. Yunyan replies that the insentient can hear it. Dongshan asks whether Yunyan himself hears it. Yunyan says that if he did, Dongshan would not hear his own preaching. Dongshan asks why he cannot hear Yunyan. Yunyan raises his whisk and asks whether he hears that; Dongshan says no. Yunyan says that Dongshan cannot even hear a human preach, so how could he hear the insentient?

Dongshan later composed a verse stating, in effect, that the preaching of the insentient is marvelous and inconceivable: one who listens with the ears will not understand, but one who hears with the eyes will know.

Dōgen takes this to mean that the teaching is not confined to words and speakers. Every part of the world expresses the dharma, and what is needed is a mode of attention not dependent on ordinary hearing: whole-body attention in which the listener is not separate from what is heard. The fascicle concerns receptivity; one closed to anything but explicit speech misses most of what occurs.

Paraphrased passages
If one listens with the ears, one will not understand; hear it with the eyes.
Walls, trees, and stones are all preaching; the task is to be able to listen.

Discernment. Real attention is wider than the obvious channel; Dōgen trains one to notice what is actually being said by everything present.

47經ButsukyōThe Buddhas’ Sutras

Central claimThe sūtras belong to buddhas and ancestors alike; they are neither props nor dead paper.

A companion to the earlier fascicle on the Buddha’s teaching, this text treats the sūtras as the buddhas’ own and asks how they relate to the words of the ancestors. The problem is the same: a style of Zen that regards the written teachings as discarded paper, quoted when convenient and ignored otherwise.

Dōgen holds that both the sūtras and the words of the ancestors are the buddhas’ expression, and that neither can be fully understood without the other. One who treats the sūtras as lifeless has not understood the ancestors, and one who supposes the ancestors to have a different message has not understood the sūtras.

He also remarks on how sūtras should be received. They are not merely information; reading and hearing them is a form of practice, to be undertaken with the attention one brings to sitting, and, done rightly, the sūtra changes the reader. As a criterion for assessing sources, a tradition able to speak from its own texts is in a stronger position than one depending solely on a charismatic person.

Paraphrased passages
The sūtras are the buddhas’ own expression, and the words of the ancestors are the same.
Neither worship the page nor discard it.

Discernment. Check the sources: a teaching that cannot be read against its own texts rests on personality alone.

48法HosshōDharma-Nature

Central claimDharma-nature is not a hidden essence; it is the way things actually are, here and now.

Dharma-nature (hosshō) names the underlying nature of things and appears widely in Mahāyāna thought. A common risk, which Dōgen sees clearly, is that it comes to be pictured as a substance, an ultimate stuff behind the world of ordinary appearances. Talk of it then becomes a way of saying that what is seen is not the real world.

The fascicle counters this tendency. Working through sayings of earlier teachers, Dōgen shows that dharma-nature is not behind things but is the actual character of things, appearing as they appear: a mountain’s nature is the mountain. One who seeks something beneath the surface has missed what is at the surface.

The text is short and works mostly by suggestion. It relates to the longer treatment of buddha-nature and carries the same message: one does not possess a hidden nature that must be found, but is what is occurring. A teaching that continually points beyond the present, to a better method or a more advanced teacher, is to that extent suspect.

Paraphrased passages
The nature of a thing is not behind the thing; it is the thing as it is.
To look for the real elsewhere is to miss it here.

Discernment. A teaching that always points beyond the present is suspect; Dōgen points back to what is here.

49陀DaraniDharani

Central claimThe holding power of the teaching is not a magical formula; it is what completed practice does.

A dhāraṇī is a long incantation of Sanskrit syllables used in many Buddhist traditions for protection and blessing. The term carries the sense of holding or retaining, and such formulas were believed to preserve and transmit the power of a teaching. In popular practice they could readily become a kind of spell.

Dōgen treats the subject with restraint. He neither condemns the use of dhāraṇī nor regards it as magic. His emphasis falls on the root meaning: the capacity to hold the whole teaching in body and mind so that it is available in whatever situation arises. In that sense one who has practiced completely is a dhāraṇī, and practice is what holds.

This guards against two opposite errors: treating ritual formulas as a technology that works regardless of the user, and dismissing ritual altogether as superstition. Dōgen asks for a person for whom the formula expresses a practice already lived. A form not supported by practice is empty, and a practice that rejects all forms loses its shape.

Paraphrased passages
To hold the teaching completely is itself dhāraṇī.
A formula without practice is empty; practice without form has no shape.

Discernment. Ritual is neither a spell nor an embarrassment; it carries weight only when the person using it is actually practicing.

50洗SenmenWashing the Face

Central claimEven washing the face is the buddha way; small acts done with care are not beneath practice.

This is one of Dōgen’s plainest fascicles and one of the most revealing. It is a manual of morning ablutions in the monastery: cleaning the teeth, rinsing the mouth, and washing the face. He cites early rules of the Buddhist community, the practice of the Buddha himself, and the habits of the Chinese monasteries he had seen.

The details are specific: how to use the water, how to hold the basin, how to avoid waste, how to be quiet and unhurried. The tone is practical and quietly devoted. Dōgen treats the cleansing of the body as a sacred act, and in his view the washing of the face is the same practice as sitting, expressed in a different posture.

The fascicle also says much about attitude. Care for the body and the immediate environment is not separate from care for the dharma, and a monk who is careless about daily routines is not in a position to claim depth. The implication is that the treatment of small tasks is a better measure of seriousness than stated ideals.

Paraphrased passages
Washing the face is not a chore preceding practice; it is the practice.
How one handles small tasks shows how one handles everything.

Discernment. Dōgen tests a claim of depth against the smallest daily habits; if it does not appear there, it should be doubted.

51面MenjuFace-to-Face Transmission

Central claimThe dharma passes from person to person, in person; books alone cannot certify it.

Written after Dōgen had been teaching for some years, this fascicle describes the meaning of face-to-face transmission and recounts how he received it. In Zen the central relationship is between a teacher and a student who actually meet, and the dharma is passed in that meeting. Dōgen insists that this is not a figure of speech.

He recounts his own experience with his teacher Rujing, a sense of something recognized and confirmed that was unavailable from books. He also describes the line of ancestors in terms of meetings, each standing before the next and being known.

The consequence concerns authority. If transmission occurs through actual meeting, then one who claims it must have met the person from whom they claim to have received it, and that person must have known them. This is a strong check on self-declared teachers and on lineage claims resting on a dream, a vision, or a reading. Dōgen also affirms the student’s own commitment: a teacher cannot do the work, but the work is not complete without the other person. The question to ask of any teacher is whom they met, and who met them.

Paraphrased passages
The dharma is passed face to face, and nothing replaces the meeting.
A claim of transmission requires a real person who actually knew the claimant.

Discernment. Dōgen treats unverifiable claims of private transmission as a basic warning sign.

52祖BussoBuddhas and Ancestors

Central claimThe line of ancestors is not past history; each ancestor is present in practice now.

The fascicle is largely a recitation. It names the seven buddhas of the past, from Vipaśyin to Śākyamuni, then the Indian ancestors beginning with Mahākāśyapa, and then the Chinese ancestors down to Dōgen’s own teacher. The list is long and ritual in character, since it was chanted and revered in the monastery.

Dōgen’s introduction explains its importance. A line of teachers is not a pedigree to be framed but a living reality, and reciting the names is a way of meeting the persons. To honor the ancestors is to enter the relationship they had with one another and with the dharma.

The fascicle also makes a subtler claim: the line is a source of authority precisely because it consists of individuals who had to verify the teaching in their own practice. It does not remove the need to practice but gives practice a context and a standard. A conviction recurs throughout his work: the way is not invented by the individual, who stands in a line and takes responsibility for passing it on cleanly.

Paraphrased passages
To recite the names is to meet the people.
The line gives practice a standard, not a shortcut.

Discernment. A teaching without a line behind it has no one to be checked against; Dōgen insists on the chain.

53梅BaikaPlum Blossoms

Central claimThe plum blossoms in the cold; realization appears in hard conditions, not despite them.

The plum blossom, which opens in late winter while snow still lies on the ground, is a favorite image in East Asian poetry and in Chan. It stands for beauty that appears in adversity and for a clear awakening not postponed until conditions are comfortable. Dōgen draws on this tradition and on the sayings of his teacher Rujing, who was fond of the image.

His treatment is characteristic: the plum blossom is not a metaphor for an awakening that is elsewhere but the awakening in that form, there on that branch. A single flower in the cold is a complete expression of the teaching, and one who sees it sees the whole.

The image also reflects Dōgen’s circumstances. He wrote the fascicle in 1243, in a period of difficulty, with a small community in remote mountains and little material support. The image of flowering in the cold may express what he hoped for his community. The lesson is that practice is done in whatever conditions exist, with what is available.

Paraphrased passages
The plum blossoms in the cold without waiting for spring.
One flower on a bare branch is the whole teaching.

Discernment. Genuine practice does not require ideal conditions; any teaching that requires them warrants skepticism.

54淨SenjōPurification

Central claimEven the toilet is a place for practice; nothing in life lies outside the way.

A companion to the fascicle on washing the face, this text treats a subject that most religious writing avoids: the use of the toilet and cleansing afterward. Dōgen explains the procedures in detail, including preparation, washing with water, washing the hands, and the order of each step, drawing on early Buddhist rules and Chinese monastic practice.

The topic invites amusement, but Dōgen was in earnest. The fascicle makes the direct claim that no part of life lies outside practice. If the body is a place where awakening occurs, then the actions of the body, including the least dignified, deserve attention; a tradition able to discuss emptiness but not hygiene has missed something.

There is also an implicit point about honesty: the most private and least glamorous parts of life reveal character, and one who is careful only in public has not integrated practice. What is done when no one is watching is the real measure.

Paraphrased passages
No part of life lies outside the way, including the toilet.
What one does unseen is the real test.

Discernment. Integrity shows in unglamorous details; Dōgen refuses to let practice live only in the dignified parts of life.

55十JippōThe Ten Directions

Central claimThe whole world is here, where one stands; the teaching is not to be sought somewhere distant.

The ten directions are the four cardinal points, the four intermediate points, up, and down, which together represent the whole universe. In Buddhist sources they often appear in references to the buddhas of the ten directions, who are everywhere, and the expression suggests vastness.

Dōgen’s use of the phrase works against the sense of distance. For him the ten directions are not a remote horizon but the place where one stands, with every direction opening out from it. To say that the dharma fills the ten directions is to say that it is fully present at this spot, and there is no need to look elsewhere.

He supports this with sayings of earlier teachers and with the logic running through his work: nothing is lacking, and no thing is a lesser version of something else. A single place is the whole world at that spot, as the moon in a drop is the whole moon. One who always looks toward a larger, more advanced situation discounts the present.

Paraphrased passages
The ten directions are not far away; they open out from where one stands.
Nothing is lacking at this spot.

Discernment. A teaching that always sends one elsewhere should be questioned; Dōgen keeps returning to where one is.

56見KenbutsuSeeing the Buddha

Central claimTo see the Buddha is not to see a figure; it is to see forms as they are and to practice.

A line in the Diamond Sūtra says that if one sees all forms as non-forms, one sees the Tathāgata. It is often read as an instruction to look through the world to something behind it. Dōgen takes up the question of what it means to see the Buddha and treats it carefully.

His answer moves away from visions and extraordinary experiences. Seeing the Buddha is not a matter of an apparition appearing. Many in the tradition have claimed such visions and he does not dismiss them outright, but he holds that real seeing is the quiet recognition of how things are in the course of ordinary practice. The Buddha is seen in the one who practices, oneself included.

He also stresses reverence: seeing a buddha is an occasion for respect, and the proper response is to bow and to practice, not to describe or collect the experience. By his standard, the test of any special experience is what it does to one’s manner of living. If it deepens care and attention it may be genuine; if it produces pride it is a distraction, however vivid.

Paraphrased passages
To see the Buddha is not to see a figure; it is to see forms as they are.
A vision that makes one feel special has gone wrong.

Discernment. Dōgen is cautious about special experiences; what matters is whether they deepen care or inflate pride.

57參HenzanExtensive Study

Central claimTravel and study with total commitment, and seek a real teacher rather than a famous one.

Henzan means visiting many teachers and studying widely. In the Chinese Chan tradition a monk would often travel from monastery to monastery, testing teachers and being tested. Dōgen did this in China, and the fascicle reflects what he learned.

His main point is that extensive study is not tourism. A monk who wanders collecting impressions is not studying; real study is complete engagement with whatever is at hand, so that even a short stay can be thorough and a long journey a series of full encounters.

He is also clear about the object of the search. A student should seek a true teacher, someone who has actually realized the dharma and can transmit it. Fame, size of the community, and impressive buildings are not reliable guides. In his own travels Dōgen found many prominent teachers who did not meet his standard, and one, Rujing, who did. The choice of whom to learn from should be made slowly, attending to what a teacher actually does and not being dazzled by reputation.

Paraphrased passages
Extensive study is not collecting impressions; it is full engagement with each encounter.
Seek a true teacher, not a famous one.

Discernment. Dōgen’s own travels are the model: visit widely, test carefully, and do not mistake reputation for realization.

58眼GanzeiThe Eye

Central claimThe eye of the buddhas is not an organ; it is the whole of a practicing life seeing clearly.

The title means the pupil of the eye, and the fascicle concerns the Buddha’s eye, the eye of awakened vision. In Zen literature the eye is often a metaphor for the capacity to see what is real, as in the title of the Shōbōgenzō itself, the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye.

Dōgen’s treatment avoids the standard picture of a special inner faculty. Working with old sayings, he shows that the eye is the whole body in action, and that to speak of seeing is to speak of everything the practitioner does. An eye not supported by conduct is not the Buddha’s eye, however sharp it may seem.

He is particularly interested in how an authentic eye distinguishes truth from imitation. One who has it recognizes a real teacher, a real teaching, and a real practice, and also notices when something is amiss. This is not suspicion but clarity. Discernment is a trained capacity, acquired slowly through looking and practice, and it is not independent of how one lives.

Paraphrased passages
The eye is not an organ; it is the whole body seeing clearly.
An eye that cannot tell the real from the imitation is not yet the true eye.

Discernment. Discernment is a trained capacity, not a mood; Dōgen ties it to the whole of a person’s practice.

59家KajōEveryday Matters

Central claimTea and rice, plain and daily, are the buddha way; the ordinary should not be overlooked.

Kajō denotes the ordinary round of household life: a meal, a cup of tea, the daily tasks that constitute a home. In Chan a famous teacher is said to have answered nearly every question with something as plain as an invitation to tea, the suggestion being that the way is not an exotic place but the everyday.

Dōgen develops the theme forcefully. He argues that people who seek the Buddha’s way in remarkable experiences and dramatic events overlook the one place where it can be seen without effort, ordinary eating, drinking, working, and resting. Tea and rice are not lesser than visions; they are the full presence of the way.

The text also gently criticizes a tendency to romanticize monastic life. A monastery is a place of daily routine, and what makes it a place of practice is the manner in which the routine is carried out. The ordinary is not a means to the sacred; the ordinary, done completely, is what the sacred looks like.

Paraphrased passages
Tea and rice, simple and daily, are the buddha way.
Do not look past the ordinary for something better.

Discernment. A teaching that disdains daily life and promises something rarer is suspect; Dōgen returns one to the table.

60品Sanjūshichihon bodai bunpōThe Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening

Central claimThe early Buddhist path is not a lesser way; Dōgen places the Buddha’s own practices at the center of Zen.

This is the most detailed treatment of the basic Buddhist teachings in the Shōbōgenzō. The thirty-seven factors are an early list of practices conducive to awakening, drawn from the oldest sources: the four foundations of mindfulness (body, feelings, mind, and objects of mind), the four right efforts, the four bases of spiritual power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, and the eightfold path.

Dōgen reviews each group and comments on it. He treats them not as beginner’s material to be left behind but as the substance of the buddhas’ teaching and the content of the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye itself. In his view they are continuous with sitting and are practiced fully by those who sit.

The effect is to place Zen squarely within the Buddha’s own teaching and to reject the idea that it is a separate, higher path that no longer needs the basics. The foundations are not outgrown. If a teacher claims to be beyond mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom, this fascicle holds that no one is.

Paraphrased passages
These thirty-seven are not a lesser path; they are the treasury of the true dharma eye.
No one outgrows mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom.

Discernment. A teaching that leaves the basics behind has left the Buddha behind; Dōgen grounds Zen in the earliest practices.

61龍RyūginSong of the Dragon

Central claimWhat appears dead can sing; when grasping stops, vitality appears.

The title comes from a saying attributed to the teacher Xiangyan, who, asked about the way, answered that a dragon sings in a withered tree. The phrase combines an image of lifeless wood with a mythic creature whose voice is thunder.

Dōgen reads the withered tree as the condition of a person who has dropped attachments, a body and mind no longer pushing in any direction. From outside it looks like nothing, dry and unadorned, yet from that condition a voice arises that is vast and alive. The song is not made by effort; it is what the tree does when it is simply there.

He combines the image with other sayings that use dead wood and cold ashes to describe the silent, unmoving mind, but separates this carefully from deadness. A meditation in which nothing happens and nothing is felt is not what he means: the tree is dry, but the dragon sings. Apparent barrenness may be a condition for something, and the task is not to force it into color but to let it be and attend to it.

Paraphrased passages
A dragon sings in a withered tree.
What looks like nothing is not nothing; the song is there.

Discernment. Dōgen distinguishes real stillness from dull numbness, which are easily confused; real stillness has life in it.

62西Soshi seirai iThe Intention of the Ancestor’s Coming from the West

Central claimThe point of Zen is not a hidden doctrine but the plain actuality shown in each answer.

The question of why Bodhidharma came from India to China is the most famous question in Chan, a way of asking for the point of the whole teaching. Teachers gave many different answers, which became cases for study.

Dōgen collects a number of these and examines them. His repeated claim is that the ancestor’s intention is not a separate message to which the answers point. Each answer, from the cypress tree in the courtyard to something even more ordinary, is itself the intention: the ancestor came to show what is already present before everyone.

The fascicle also resists treating these answers as clever tricks aimed at producing surprising statements. They are not performances but what the person saw at that moment, and they work because the questioner was meant to look at the thing, not puzzle over the reply. The text is quietly demanding on discernment: the real thing is not obscure but plain and often unexpected, and it needs little staging, in contrast to a show of being enigmatic.

Paraphrased passages
The ancestor came to show what is already before everyone.
A cryptic answer is not deeper than a plain one.

Discernment. Mystification is a common counterfeit of depth; Dōgen’s answers are plain.

63發Hotsu mujō shinRaising the Supreme Mind

Central claimThe vow to benefit others before oneself is the supreme mind; it begins with a decision available to anyone.

The supreme mind is the aspiration for complete awakening for the sake of all beings, bodhicitta in Sanskrit. In Mahāyāna it is the beginning and root of the whole path, the point at which a person takes up the way for something larger than personal comfort or advantage.

Dōgen discusses what it means to raise this mind and why it matters. He emphasizes that it is not reserved for an elite: it can occur in an ordinary person in unlikely circumstances and does not depend on prior achievement. What matters is that it is a genuine turning, a decision that changes what one is working for.

He also distinguishes the aspiration from the pursuit of reputation. Many take up practice for status, admiration, or an interesting identity, a motive he regards as common and corrosive. The vow to help others corrects it by pointing away from the self. The purpose behind work matters: work done for praise tends to bend toward what is praised, whereas work done for something beyond oneself tends to steady.

Paraphrased passages
To raise the supreme mind is to vow to help others cross before oneself.
Anyone can make this turn, regardless of position.

Discernment. Examine the motive: practice undertaken for reputation is already off course, and Dōgen says so plainly.

64優Udon geThe Udumbara Flower

Central claimThe flower is not a symbol of the transmission; it is the transmission as it actually occurred.

The udumbara is a legendary flower said to bloom only once in a very long time. In the story behind Zen’s claim of a special transmission, the Buddha held up a flower before an assembly, and no one responded except Mahākāśyapa, who smiled. The Buddha then said that he possessed the treasury of the true dharma eye and entrusted it to Mahākāśyapa.

Dōgen takes up the story and rejects the standard interpretation in which the flower is a gesture standing for a silent teaching beyond words. For him the holding up of the flower and the smile are the teaching, fully expressed and received, not a code to be decoded. Nor is the story about a magical communication between two special people: the transmission is carried in ordinary activity, and the flower is a flower.

The fascicle belongs with those that guard against turning Zen into a narrative of mystical elitism. The tradition is not a secret club with special signals but a line of people who have shown in plain actions what the teaching is. For anyone sifting claims of hidden knowledge it is a useful corrective.

Paraphrased passages
The flower held up is not a symbol; it is the teaching fully expressed.
The line begins with an ordinary act, plainly done.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses a mystical elitism around the tradition’s own founding story.

65如Nyorai zenshinThe Whole Body of the Tathagata

Central claimThe Buddha’s real body is the teaching, which includes the scrolls and the one who practices it.

A phrase in the Lotus Sūtra speaks of the Tathāgata’s relics, and in some places the sūtras themselves are described as the whole body of the Tathāgata. Dōgen takes up this claim and works through what it means.

In an earlier tradition the Buddha’s remains were enshrined in stūpas and reverence was directed to the physical relics. Mahāyāna moved the center of reverence to the teaching: the body of the Buddha is the dharma, and the sūtra scrolls are its form. Dōgen goes a step further, saying that the whole body of the Buddha is not limited to written texts but is present wherever the teaching is practiced.

This has a consequence for how both texts and practitioners are treated. A scroll is to be honored, but so is a person living the teaching, who is also the body of the Buddha. The fascicle becomes a reflection on what is worth revering: not a relic as an object of superstition, not a text as a prop, but the living teaching as it appears in action. It offers a means of sorting substance from decoration in a tradition.

Paraphrased passages
The sūtra scrolls are the whole body of the Tathāgata.
The living practice is also the Buddha’s body.

Discernment. Reverence should be directed at the living teaching, not at objects or reputations.

66王Zanmai ō zanmaiThe King of Samadhis Samadhi

Central claimSitting upright is the king of concentrations; nothing replaces it and nothing is above it.

In this fascicle Dōgen makes his most emphatic claim about zazen. Sitting with crossed legs, he says, is the king of samādhis, the practice that contains all others, and the whole body in that posture is the form of the buddhas and ancestors.

He reviews the traditional language for the posture, including the old description of the cross-legged form as the skin, flesh, bones, and marrow of the Buddha. In his reading, to sit is to express the entire teaching, and there is nothing further toward which sitting aims.

He is pointed about those who treat the posture as optional. He quotes teachers who emphasized sitting and criticizes those who claim that real Zen can dispense with it or substitute something more exciting. He treats the loss of the sitting posture as the loss of the buddha way itself. The text reflects his conviction that the practice he brought from China was complete and needed no supplement: sitting is the one practice that does not become obsolete.

Paraphrased passages
Sitting cross-legged is the king of samādhis.
To lose the sitting posture is to lose the buddha way.

Discernment. Dōgen is blunt about practices that claim to replace the core; the simple thing remains the center.

67轉Ten hōrinTurning the Dharma Wheel

Central claimThe wheel is turning now wherever the teaching is practiced; one both turns it and is turned by it.

Turning the dharma wheel is the traditional description of the Buddha’s first discourse, delivered to five ascetics at Deer Park. The wheel is the teaching, and its turning is the start of a continuing activity.

Dōgen reads the image as describing an event that is not finished. The wheel is not a historical act but is turning whenever the dharma is practiced, taught, or heard. One who sits down to practice turns the wheel, and the wheel also turns that person.

The emphasis on mutuality is typical. A teaching is not an object that one picks up and uses; it acts on one as one acts with it, and the line between user and used becomes unclear. This is the same relation as in the boat that carries the rider who steers it. The fascicle is brief and recommends approaching the teaching as a participant rather than an owner: something is already moving, and the practitioner joins it.

Paraphrased passages
The wheel is turning wherever the dharma is practiced.
One turns the wheel, and the wheel turns one.

Discernment. A teaching owned outright is not the teaching; Dōgen keeps the practitioner inside something larger.

68修Dai shugyōGreat Practice

Central claimEven the greatly awakened do not escape cause and effect; to claim otherwise is a grave error.

The fascicle retells the story of Baizhang and the fox. After a lecture, an old man who regularly attended stayed behind and explained that long ago he had been a teacher on that mountain. A student had asked whether a greatly practiced person falls into cause and effect, and he had answered that such a person does not fall into cause and effect. For this answer he had been reborn as a fox for five hundred lifetimes. He asked Baizhang to answer the question properly. Baizhang said that such a person does not obscure cause and effect, and the old man was released.

Dōgen examines the two phrases, “does not fall into” and “does not obscure,” with great care. The first suggests that realization places one outside the law of consequence; the second says that realization sees consequence clearly and does not hide from it. He holds that the difference between them is the difference between delusion and truth.

What emerges is a firm statement that practice does not lift anyone above the consequences of their actions. Cause and effect is the structure of the world, and an awakened person lives in it with open eyes. This is among his clearest teachings on discernment: anyone who claims that their realization exempts them from accountability stands where the old man stood before his release.

Paraphrased passages
Does a greatly practiced person fall into cause and effect? Such a person does not obscure cause and effect.
To claim to stand outside consequence is the fox’s mistake.

Discernment. The most direct test of a claim to realization is whether it accepts consequence and accountability.

69證Jishō zanmaiThe Samadhi of Self-Verification

Central claimVerification is not private certainty; it is confirmed by the dharma and by a real teacher.

Self-verification, in Dōgen’s usage, does not mean what it may appear to. It is not the experience of being inwardly convinced of one’s own awakening, and the self in question is not a private subject judging itself. It is the self verified by all things, in the manner of Genjōkōan, where allowing the myriad things to come forward confirms the self.

He argues that real verification occurs in practice and is then recognized by a teacher who has had the same experience. A person who claims to have verified themselves without reference to a lineage or to any external check has misunderstood the term.

The fascicle includes a warning against a self-assured individualism already present in some circles, in which the language of direct realization was taken as license to bypass teachers and traditions. Dōgen sees this as a risk and responds by tying verification to relationship. The distinction between feeling certain and being verified is practical: certainty is easy to have and a poor indicator, whereas a skilled teacher, a careful reading of the tradition, and an honest examination of one’s conduct can test it.

Paraphrased passages
Self-verification is not self-certification.
Feeling certain is not the same as being verified.

Discernment. Private certainty is cheap; Dōgen insists on confirmation by the dharma and by someone who has actually seen it.

70空KokūEmpty Space

Central claimSpace is not an absence; it is the room in which everything occurs, and it is itself a way the teaching appears.

Kokū means empty space or sky, and in Buddhist writing it often denotes the open expanse that contains all things. It can suggest the void, and in some teachings it stands for negation, an absence of everything.

Dōgen does not accept the negative reading. For him space is as real as a mountain and is part of the teaching. The sky is not a nothing between things but the openness that lets things be what they are. He draws on Chan sayings in which space is spoken of as a body with its own function.

He also connects space with practice. One who sits well is spacious, in the sense of not being filled with agendas and opinions, and this openness is not vacancy but a capacity to receive what comes without distortion. By analogy, leaving room in a layout, a schedule, or a conversation is not waste but what allows the other parts to function. Emptiness is not nihilism; space is active, not empty.

Paraphrased passages
Space is not a nothing between things; it is the room in which things are.
An open mind is not vacant; it is available.

Discernment. Emptiness is not nihilism; Dōgen makes space a positive condition of seeing clearly.

71鉢HouThe Almsbowl

Central claimThe bowl is the Buddha’s body; the genuine form is to be used with care, not replaced by a convenient substitute.

The almsbowl is the container a monk uses for food, and from the beginning of Buddhism it has been one of the monk’s few possessions. Dōgen’s fascicle concerns its history, meaning, and use, and he traces the bowl back to the Buddha and to the transmission from Mahākāśyapa.

The text gives practical details on the shape, material, and size of the proper bowl and on how the nested set should be arranged. Dōgen had seen how the bowl was used in Chinese monasteries and knew that in many places the proper form was neglected or mixed with other styles. His response is firm: he insists on the traditional bowl and on using it as the buddhas and ancestors did.

His reasoning is that form carries teaching. The bowl is not merely a utensil but a link with a line of people who used the same vessel in the same way, and substituting a different form for convenience or fashion cuts that link. A modern reader may question how much of this is essential. The underlying point, however, is relevant to discernment: a tradition that is careless about its forms, or adapts them to suit marketing, must explain what it keeps and what it surrenders.

Paraphrased passages
The bowl is the body of the buddhas and ancestors.
A convenient substitute breaks the link with the line.

Discernment. Dōgen holds the forms of the tradition to exact standards; casual substitution is how a lineage quietly drifts.

72安AngoThe Practice Period

Central claimCommit to a fixed container of practice and remain within it; the container does part of the work.

Ango, the practice period, is a retreat of about three months during which monks remain in the monastery and devote themselves to training. It originates in the rainy season in India, when monks stopped wandering and stayed in one place; in China and Japan it became a fixed part of the year.

Dōgen gives the history and the rules, explaining the dates, the purpose, and the obligations. A monk who enters the practice period commits to staying and to following the schedule, and leaving early is a serious matter.

A deeper idea lies in the text: the container itself is part of the practice. A fixed period, place, and schedule remove the daily question of whether to practice today. The choice has been made, and practice is what remains; Dōgen treats this as a gift, not a restriction. The model holds outside a monastery as well, since many efforts to build a practice or a body of work fail because the daily decision is renegotiated each morning.

Paraphrased passages
Within a fixed period, the question of whether to practice today is already answered.
The container does part of the work.

Discernment. A tradition with real forms of commitment is easier to trust than one that offers only inspiration.

73他Tashin tsūPenetration of Other Minds

Central claimMind-reading powers prove nothing; a genuine teacher cannot be found out by a trick.

The fascicle is built around a well-known story about National Teacher Huizhong and an Indian monk, Daer Sanzang, said to have the power to read minds. The emperor invited the visitor to the capital, and Huizhong came to test him.

On the first occasion, Huizhong asked where he was, and the visitor said he was at the West River boat. On the second, the visitor said he was on the bridge watching a monkey show. On the third occasion the visitor could not find him. Huizhong then said that this fox spirit, who claimed to know the minds of others, did not know where he was.

Dōgen uses the case to show what the story tests. A power to read what is in other minds can find only a mind that is somewhere; it cannot find a mind fixed nowhere. The third time Huizhong could not be found because he had no position to be located. The test shows that a powerful trick is not the same as understanding. It accords with the fascicle on spiritual powers: impressive displays are never the criterion, and one who can perform a trick may be less clear than one who can perform none.

Paraphrased passages
The third time, the mind-reader could not find the teacher; there was no place to find.
A power to read minds is not wisdom.

Discernment. Dōgen shows that a spectacular ability can be entirely out-seen by ordinary clarity.

74索Ō saku sendabaThe King Asks for Saindhava

Central claimDiscernment is responding rightly to what is actually meant in this moment, not following a rule.

The Sanskrit word saindhava has several meanings: salt, water, a bowl, or a horse. The story concerns a king whose attendant was clever enough to know which meaning the king intended at any moment. When the king was washing his hands and said saindhava, the attendant brought water; at a meal, salt; after the meal, a cup; and when the king was about to go out, his horse.

Dōgen takes the story as a model of right response. The attendant had no rule; he knew the situation, read the person, and gave exactly what was needed. For Dōgen this pictures what a practitioner is meant to do: see the actual circumstances and act accordingly.

The fascicle makes several points about teaching and learning. A teacher’s words must be received in context, and an attendant who brought the wrong item would be foolish however knowledgeable. Equally, a student must learn to read the situation and not cling to a single meaning. Many fields have rules; the skill that makes the difference is knowing what the moment calls for.

Paraphrased passages
The king said saindhava; the wise attendant brought water, salt, a cup, or the horse, as the moment required.
Knowing what is needed is not a matter of knowing a rule.

Discernment. Discernment is the skill of reading the actual situation; no formula substitutes for it.

75出ShukkeLeaving Home

Central claimLeaving home is the beginning of practice, and it means leaving the habits of the self as well as a house.

In the Buddhist tradition leaving home means taking up the life of a monk or nun. The Buddha himself left his family and palace to seek awakening, and the act has been the model for those who follow.

Dōgen’s fascicle is partly a defense of monastic life and partly an examination of its meaning. He argues that leaving home is the fundamental act of the buddhas and ancestors, the thing they all did, and stresses that it is not an escape from responsibilities but the assumption of a larger one.

He also makes clear that the outward act is insufficient. A person can leave home in the outward sense and remain bound to every habit and ambition that home represented. The real departure is internal, a letting go of the picture of oneself as someone who must protect and enlarge a position; without it the robe and shaved head are costume. The question the fascicle poses, even to non-monastics, is whether the turn toward practice has actually changed what one lives for.

Paraphrased passages
Leaving home is the act of all the buddhas and ancestors.
A robe worn over unchanged ambitions is only a costume.

Discernment. The outward form is not the proof; Dōgen asks whether the inward turn has actually occurred.

Part two: the twelve-fascicle collection

Texts of Dōgen’s final years, ending with his last fascicle.

01功Shukke kudokuThe Merit of Leaving Home

Central claimCommitment makes practice real; a serious turn must cost something and must be formal.

Among the last texts Dōgen wrote, this fascicle praises ordination, the formal step from lay life into the monastic path. He gathers statements from the sūtras and the ancestors on the great benefit of leaving home and holding the precepts and states plainly that the merit exceeds that of any ordinary good.

The text may be read as a simple recommendation of monasticism, but its principal concern is seriousness. Dōgen had watched many people treat practice as an interest to be fitted around an otherwise unchanged life. His late writing recalls what he thought the way required: a decision, a vow, and a form that makes the decision public and hard to retract.

He also seeks to dispel the notion that the outward act is empty ritual. In his view form and intention support one another: the ceremony expresses a commitment already begun in the heart, and the commitment is strengthened by being made concrete. For non-monastics the principle still applies: whatever is taken up seriously benefits from a point of visible commitment, since vague intention rarely lasts.

Paraphrased passages
The merit of leaving home cannot be matched by ordinary good deeds.
A vow made in the open is harder to abandon than a private wish.

Discernment. Dōgen distrusts casual interest; in his late work the test of seriousness is a commitment not easily undone.

02戒JukaiReceiving the Precepts

Central claimThe precepts are the way the awakened live, taken up by decision and ceremony and renewed daily.

Another of Dōgen’s late texts, this fascicle describes the formal reception of the sixteen bodhisattva precepts: the three refuges in Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha; the three pure precepts, which are to avoid evil, to do good, and to benefit all beings; and the ten grave precepts. The grave precepts forbid killing, stealing, misusing sexuality, lying, dealing in intoxicants, speaking of others’ faults, praising oneself while criticizing others, stinginess, anger, and disparaging the three treasures.

Dōgen presents reception of the precepts as entry into the Buddha’s way. They are not a rulebook arriving from outside but the content of awakened conduct, handed on from the buddhas and made one’s own through the ceremony. He also stresses repentance as part of the process, an honest acknowledgment of wrongs committed.

He is careful about how the ceremony is conducted. It requires a proper teacher who has himself received the precepts in an unbroken line, and a sincere student; casual or commercial conferral does not count. The fascicle draws together much of what he says about authenticity: ethics is not decoration but the shape of practice, and it must be transmitted by someone who can be held to the same standard.

Paraphrased passages
To receive the precepts is to enter the ranks of the buddhas.
Precepts handed out casually are not precepts.

Discernment. The ethical core of the tradition is not optional; Dōgen links it to a traceable transmission and an accountable teacher.

03袈Kesa kudokuThe Merit of the Robe

Central claimThe robe is not decoration; it is to be made and worn correctly, and its form is part of the teaching.

Dōgen’s long essay on the kāṣāya, the Buddhist robe, is one of the more extended treatments in the Shōbōgenzō. He explains its origins, its meaning, how it should be made, and why it deserves reverence. The robe is a patchwork garment made from discarded cloth, a deliberate rejection of luxury, and the mark of one who has left home.

He insists on the correct form. The pattern of stitching, the choice of cloth, and the manner of wearing are all part of what makes the robe what it is, and he criticizes the mixing of styles and the substitution of fancier or more convenient materials as a loss of the tradition’s meaning. A strong note of devotion runs through the text: Dōgen cites many accounts of the power of the robe and the merit of making, wearing, and honoring it, some of them miraculous, which a modern reader may take as expressions of reverence rather than literal claims.

The theme relevant to discernment is fidelity. A tradition’s forms are not arbitrary, and abandoning them for taste or marketing has consequences. Dōgen’s severity may seem excessive, but it derives from a consistent conviction that the way is carried in the details, not only in ideas.

Paraphrased passages
The robe is the buddhas’ own garment; make it and wear it as they did.
Changing the form to suit convenience loses what the form carries.

Discernment. Dōgen is strict about form because he treats fidelity as a test of seriousness.

04菩Hotsu bodai shinArousing the Aspiration for Awakening

Central claimThe beginning of the path is the vow to awaken for the sake of others; without it practice drifts into self-interest.

The aspiration for awakening, bodhicitta, is the starting point of the bodhisattva path. Dōgen takes it up in a late fascicle that appears directed to those within the monastery as much as to a broader audience.

His central claim is that the aspiration is more than a feeling of inspiration. It is a commitment to awaken in order to help all beings and requires willingness to put others first. He warns against taking up practice for personal gain, reputation, comfort, or even the satisfaction of a spiritual identity, motives that are common and corrosive.

He also encourages. The aspiration can be raised by anyone, in any condition, even when a person is sick, ashamed, or distracted, and it requires neither a clean record nor a stable situation. It is renewed whenever it is remembered and can be raised again after being forgotten. The text is sober and warm together: sober in insisting on purpose, warm in assuring that anyone may begin. The question it poses, for whom the effort is made, shapes everything that follows.

Paraphrased passages
Awaken for the sake of others, and do not seek fame or profit by it.
The aspiration can be raised again at any time.

Discernment. Practice undertaken for reputation is already off course; the check is the purpose behind the effort.

05供Kuyō shobutsuMaking Offerings to the Buddhas

Central claimOffering is a practice of generosity; a sincere small gift is worth more than an extravagant empty one.

To make offerings is to present incense, flowers, light, food, and other gifts to the buddhas and, by extension, to teachers and the community. Dōgen gives the practice serious attention in a late fascicle that gathers stories and sayings about its merit.

He is clear that the value of an offering lies not in its cost. A handful of grass, a bowl of water, or a single flower offered in good faith is complete, whereas an elaborate offering made from vanity or the desire to be seen is empty. The act trains the mind away from stinginess and toward giving.

He also ties the practice to reverence for the Three Treasures and for the living line of teachers. To make an offering is to recognize that one has received something, which counters the habit of treating teaching and tradition as a service that one pays for and then owns. The principle generalizes to giving without a hidden bill and acknowledging what one has been given, which Dōgen treats as part of the way, not as an extra.

Paraphrased passages
A single flower offered sincerely is complete.
An offering made to be seen is only a display.

Discernment. Sincerity is the test; Dōgen measures an act by the intention behind it, not by its size or visibility.

06歸Kie buppōsōbōTaking Refuge in the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha

Central claimRefuge is the doorway; without it nothing else in the way stands on solid ground.

Taking refuge in the Three Treasures, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, is the formal act that marks one as a Buddhist. It is the first of the precepts and the first thing a student does in the ordination ceremony.

Dōgen’s late fascicle treats the practice with great seriousness. He cites teachers and sūtras to show that taking refuge is no small matter: it is the point at which a person turns from seeking shelter in worldly securities, such as reputation, wealth, and power, to relying on the way the buddhas walked. He also stresses that those who take refuge sincerely are met by the power of the Three Treasures.

An important point is that he treats the three as objects of genuine respect. The Buddha is not an abstract ideal, the Dharma is not a set of ideas, and the Saṅgha is not a social club; each is a living reality, and the community, with all its flaws, is part of the refuge. The fascicle makes a plain statement: everyone relies on something, and the question is whether what is relied upon will hold. For the Three Treasures Dōgen’s answer accords with all his writing: the way proven by a line of people over time.

Paraphrased passages
To take refuge is to turn from shaky securities to the way the buddhas walked.
The community, with its flaws, is part of the refuge.

Discernment. Everyone relies on something; Dōgen asks what has actually held up over time.

07因Jinshin ingaDeep Faith in Cause and Effect

Central claimCause and effect must not be denied; those who deny it are outside the buddha way, however advanced they appear.

Cause and effect, in Buddhist terms, means that actions have consequences and that this is part of the structure of the world. In some Chan circles of Dōgen’s day the language of emptiness and non-attachment was used to suggest that the awakened were beyond such concerns and that talk of consequence was for beginners.

Dōgen rejects this strongly. The fascicle opens with the story of Baizhang and the fox, which also appears in Daishugyō, and uses it to make the point as sharply as possible. A teacher who said that a person of great practice does not fall into cause and effect was reborn for five hundred lifetimes as a fox; the correction, that such a person does not obscure cause and effect, ended the suffering.

He then marshals a long list of arguments and quotations to show that denial of cause and effect is heresy. The tone is severe, and he states plainly that those who hold this view are outside the way. The fascicle is among the most direct statements of the link between realization and ethics: a teaching that leads people to believe that their actions do not matter, or that realization excuses harm, is false.

Paraphrased passages
Do not deny cause and effect.
A teaching that excuses harm in the name of emptiness is not the buddha way.

Discernment. The clearest lesson in the late work: no realization places anyone above consequence.

08業Sanji gōKarma of the Three Times

Central claimActions bear fruit in this life, the next, or later; nothing should be assumed to escape.

Karma, in the traditional account, ripens at different times. Some actions produce their results in this very life, some in the next, and some in lives further off. Dōgen’s short fascicle explains this and presents it as an essential teaching.

The reason he stresses it relates to the problem of apparent injustice: a good person suffers while a bad person prospers, and an observer concludes that actions do not matter. Dōgen’s answer is that results are not always visible on a short timescale. The fruit is certain, though its timing is not.

He also uses the teaching to caution against complacency. One who has done something harmful and is not yet suffering for it should not conclude that nothing will follow, and one who has done good and received no reward should not give up. The teaching is not meant to be mechanical or punitive but a way of taking responsibility seriously over the long run. For consequential work the appropriate approach is to do it properly regardless of the immediate reaction.

Paraphrased passages
Some karma ripens now, some next, some later.
Not yet seeing the result is not the same as there being no result.

Discernment. Dōgen asks for a long view of conduct; short-term outcomes are a poor guide to what is right.

09馬ShibaFour Horses

Central claimRespond at the shadow of the whip; do not wait for pain before beginning to practice.

A passage in the early sūtras describes four kinds of horses. The best starts moving at the mere shadow of the whip; the second moves when the whip touches its hair; the third when it touches the skin; the fourth only when the pain reaches the bone. The image was used to describe different kinds of students, from those who respond to the faintest hint to those who require a serious crisis.

Dōgen applies the image to practice. The teaching of impermanence is the whip, and the question is how soon one responds. One who sees that life is passing and that death may come at any time can begin practicing at once; one who waits for illness, loss, or disaster is the fourth horse.

His emphasis is both encouraging and urgent. All four horses are capable of running, and no one is excluded, but the sooner one begins the better. Delay is dangerous because it assumes there will always be another day. One who responds to the shadow of the whip is not anxious but simply awake to how short the time is.

Paraphrased passages
The best horse moves at the shadow of the whip.
Do not wait for the pain to reach the bone.

Discernment. A teaching that advises starting later is suspect; Dōgen’s urgency is plain and kind.

10禪Shizen bikuThe Monk of the Fourth Dhyana

Central claimA deep meditative state is not realization; mistaking one for the other is a catastrophic error.

The fascicle retells a story from the Buddhist sources about a monk who reached the fourth dhyāna, a state of deep meditative absorption in which breathing seems to stop and the mind becomes extremely still. The monk took this as proof that he had attained the final fruit of the path and told others so.

At death, according to the story, he saw signs of rebirth that did not match his expectations. In confusion and anger he concluded that the Buddha’s teaching was false, and the consequence of that mistake was a descent into a terrible realm. His error was double: he had taken a high state of concentration for liberation, and he had then denied the truth when reality did not conform.

Dōgen uses the story as a warning to practitioners and teachers. Intense experiences, however profound, are not self-validating; they can be produced by skillful practice and still not be what they seem. The only protection is accurate knowledge of the tradition and willingness to be corrected. Claims of attainment resting mainly on an experience, especially one the claimant cannot test against the teachings or a reliable teacher, warrant caution.

Paraphrased passages
A state of deep stillness is not the same as liberation.
To claim an attainment one does not have endangers oneself and others.

Discernment. The work’s most direct warning against mistaking an experience for realization, and against teachers who claim attainments they have not tested.

11百Ippyakuhachi hōmyō monThe One Hundred and Eight Gates of Illumination

Central claimThere are many entrances to the one way, and each gate is the whole of it.

This text is distinctive in the Shōbōgenzō and was lost for centuries. It survives in a single manuscript discovered only in the twentieth century, which is why it is sometimes omitted from older editions. It is built as a litany, working through one hundred and eight gates of illumination.

The number has a long association in Buddhism with the one hundred and eight defilements, the varied forms of delusion. Here it is inverted, and each gate is a way of entering clarity. Dōgen treats the list as a recitation in which each item is a gate that opens onto the whole.

Because the text is rarely discussed, a summary should remain modest. It expresses a belief that appears throughout his work: the way does not have a single narrow entrance but can be entered through many doors, each of them complete, and one at one gate stands in no lesser place than one at another. The implied attitude is generosity in method and strictness in practice; it does not endorse every approach, but it recognizes that the same thing can be approached from many sides.

Paraphrased passages
There are many gates, and each is the whole way.
No gate is a lesser place.

Discernment. Dōgen is open about entry points and strict about what must be done once inside.

12八Hachi dainin gakuThe Eight Awakenings of Great Beings

Central claimDesire little, be content, seek quiet, be diligent, maintain mindfulness, practice concentration, cultivate wisdom, and avoid idle talk.

This is the last fascicle Dōgen wrote, in 1253, the year he died. It is based on a teaching traditionally attributed to the Buddha’s final instructions to his disciples, which Dōgen presents as the essence of the way. It describes eight qualities that awakened beings practice and that those who wish to awaken can cultivate.

The first is having few desires; the second, contentment with what one has; the third, enjoyment of quiet, a preference for solitude and stillness over agitation; the fourth, diligence, the steady, unhurried effort that persists; the fifth, not forgetting mindfulness, remaining with what is present; the sixth, practicing concentration, the settled mind of samādhi; the seventh, practicing wisdom, the clear seeing of how things are; and the eighth, avoiding idle talk, a refusal to dissipate attention in frivolous chatter and argument.

Dōgen’s closing note carries the force of a farewell. He emphasizes that these are not preliminary teachings but the whole path: one who practices these eight has understood the Buddha’s teaching, and nothing is left out. The text can be applied as a daily checklist: want less, be content, protect quiet, persevere, attend, concentrate, see accurately, and speak less.

Paraphrased passages
Few desires, contentment, quiet, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, and no idle talk.
Those who want many things have much trouble; those who want little are at ease.

Discernment. Dōgen’s last word is practical and checkable; one can see within a day whether one is doing it.

01辨BendōwaA Talk on Endeavoring in the Way

Central claimSit, and let the sitting itself be practice and realization together; this one gate is complete.

Written in 1231, shortly after Dōgen’s return from China, Bendōwa is his earliest major statement and is often printed with the Shōbōgenzō. It is cast as a series of questions and answers in which an imagined questioner presses him on the unusual claims he is making.

He opens by stating what he has brought: the buddha dharma transmitted in an unbroken line from Śākyamuni, and above all the practice of zazen, which he calls the right gate. He describes zazen as a self-fulfilling samādhi, an activity complete in itself that aims at nothing outside the sitting. When a person sits upright, even briefly, he says, they express the Buddha’s posture with the whole body, and the entire world joins in.

The questions pursue obvious objections. Why only sitting? Why not chanting, incense, bowing, or reading? Must one be a monk? Can laypeople awaken? What of the view that the mind is permanent while the body perishes? To each he gives a firm answer. He rejects the notion of an eternal inner spirit, and he says that those who think they can skip sitting for other practices have not understood what is being offered. The text also includes his account of his search for a true teacher, and is as much an argument for authenticity as for a method: the way must be received from someone who has it and then tested in practice.

Paraphrased passages
Practice and realization are one; sitting is not a means to awakening but awakening taking place.
Even a single period of upright sitting expresses the buddhas’ posture, and the whole world joins in.

Discernment. The whole treatise argues for verified transmission and complete practice, against casual blending and shortcuts.

02生ShōjiBirth and Death

Central claimDo not flee birth and death, and do not cling to them; let go into the buddha’s way and follow it.

A short text on the problem of birth and death, the basic human concern. It is not part of Dōgen’s own arrangement of the Shōbōgenzō, but it appears in the traditional Sōtō editions and has long been read with it. Some scholars question how much of it comes directly from him.

Its main idea is that birth and death, the cycle of ordinary existence, is itself the life of the Buddha. To try to escape birth and death in order to find the Buddha elsewhere is to lose the Buddha’s life, and to cling to birth and death as if solid and final is to be lost in them. The way lies in the middle of what is occurring.

The text ends with a famous instruction: throw body and mind into the house of the Buddha and let the Buddha act on them. Following along without effort of body or mind and without grasping, one leaves birth and death behind and becomes a buddha. The message is trust in practice, not in a plan. Together with the fascicle on total dynamism it forms a pair on how to face mortality, neither avoiding nor fixating but taking part fully in what is occurring.

Paraphrased passages
Birth and death is itself the buddha’s life.
Do not hate birth and death; do not cling to them.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses both escapism and fixation; the honest posture toward mortality is full participation.

03唯Yuibutsu yobutsuOnly a Buddha Together with a Buddha

Central claimRealization is mutual; it is verified by those who have it, and no one confirms themselves alone.

The title is taken from the Lotus Sūtra’s statement that only a buddha together with a buddha can fully fathom the true reality of all things. Dōgen returns to this line repeatedly, and this fascicle takes it as its main subject.

His claim is that the realization of the dharma cannot be assessed from outside: one who has not realized it cannot judge it, and one who has realized it is not alone. The knowledge is shared and recognized between persons; a buddha knows a buddha, and transmission is carried in that recognition. A student cannot decide alone that they have it, and a teacher cannot give it to someone unready or unwilling. Both are part of a mutual process requiring time and honesty.

The fascicle also contains a certain humility. If only buddhas fully know buddhas, those still learning should be slow to pass final judgment on others and quick to accept the check of a teacher who knows more. This is not a doctrine of mystique but of the limits of self-assessment. With the fascicles on self-verification and face-to-face transmission it forms the core of Dōgen’s view of how authenticity is established.

Paraphrased passages
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
No one verifies their own realization alone.

Discernment. The limits of self-assessment are built into the tradition; authenticity is established between persons, over time.

Principal founder of the Sōtō school of Zen in Japan.

  • Early life
    • Born in Kyoto in 1200 to an aristocratic family and orphaned in childhood
    • Ordained on Mount Hiei in the Tendai school
    • Troubled by an unresolved question: if all beings already possess buddha-nature, why is practice necessary?
  • Study in China
    • Studied at Kennin-ji, then traveled to Song China in 1223
    • Visited several monasteries and judged many of their teachers to hold rank without insight
    • Met Rujing at Tiantong monastery and trained under him
  • The turning point
    • In 1225, during intensive meditation, he underwent what he described as the dropping away of body and mind
    • Rujing confirmed the experience, and Dōgen received dharma transmission
  • Return and work
    • Returned to Japan in 1227, teaching that seated meditation is itself the practice of awakening
    • Taught near Kyoto until 1243, then moved to the mountains of Echizen and founded Eihei-ji in 1244
    • Died in 1253 at the age of fifty-three
  • The Shōbōgenzō
    • Composed between 1231 and 1253 in vernacular Japanese rather than classical Chinese
    • Dōgen assembled a seventy-five-fascicle collection and began a later collection of twelve
    • Modern editions contain ninety-five fascicles, counting variant versions and additional texts

5Authenticity

Distinguishing truth from delusion in teachers, in teachings, and in oneself.

Authenticity

Discernment
Question
What is true, and what is delusion?
Hardest case
The self
Method
Examine the claim, its consequences, and the examiner
Test
Conduct over time

Authenticity here denotes neither a personal brand nor a sense of being genuine, but the continuing work of distinguishing truth from delusion.

  • Definition
    • The work of distinguishing truth from delusion in teachers, in teachings, and above all in oneself
    • The self is the most difficult case, because cognition continually constructs a narrative of a unified agent, revises it as events unfold, and presents it as direct experience
    • Sustained seated meditation allows these constructive processes to be observed, including the recognition that the observer is itself part of what is constructed
    • Delusion is not a personal defect but the default condition. The Shōbōgenzō repeatedly prescribes the same discipline: examine the claim, examine its results, and examine the person doing the examining
  • Truth and delusion
    • Both are present in every situation, so discernment is never completed
    • A claim should be tested by its consequences in conduct rather than by its persuasiveness
    • Genjōkōan: carrying the self forward to verify the myriad things is delusion, while allowing the myriad things to verify the self is awakening
  • The construction of the self
    • The narrative of a continuous self is produced continually and seems most convincing when most emphatic
    • The sense of having arrived at understanding is itself a further construction, which is why Dōgen treats it as a warning sign
    • Muchū setsumu (a dream within a dream), Kūge (sky flowers), Gabyō (painted cakes)
  • The three poisons
    • GreedtonCraving for more, including for an enhanced version of oneself.
    • AngershinAversion toward whatever threatens one’s self-image.
    • DelusionchiFailure to perceive these processes, together with failure to perceive that one has failed.
    • Related fascicles: Ippyakuhachi hōmyō mon, and Shoaku makusa on the avoidance of harm.
  • Compassion for the deluded
    • Dōgen, paraphrased from Bodaisatta shishōbōAddress beings as one would address an infant in one’s care. Praise those who have virtue, and pity those who lack it.
    • Read alongside the three poisons, this implies that most people caught in greed, anger, and delusion cannot perceive them and so cannot perceive that they suffer. Blame treats an inability to see as a choice; pity treats it as a condition
    • The same applies to one’s own mind, and the same pity is owed to it

The theme in the Shōbōgenzō

The theme of discernment traced through individual fascicles.

  • Verified transmission
    • Authentic teaching passes between identifiable persons whose claims can be checked
    • Shisho, Menju, Den e, Busso, Jishō zanmai, Yuibutsu yobutsu
  • No exemption from consequence
    • No attainment excuses harm, and states of meditative absorption are not equivalent to realization
    • Dai shugyō, Jinshin inga, Sanji gō, Shoaku makusa, Shizen biku
  • The ordinary over the spectacular
    • Supernormal powers, secrecy, and display are not criteria of realization; ordinary conduct is
    • Jinzū, Tashin tsū, Hakujushi, Mitsugo, Kajō, Kobutsushin
  • Honoring what is real regardless of title
    • The dharma deserves respect wherever it appears, and criticism should be accurate
    • Raihai tokuzui, Henzan, Arakan, Butsudō, Kankin
  • Form carries the teaching
    • Precise forms and small actions are where a practice demonstrates its seriousness
    • Zazen gi, Hou, Senmen, Senjō, Kesa kudoku
  • Never finished
    • Awakening is not a fixed status, and the sense of having arrived should be treated as a warning
    • Genjōkōan, Daigo, Bukkōjōji, Gyōji, Keisei sanshoku
