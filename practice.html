Practice

Dōgen and the Shōbōgenzō, the four noble truths, the teacher Brad Warner, and the ongoing work of telling truth from delusion.

正Shōbōgenzō

The Treasury of the True Dharma Eye, one entry per fascicle. Open any entry for a quick synopsis, the takeaway, and a few paraphrased passages.

Part one: the seventy-five

Dōgen’s own arrangement, in the order of the seventy-five-fascicle collection.

01現GenjōkōanActualizing the Fundamental Point

The takeawayAwakening isn’t something you get and keep. It’s something you keep doing.

Dōgen wrote Genjōkōan in 1233 as a letter to a lay student, and it’s still his most-read piece. It opens by holding two views at once. From one angle, delusion and awakening, buddhas and ordinary beings, are distinct. From another, nothing has a fixed identity at all. The way goes beyond both views. But Dōgen doesn’t leave it at that abstraction. He brings it back to ordinary life: we still love flowers and dislike weeds, and flowers still wither and weeds still spread whatever we feel about it. Seeing past fixed categories doesn’t remove our likes and dislikes, and it doesn’t make the world behave as we’d prefer. Translators word the line differently (“in attachment blossoms fall,” “flowers fall even though we love them”), but commentators generally read it as Dōgen treating this ordinary experience as part of reality, not as a failure of practice.

The pivot is a reversal. Hauling yourself out to verify the world is delusion. Letting the world come forward and verify you is awakening. Dōgen doesn’t argue this so much as show it: a boat passenger sure the shore is moving, firewood that doesn’t turn into ash but has its own before and after, the moon sitting whole in a dewdrop without wetting it.

He closes by warning against stopping. A fish and a bird each go exactly as far as their element allows, and neither would last long hunting for its edge. In the last story, a monk fanning himself is asked why bother, since wind is everywhere. The master just keeps fanning. Realization is what practice looks like while it’s happening.

In his words, roughly
To study the way is to study the self; to study the self is to forget the self; to forget the self is to be verified by the myriad things.
When the dharma hasn’t filled you yet, you think you already have enough. When it has, you notice something’s missing.

Discernment. The second line is a handy self-check. The feeling of “I’ve got this” is your cue to look again.

02般Maka hannya haramitsuThe Great Perfection of Wisdom

The takeawayWisdom isn’t an idea about emptiness. It’s a whole person responding to whatever wind blows through.

Dōgen starts from the opening of the Heart Sūtra, where the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, practicing deep wisdom, sees that the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, volition, consciousness) are empty. Most readers treat emptiness as a verdict on those aggregates. Dōgen flips it: the aggregates are the wisdom, and seeing them clearly is practice, not a conclusion you reach afterward.

He’s pushing against a habit scholars fall into, filing the sūtra away as a teaching to be understood. Wisdom doesn’t live in the head. The whole body-mind is prajñā, and so is the world it moves through. Emptiness doesn’t mean nothing is there. It’s what lets each thing be fully what it is.

The signature image comes from his teacher Rujing: a wind bell hanging in open space, ringing the same wisdom whether the wind comes from east, west, south, or north. The bell isn’t clever about wind. It’s just hung, open, and responsive.

In his words, roughly
The whole body is like a bell hung in empty space; whatever wind comes, it rings forth prajñā.
The five aggregates aren’t in wisdom’s way. They’re wisdom at work.

Discernment. Real understanding shows up in how you respond, not in how well you can explain it.

03性BusshōBuddha-Nature

The takeawayBuddha-nature isn’t something you have or lack. It’s everything that exists, change included.

Busshō is one of the longest fascicles. Dōgen takes “all sentient beings have buddha-nature,” which usually sounds like each being carries a hidden essence, and rereads it as “all beings, the whole of being, is buddha-nature.” Nothing gets added to a being. Its existence is the nature.

He works through classic cases. When the Fifth Ancestor told Huineng, a newcomer from the south, that southerners lack buddha-nature, Huineng answered that people may be northern or southern but buddha-nature isn’t. In the case of Zhaozhou’s dog, he tells one monk a dog has buddha-nature and tells another it doesn’t. Dōgen reads neither answer as a doctrine to defend. Each is a live response to that person at that moment.

The line people quote most is Huineng’s: impermanence is itself buddha-nature. That rules out an unchanging soul sitting under the flow of things. Grass, trees, and change aren’t obstacles to the nature. They’re it. Dōgen ties this to time (see Uji): you’re not waiting for buddha-nature to arrive. You’re standing in it.

In his words, roughly
Buddha-nature isn’t having and isn’t lacking. It’s the whole being itself.
Impermanence isn’t buddha-nature’s enemy. Impermanence is buddha-nature.

Discernment. This blocks the oldest misreading in the book, the permanent inner essence, by putting realization in the changing present instead of in a private possession.

04學Shinjin gakudōLearning the Way with Body and Mind

The takeawayStudy the way with body and mind together. Neither one is the road by itself.

Dōgen says studying the way has two sides, body and mind, and you can’t pull them apart. Studying with the mind starts with the aspiration to practice and gets renewed by it. Studying with the body means sitting, posture, and the physical forms of training. Neither is optional, and neither is the “real” one hiding behind the other.

Watch how he uses “mind.” He keeps warning against reading it as the thinking, opinionated voice in your head. For him mind includes the world, so studying mind isn’t retreating inward. And the body isn’t an obstacle to get past. It’s where practice happens.

That closes off two easy exits: a spirituality that lives entirely in ideas, and a technique that’s all posture and no heart. His tone is firm. This isn’t a hobby you fit in once everything else is sorted. It’s what everything else gets arranged around.

In his words, roughly
Studying with the mind isn’t following the chatter of thought, and studying with the body isn’t ignoring the mind.
Body and mind aren’t two roads. Walk the one road with both.

Discernment. Real practice involves the whole person, not just the head or just the posture.

05即Sokushin zebutsuThe Very Mind Is Buddha

The takeaway“This very mind is Buddha” becomes a trap if you read it as a private inner self. It points to the whole activity of practice.

The slogan is everywhere in Zen, and Dōgen thinks it’s dangerous when read casually. One lazy reading says your ordinary thinking mind already is Buddha, so skip the training. Another says an eternal spirit lives inside you and survives the body. He rejects both, and cites Nanyang Huizhong, who called the idea of a permanent mind inside a perishing body a heresy.

In its place he describes mind as the whole movement of practice: arousing the aspiration, training, realizing the way, entering nirvana. Each stage is “the very mind,” and each is Buddha. The phrase doesn’t point to something inside you. It points to the entire activity of buddhas practicing.

The tone is brisk and argumentative. Dōgen is defending a phrase from its own popularity. The slogan is true, he says, in the doing, not as something to believe. Any teaching that tells you you’re already complete and asks nothing of you is a candidate for this critique.

In his words, roughly
The very mind isn’t a thing in the head. It’s arousing, practicing, realizing, and entering nirvana, all of it.
Believing in an eternal inner spirit isn’t the buddha way.

Discernment. A true teaching can go false the moment it turns into a comforting slogan.

06威Gyōbutsu igiThe Dignified Conduct of the Practicing Buddha

The takeawayAwakening shows up in concrete, dignified action. There’s no buddha apart from practice.

The title means roughly the dignified conduct of the practicing buddha. Dōgen makes a point he repeats everywhere: no buddha ever finished practicing and stepped out. Buddhas are the ones practicing. Practice isn’t prep for something else. It’s what buddhahood looks like.

That’s why he cares about igi, dignity in conduct. In monastic training, how you walk, sit, stand, eat, and bow isn’t decoration. It’s where realization takes physical shape. A buddha doesn’t float above the body. How a buddha picks up a bowl is part of the teaching.

He’s also pushing against the picture of awakening as one big flash followed by anticlimax. If a flash happens, it’s an event inside practice, not its finish. What he describes is the dignity of continuing.

In his words, roughly
Buddhas don’t stop practicing. Practice is what a buddha is doing.
How you walk, sit, and stand isn’t decoration. It’s where the teaching takes shape.

Discernment. Watch conduct, not claims. How someone handles ordinary tasks is the evidence.

07珠Ikka myōjuOne Bright Pearl

The takeawayThe whole world is one bright pearl, and you don’t need to explain it to be it.

The fascicle hangs on a short exchange. Xuansha says the whole universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl, then asks what use there is in understanding it. The next day he asks a monk how he understands it now. The monk repeats Xuansha’s own line back, and Xuansha tells him he’s living in a ghost cave in a black mountain.

Dōgen doesn’t read this as a trick. It’s about the gap between holding an idea and being the thing. The monk got the words right and turned the pearl into a concept. Xuansha’s rebuke isn’t that the words are wrong. It’s that, repeated back, they’ve gone lifeless.

For Dōgen the bright pearl isn’t a metaphor for everything at once. It’s each thing, fully itself, with nothing hidden. A pearl doesn’t need your analysis to shine. A correct statement and a living realization are different, and the difference isn’t vocabulary. It’s whether you’re speaking from inside the experience or only about it from outside.

In his words, roughly
The whole universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl. What use is understanding?
Saying the right words from the wrong place is living in a cave of ghosts.

Discernment. A right answer, repeated, can still be dead. This one trains you to hear the difference.

08心Shin fukatokuThe Mind Cannot Be Got

The takeawayYou can’t hold onto mind, past, present, or future. Stop grasping and mind is just what’s happening.

The title comes from the Diamond Sūtra: past mind can’t be obtained, present mind can’t be obtained, future mind can’t be obtained. The story is about Deshan, a scholar famous for his command of that sūtra, hauling his commentaries south on his back. He stops to buy a snack from an old woman selling cakes (the word is tenshin, a pun on “refreshing the mind”). She asks which mind he plans to refresh, since the sūtra he knows so well says none can be obtained.

Deshan has no answer. She sends him to a teacher, and tradition says he later burned his commentaries. Dōgen takes the line seriously and makes it practical. Mind isn’t an object you can hold up and inspect, because by the time you point at it, it’s changed. Trying to capture a good meditation or a clear moment is itself grasping, and letting go of that isn’t losing mind. It’s mind with nothing in the way.

There’s humility in the story too. A man who knows the text cold gets stopped by someone selling cakes.

In his words, roughly
Past mind can’t be obtained. Present mind can’t be obtained. Future mind can’t be obtained.
Which mind will you refresh? The author of the commentary has no answer.

Discernment. Book knowledge and lived understanding get tested against each other here, and the scholar has to admit the gap.

09古KobutsushinThe Old Buddha Mind

The takeawayThe mind of the ancient buddhas isn’t a secret inner state. It’s fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles.

Dōgen presented this fascicle in 1243. It opens with a monk asking National Teacher Nanyang Huizhong what the ancient buddha mind is. The answer: fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles. The monk wanted something refined and interior and got a list of ordinary objects.

Dōgen takes that at face value, then pushes it. If the ancient buddha mind were hidden inside, it would be a mystery to unlock. Instead it’s what’s right in front of you. The wall isn’t a symbol of mind. The wall is mind at work. Tile and pebble aren’t beneath awareness. They’re the shape it takes.

He’s careful about “ancient,” too. It doesn’t mean antique. It means complete, with nothing a later upgrade could add. And because it’s fully present, you can’t store it in a book or inherit it like property. Everyone has to meet it directly.

In his words, roughly
What is the ancient buddha mind? Fences, walls, tiles, pebbles.
Not a symbol of mind. The wall, doing what walls do, is mind.

Discernment. Dōgen passes on the glamorous version of awakening and points at ordinary things. That’s the opposite of a performance.

10悟DaigoGreat Awakening

The takeawayGreat awakening isn’t a single finished event. There’s awakening within awakening, and practice keeps going after it.

Dōgen is wary of “great awakening” whenever someone treats it as a finish line. Awakening, he says, isn’t a peak you reach and keep, and it has no fixed shape you could point to. An awakened person isn’t a spectator standing outside delusion.

A big thread in the fascicle: awakening and delusion aren’t simple opposites. You can awaken within delusion and be deluded within awakening. That sounds paradoxical, but it matches what serious practitioners report. A moment of clarity doesn’t guarantee a clear day, and Dōgen wants you to hold both facts without drama.

He also takes aim at using the term as a status. If someone presents themselves as having had a great awakening and therefore sits above the rest of us, his whole approach says to trust them less, not more. The real thing is quiet, shows up in ordinary activity, and doesn’t need an audience.

In his words, roughly
Great awakening isn’t finished. There’s awakening upon awakening.
An awakened person who stands above the world has misunderstood what awakened means.

Discernment. The strongest warning here is about status. Awakening offered as a credential is a red flag.

11坐Zazen giPrinciples of Zazen

The takeawaySit upright, think non-thinking, and let sitting itself be the practice.

Zazengi (Principles of Zazen) is the practical core of Dōgen’s teaching, and it reads like plain instructions. Find a quiet room, use a thick mat and a cushion, set aside your involvements, and let body and mind settle. Sit in full or half lotus with your hands one in the other, thumbs lightly touching. Spine straight, ears over shoulders, nose over navel, tongue against the palate, lips and teeth closed, eyes open, breathing quietly through the nose.

Then the mind gets addressed with a famous exchange. What do you think about while sitting still? You think of not-thinking. How do you think of not-thinking? Non-thinking (hishiryō), meaning wakeful attention that neither grasps at thoughts nor pushes them away. That, Dōgen says, is the essential art of zazen.

Across his writing, he goes further than technique. Zazen isn’t meditation as a means to something else. It’s practice and realization happening in a single act. Sitting doesn’t lead you to awakening. It’s awakening taking this shape. And when you get up, move slowly and carry the settledness into the rest of your day.

In his words, roughly
Think of not-thinking. How? Non-thinking. That’s the essential art of zazen.
Zazen isn’t a practice for gaining awakening. It’s the gate of great ease and joy.

Discernment. The details matter. Dōgen gives exact form because he doesn’t trust a practice that’s only an attitude.

12箴Zazen shinThe Lancet of Zazen

The takeawayNon-thinking isn’t a blank. It’s the free activity of a fish in water or a bird in the sky.

A lancet is the small blade you use to lance a boil, and Dōgen means this short, dense, partly poetic text to cut cleanly through confusion about sitting. It builds on an earlier poem by the Chinese teacher Hongzhi and ends with a poem of Dōgen’s own.

At the center is Yaoshan’s exchange. Asked what he thinks about while sitting so steadily, he says he thinks of not-thinking. Asked how, he says: “beyond thinking” (the same word, hishiryō, that Zazengi renders as non-thinking). Dōgen spends most of the text here. Non-thinking isn’t dullness, and it isn’t a trance with thought switched off. It isn’t thinking about some particular thing either. It’s wakefulness in which thoughts pass without being grasped.

The images are physical. A fish swims, and however far it goes there’s no end to water. A bird flies, and however far it goes there’s no end to sky. The sitter is like both, not heading for a goal but moving completely within an element. He warns against two mistakes: treating sitting as a technique that produces a result, and treating it as passivity.

In his words, roughly
Thinking of not-thinking. How? Beyond thinking.
The fish swims and there’s no end to the water. The bird flies and there’s no end to the sky.

Discernment. Dōgen turns down both the technique that promises results and the passivity that avoids work. The only way to tell the difference is to sit.

13海Kaiin zanmaiThe Ocean Seal Samadhi

The takeawayIn each moment of settled practice, the whole world shows up at once and at full depth.

The title borrows a Mahāyāna image of a samādhi, a deep concentration, in which everything is reflected the way a calm ocean holds the sky, the shore, and every passing cloud. “Seal” is meant like a stamp: one impression that fits everything.

Dōgen ties this directly to ordinary sitting. The samādhi isn’t a special state far from daily practice. The same activity that keeps your spine upright and your mind quiet holds the whole world in view, because the world isn’t outside the person sitting.

He also corrects a common picture, the ocean as one huge thing and each wave as a small, separate piece. In his version the ocean isn’t more real than the wave. Each wave is the entire ocean, right there. One moment of practice isn’t a fragment of some bigger enlightenment coming later. It’s the whole thing. The text works by echo and accumulation, so it rewards slow reading.

In his words, roughly
The ocean isn’t larger than the wave. Each wave is the whole ocean, right where it is.
One moment of settled practice isn’t part of enlightenment. It’s the whole of it.

Discernment. A real thing carries the same imprint everywhere. It doesn’t need a special stage or setting.

14華KūgeSky Flowers

The takeawayWhat looks like illusion is also the actual flowering of things. Don’t dismiss appearance, and don’t cling to it.

Sky flowers are the spots a person with a diseased eye sees floating in clear air, an old Buddhist image for delusion. They look like flowers, but there aren’t any, and the image usually makes a point about how deluded seeing produces things that aren’t real.

Dōgen takes the image and refuses the easy conclusion. If sky flowers are just illusions to dismiss, then the person dismissing them has assumed a “real” flowerless sky behind appearance and is clinging to that. He asks for a closer look: the flowers bloom and fall, and the whole world is in this blooming and falling.

This isn’t credulity about whatever you perceive. It’s a rebuke to the lazy kind of skepticism that takes pride in seeing through everything. Dōgen wants accurate seeing, and accurate seeing includes noticing that appearance has its own way of being that isn’t simply a mistake.

In his words, roughly
The flowers in the sky bloom and fall. Calling them mere illusion is just another way of clinging.
See through appearance without turning “seeing through it” into something new to cling to.

Discernment. Cynicism is a knockoff of discernment. Dōgen wants accuracy, not reflexive disbelief.

15光KōmyōIlluminating Wisdom

The takeawayLight isn’t a glow in the mind. It’s the actual world, seen without obstruction.

Dōgen builds this fascicle on a teaching of Yunmen: everyone has their own light, but when they look for it they see nothing and it’s all dark. A monk asked what everyone’s light is, and Yunmen answered with the monks’ hall, the kitchen, and the gate. Deliberately flat.

Dōgen’s reading is that illumination, in the Zen sense, isn’t a luminous inner experience. If you sit hoping to see a light, you’re looking for the wrong thing, which is why you only find darkness. The light is the ordinary world, plainly shown and available any time because it isn’t hidden. What gets in the way is the effort to find it somewhere else.

It’s a quiet correction for anyone who’s absorbed popular ideas about spiritual light, energy, or auras. Dōgen doesn’t deny that people have vivid experiences. He says they aren’t what the teaching is about, and that chasing them is the dark.

In his words, roughly
Everyone has their own light. When they look for it, they can’t see it. What is it? The hall, the kitchen, the gate.
If you’re waiting for a glow, you’re looking in the dark.

Discernment. A pointed correction to anyone selling inner light. Illumination is the plain world, seen clearly.

16持GyōjiContinuous Practice

The takeawayPractice isn’t your personal project. It’s the unbroken activity of the whole lineage, running through you.

Gyōji (Continuous Practice) is the longest and most moving of the practice fascicles, usually printed in two parts. Dōgen walks through the lives of the ancestors to show what continuous practice looked like for each. They aren’t heroes who had a breakthrough and coasted. They’re people who kept at it day after day, often unnoticed.

The best-known story is Baizhang, who kept working in the fields with the monks well into old age. Worried about him, the monks hid his tools. He refused to eat, saying a day without work is a day without eating. For Dōgen this isn’t about farming. It’s about refusing to treat practice as optional once you’re senior or tired.

He insists continuous practice can’t be treated as your own personal achievement. Its merit belongs to the whole world. You don’t practice for personal credit, and you can’t see where it starts or stops. It also sets a quiet standard: authentic practice doesn’t announce itself.

In his words, roughly
Continuous practice isn’t for yourself. It’s the practice of the whole world, passing through you.
A day without work is a day without eating.

Discernment. The test is persistence without display. Quiet, unbroken, unadvertised practice is what Dōgen holds up as the real thing.

17恁InmoThus, What Is It?

The takeawayThe real thing can’t be named like an object, but it isn’t absent. Practice and realization can’t be stained by grasping.

This fascicle turns on one of the most important exchanges in Zen. The Sixth Ancestor, Huineng, asks a newcomer, Nanyue, “What is it that thus comes?” Nanyue can’t answer and stays with the question for eight years. When he finally speaks, he says that calling it anything like a thing misses the mark.

Huineng asks whether there’s practice and realization. Nanyue says it’s not that there’s none, but they can’t be defiled. Huineng replies that this very non-defilement is what the buddhas protect and keep in mind.

Dōgen takes each line slowly. “What is it?” isn’t asking for an answer to file. It keeps a person in the actual, and the eight years matter: Nanyue didn’t produce a clever reply, he lived with the question until it became part of him. Practice and realization aren’t absent, which would make Zen a negation. They also can’t be grabbed and owned, because the grabbing is the stain. What the ancestors protect is practice’s freedom from becoming an object or an achievement.

In his words, roughly
What is it that thus comes? To say it’s like a thing misses the mark.
It’s not that there’s no practice and realization. It’s only that they can’t be defiled.

Discernment. Naming and grasping are the contaminants. Real practice stays free of both.

18觀KannonAvalokiteshvara

The takeawayCompassion is a whole-body, unplanned response. And a good teacher says so when an answer is only eight-tenths there.

The fascicle is about the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, often pictured with a thousand hands, each with an eye. Dōgen uses an exchange between two dharma brothers, Yunyan and Daowu. Yunyan asks what the great compassionate bodhisattva does with so many hands and eyes. Daowu says it’s like reaching behind you in the night for a pillow.

Yunyan says he gets it. Daowu asks what he gets. Yunyan: the whole body is hands and eyes. Daowu says that’s well put, but only eight-tenths. Yunyan asks how he’d say it. Daowu: the whole body is hands and eyes throughout.

The pillow image says compassion doesn’t involve planning, deliberating, or checking a rulebook. The body answers a need it knows without thinking. The second half matters just as much. Daowu doesn’t flatter a good answer. He pushes for the rest, which is a model of how to be honest with someone you respect.

In his words, roughly
It’s like reaching behind you in the night for the pillow.
Well said, but only eight-tenths.

Discernment. Honest evaluation is a kindness. Daowu doesn’t flatter, and Dōgen records it approvingly.

19鏡KokyōThe Old Mirror

The takeawayA mirror shows each thing exactly as it is. Realization is that kind of clarity, not something you hold on to.

Dōgen uses the old image of the mirror to talk about awareness, working through several traditional sayings. One says that when a foreigner stands in front of the mirror, a foreigner appears, and when a Chinese person does, a Chinese person appears. The mirror doesn’t pick or prefer. It shows what’s there.

He pushes further. The mirror isn’t a mind storing images, and the images aren’t impressions inside it. Mirror and reflected thing are a single event. An “old” mirror is old in having no starting point: the clarity that’s always been there.

He’s also critical of readings that make the mirror a passive device that needs polishing. In the Chan tradition, one poem about polishing the mirror to remove dust was set against another saying there’s no mirror to polish. Dōgen doesn’t pick a side. He points to practice that’s neither polishing in order to get somewhere nor dismissing the polishing. Clarity is a way of meeting things, not a state you guard.

In his words, roughly
When a foreigner comes, a foreigner appears. When a Chinese comes, a Chinese appears.
The mirror isn’t a storehouse of images. Images and mirror are one event.

Discernment. Clear seeing means not bending the picture toward what you’d like to see.

20時UjiBeing-Time

The takeawayYou’re not in time. You are time. Each moment is complete and is the whole of existence.

Uji is one of the best-known fascicles and the most philosophically daring. Dōgen takes the ordinary word uji, “sometimes,” and splits it into being (u) and time (ji). All being is time, and all time is being. A mountain, a pot, and a person are each a moment of time.

He starts from a classical verse about the forms a person might take at different times: on a mountain peak, at the bottom of the sea, with three heads and eight arms, as a sixteen-foot golden Buddha. Dōgen reads it as a series of complete beings, each whole in its own moment. His favorite image is firewood and ash. Firewood doesn’t become ash. Firewood is firewood, with its own before and after. Ash is ash, with its own before and after.

This dissolves the picture of time as a river carrying you from past to future. The past isn’t somewhere behind you waiting to be revisited. It’s part of how this moment exists. Whatever you’re doing right now is time in full, not a step toward some later moment. That’s a problem for any practice that treats itself as a means to a later state.

In his words, roughly
Time doesn’t fly past you. Each being is a moment of time, whole in itself.
Firewood doesn’t become ash. Firewood abides in its own before and after, and ash in its own.

Discernment. The hardest test of authenticity is the present moment. Dōgen won’t let practice defer its meaning to later.

21授JukiConferring Predictions

The takeawayReal confirmation is given face to face and lived. You can’t just claim it.

In Buddhist tradition, a prediction (vyākaraṇa) is the moment a buddha assures a practitioner they’ll awaken. It shows up across the sūtras, especially the Lotus Sūtra, where whole groups of disciples are told what kind of buddhas they’ll become. Dōgen asks what this ritual of assurance means inside Zen transmission.

His answer pulls it out of the future and into the present. A prediction isn’t a forecast about later. It’s recognition, in the middle of practice, that this person is already inside the way, given by someone who can actually see it. The teacher confirms. The student doesn’t appoint themselves.

That focus on who’s confirming is typical Dōgen. For him the lineage is a chain of people who verified each other. A claim to awakening with no teacher, no lineage, and nobody who’s watched you practice isn’t a prediction. It’s a story you’re telling yourself. Real recognition is external and humbling, so be careful with anyone, yourself included, who hands it out unchecked.

In his words, roughly
A prediction isn’t a forecast of later. It’s a recognition, given now, by someone who can see.
You can’t certify yourself.

Discernment. The thread is verification. Dōgen ties awakening to being seen by a real teacher, not to a private claim.

22全ZenkiTotal Dynamism

The takeawayLife and death are each the whole function. Meet each completely and don’t flee either.

Zenki means the full activity of a thing, everything working at once. Dōgen applies it to the most basic subject there is: life and death. Life isn’t a stretch of time that death eventually cuts off, and death isn’t what comes after life. Each is a complete, independent function of the whole.

His image is a person riding a boat. The rider raises the sails, works the oar, steers with the pole, and the boat carries the rider. Neither exists without the other, and it’s the rider who makes the boat a boat. Life works like that. You’re living, and life is also living you, and you can’t step outside to look at it. Birth doesn’t come from somewhere and death doesn’t go anywhere. In birth you’re entirely birth. In death you’re entirely death.

This isn’t offered as comfort. It’s harder than that. Dōgen is saying there’s no second self standing off to the side to be threatened, and he’s asking you to stop treating your life as a project that might get spoiled, because each moment is already complete.

In his words, roughly
Life is like riding in a boat. You work the sails and the oar, yet the boat carries you.
In life, be entirely life. In death, be entirely death.

Discernment. Dōgen won’t hand you comfort that isn’t true. He offers a harder, more accurate way to look at what scares us.

23月TsukiThe Moon

The takeawayThe moon isn’t a symbol for the Buddha. It’s the whole thing, present everywhere it appears.

Dōgen takes the traditional Buddhist moon, which stands for awakened nature, and does what he usually does: refuses to treat it as a metaphor. The moon isn’t like the Buddha. In this fascicle the moon is the Buddha’s body, showing in sky and water, whole in each place.

The old image of one moon reflected in many waters has a built-in weakness. It suggests one real moon far away and lots of small copies. Dōgen blocks that reading. The moon in a puddle is the whole moon, and so is the moon in a dewdrop. Each appearance is complete and none is lesser. Same logic as his ocean and wave.

He also draws on sayings where a teacher is asked about the moon and answers with something practical and plain. The teaching isn’t in a clever interpretation but in how someone responds to what’s in front of them. The fascicle is gentle and light on argument, and works by repeating a few images until they land.

In his words, roughly
The moon in the dewdrop is the whole moon, not a piece of it.
There isn’t one true moon and many copies.

Discernment. The moon is whole wherever it appears, so nobody gets to claim a bigger or more real share of the teaching.

24餠GabyōPainted Cakes

The takeawayThere’s no real world behind the painted one. Only a painted cake can satisfy hunger.

The fascicle starts from a saying attributed to the Chinese teacher Xiangyan: a painted rice cake doesn’t satisfy hunger. It sounds like common sense, and much Buddhist teaching used it as a warning that words, pictures, and doctrines are only paintings of the real thing. Dōgen turns it over.

His claim: there’s no real cake somewhere else that the painted one copies badly. Everything we encounter is painted in a sense, formed, composed, presented. Buddhas and ancestors, mountains and rivers, sūtras, and the self all belong to the same painted world. If you decide the painting’s worthless and the real is elsewhere, you haven’t seen through anything. You’ve just invented another painting.

So Dōgen says only a painted cake can satisfy hunger. The medicine for hunger isn’t outside the world of appearances. It’s using what’s present, words, images, and teachings included, with full attention. That’s a real answer to the high-minded dismissal of art, text, and craft: they’re how the dharma shows up.

In his words, roughly
A painted cake can’t satisfy hunger, says the old teacher. Dōgen answers: only a painted cake can.
Don’t look for a real world behind the painted one.

Discernment. Contempt for forms is a knockoff of insight. Dōgen gives craft, art, and language a place as the actual medium of practice.

25谿Keisei sanshokuSounds of the Valley, Forms of the Mountain

The takeawayAnything can trigger awakening, but one experience isn’t the end. Keep practicing and stay humble.

Dōgen wrote this in 1240, and it opens with a poem by Su Dongpo, a poet and official who was a layman, not a monk. After studying with a Chan teacher, he heard a stream in a valley at night and wrote that its sound was the Buddha’s long, broad tongue and the mountain’s form his pure body. Through the night, he said, he heard eighty-four thousand verses, and he wondered how he’d ever explain them the next day.

Dōgen treats the poem as a real expression of realization and makes three points. Awakening doesn’t belong only to monks, since a layman’s poem can show clear seeing. The sound and the form aren’t metaphors: the stream is the teaching, the mountain the body. And most important, don’t take a vivid event for a conclusion. He brings in Xiangyan, who awakened when a pebble hit bamboo, and Lingyun, who awakened on seeing peach blossoms, and says what mattered is that both kept practicing. An isolated experience proves nothing.

The fascicle ends on an unusually personal note about humility. Even a good practitioner should stay cautious and keep going.

In his words, roughly
The sound of the valley stream is the Buddha’s long broad tongue. The mountain’s form is his pure body.
Don’t mistake one vivid moment for the end of the road.

Discernment. Dōgen honors what’s real wherever it shows up, in a layman and in a poem, and still won’t let one experience become a credential.

26向BukkōjōjiWhat Is Beyond the Buddha

The takeawayThere’s no resting place called Buddha. Even realization keeps going.

The title is a Chan phrase meaning roughly “going beyond the Buddha,” and it’s provocative on purpose. It sounds like there’s a level above awakening. Dōgen uses it to talk about continued practice instead.

If you think awakening is a final state where the work stops, you’ve turned buddhahood into a chair and sat down in it. His point is that the truly awakened don’t rest there. They take another step, and another, inside the way. Going beyond the Buddha doesn’t mean climbing to a higher spot. It means refusing to settle into any attainment.

This fits his insistence that practice and realization are one. If realization were a finish line, practice would be over once you crossed it. Instead, realization is a way of continuing. Deciding you’re finished, in any field, is the real danger for him, because that’s when learning stops.

In his words, roughly
Don’t make a seat out of buddhahood and settle into it.
To go beyond the Buddha is to keep walking, not to climb.

Discernment. Authentic practice doesn’t turn its achievements into a throne. Resting on attainment is how practice dies.

27夢Muchū setsumuExpounding a Dream Within a Dream

The takeawayThe teaching is given inside this world, which is itself dream-like. Don’t go looking for solid ground outside it.

The phrase describes buddhas teaching the dharma inside a dream, and inside a dream within a dream. Buddhist sources often compare the world to a dream, usually to say it’s less solid than it looks. Dōgen won’t set up a “waking reality” outside the dream where things are finally firm.

The usual picture goes like this. We’re dreaming, living among unstable appearances. A buddha comes and says all of this is a dream. We nod and think we’ve understood. Dōgen says that’s just one dream explaining another. There’s no outside to explain it from.

The practical upshot: the teaching isn’t a message from beyond. It’s spoken from inside the same confusion and appearance its listeners are in. The buddha is in the dream with us, not hovering over it, and practice happens right where we are. You don’t need a perfectly clear view before you act.

In his words, roughly
Even the awakened teach from inside the dream. There’s no outside to explain it from.
One dream explaining another isn’t yet seeing.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses a vantage point above the world. Real teaching happens in the middle of it.

28髓Raihai tokuzuiGetting the Marrow by Doing Obeisance

The takeawayHonor the dharma wherever it’s found and whoever carries it. Rank, age, and sex don’t matter.

The title points to the story of Bodhidharma, who asked four disciples to show their understanding. After the last, Huike, bowed three times and stood in his place, Bodhidharma said Huike had gotten his marrow. Dōgen takes the bowing seriously. Bowing isn’t servility. It’s how you receive a teaching from someone who has it.

The heart of the fascicle is whom to bow to. If a person has realized the dharma, Dōgen says, bow to them, whatever their appearance, age, or station. He names cases: a young child, a woman, someone the world looks down on. He sharply criticizes keeping women out of sacred places and instruction, and ridicules people who put their own rank above the dharma. That was unusual for its time, though scholars still debate his views on women elsewhere in his writing.

The underlying principle is that authority comes from realization, not title. A famous abbot who hasn’t realized the dharma has no claim on your bow. A seven-year-old girl who has, does. It’s one of Dōgen’s clearest statements on authenticity: look at what a person actually has, not what they’re called.

In his words, roughly
If a person has the dharma, bow to them, woman or man, old or young.
Bowing to a title isn’t bowing to the dharma.

Discernment. Respect what’s real wherever it appears, and don’t defer to what’s merely titled.

29山Sansui kyōThe Mountains and Waters Sutra

The takeawayMountains and waters are the teaching, walking and flowing in plain view. Learn to see their activity.

The fascicle opens with a line that gets quoted out of context a lot: the mountains are constantly walking. Dōgen builds on a verse by Furong Daokai, who said blue mountains are always walking and a stone woman gives birth to a child at night, and he doesn’t try to explain it away.

His claim is that mountains don’t just stand there waiting to be admired. They’re always doing what mountains do, and that includes walking. We miss it because we assume walking needs legs and a destination. If you doubt that mountains walk, Dōgen suggests, you don’t yet understand your own walking.

The fascicle also looks at water. Different beings see water differently: a fish sees a palace, a heavenly being sees jewels, a hungry ghost sees fire, a human sees water. Dōgen isn’t saying reality is just perspective. He’s showing how much of what we call real depends on our situation, and asking us to hold that lightly while still taking the world seriously. He ends by encouraging training in the mountains, which aren’t an escape from the world. They’re the sūtra, written in the landscape.

In his words, roughly
The blue mountains are constantly walking.
To doubt that the mountains walk is not yet to know your own walking.

Discernment. Dōgen trains attention on what’s actually present and won’t let a familiar assumption stand in for looking.

30看KankinReading Sutras

The takeawayRead the teachings with your whole body and without contempt. Don’t worship the words, and don’t dismiss them.

A short fascicle on how to read sūtras. A common Zen attitude of the time said scriptures were just words and real practice was somewhere else. Dōgen pushes back, though not like a scholar would.

He tells a story about Yaoshan, a master who wouldn’t let his monks read sūtras but read them himself. Asked why, he said he was only trying to cover his eyes. Dōgen doesn’t read that as hypocrisy. He reads it as a comment on what kind of reading matters: not reading to collect opinions, but reading to see the world through the teaching, until the page goes transparent.

The real sūtra, he says, isn’t limited to the scroll. The whole universe is a sūtra, and the scroll is part of it. When you read well, you read with your whole body, and you’re being read too. He asks that you leave out neither the written text nor the unwritten world.

In his words, roughly
Reading a sūtra isn’t done only with the eyes. It’s done with the whole body.
The whole world is a sūtra, and the scroll is one part of it.

Discernment. Dōgen rejects both text-worship and text-dismissal. Real reading changes the reader.

31莫Shoaku makusaNot Doing Evil

The takeawayNot doing harm isn’t a rule imposed from outside. It’s how an awakened life naturally moves.

The fascicle takes up an exchange between the poet-official Bai Juyi and the teacher Niaoke, who lived in a tree. Bai Juyi asked what the great meaning of the Buddha’s teaching was. Niaoke said it’s to do no evil and practice all good. Bai Juyi said even a three-year-old could say that. Niaoke replied that a three-year-old can say it, but an eighty-year-old can’t do it.

Dōgen’s reading is the center of the fascicle. The traditional verse usually reads as an instruction: don’t do evil, do good. Dōgen reads the same line as a statement of fact instead of a command, so “not doing evil” becomes a description. When awakening is actually happening, evil just isn’t done, because that’s not what that way of being does. The precept isn’t a fence around the way. It’s what the way looks like when it moves.

He doesn’t let that turn into complacency. The eighty-year-old can’t do it, which means the words are easy and the doing is hard. Someone who claims realization and then causes harm hasn’t realized. This is core Dōgen on ethics and authenticity: no gap between understanding and conduct, observed over time.

In his words, roughly
A three-year-old can say it. An eighty-year-old can’t do it.
Not doing evil isn’t a command from outside. It’s how awakening acts.

Discernment. Conduct is the proof. If understanding allows harm, it wasn’t understanding.

32衣Den eTransmitting the Robe

The takeawayThe robe stands for an actual, verified transmission. It’s not a costume or a trophy.

In Zen history the robe, the kesa, gets passed from teacher to student as a sign the teaching has been handed on. The legend goes back to the first ancestor, Mahākāśyapa, said to have received it from the Buddha, and in the story of the Sixth Ancestor, Huineng, it’s a physical object that other monks chase him to seize.

Dōgen treats the robe as an expression of what it stands for. Transmitting it isn’t a ceremony bolted onto the teaching, and it isn’t a certificate. It’s how the teaching continues from one person to another. The robe is the dharma in a form you can hold.

This fascicle belongs with the writings that insist on authentic lineage. Dōgen traveled to China precisely to find a teacher whose transmission was genuine, and came back with his own transmission from Rujing. The principle is that practice should connect to a real line of people who recognized each other, not to a self-declared status. A real transmission can be traced, is given by someone who has it, and carries obligations.

In his words, roughly
The robe isn’t a costume. It’s how the teaching passes from hand to hand.
A robe with no lineage behind it is only cloth.

Discernment. Verify the line. Dōgen crossed the sea for exactly this reason.

33得DōtokuAble to Speak

The takeawayExpressing the way isn’t repeating sayings. It’s speaking or acting from your own realization.

Dōtoku means “being able to say,” the expression of the way. In Chan literature a teacher often presses a student to say something about their understanding, and the reply shows how deep it goes. Dōgen makes this idea central.

His main claim is that dōtoku isn’t the same as speaking. Words are one form, but so are silence, a gesture, or how you do a task. What matters is that the expression is alive and comes from realization, not memory. A teacher’s phrase isn’t yours to borrow. If you just repeat what an ancestor said, you haven’t expressed anything.

He’s critical of both extremes. Covering a lack of understanding with a flood of clever phrases doesn’t express the way, and neither does hiding behind silence as a pose. Both dodge the risk of actually being seen. Genuine expression can’t be handed off to a template or someone else’s phrases. It has to be tested in the open.

In his words, roughly
Repeating an ancestor’s words isn’t expressing the way.
Silence used as a pose is another way of not speaking.

Discernment. Borrowed eloquence and performed silence are both counterfeits. Real expression is risky and your own.

34教BukkyōThe Buddha’s Teaching

The takeawayDon’t set the sūtras against the ancestors. The Buddha’s teaching and the mind that transmits it are one.

A frequent claim in Zen is that it’s a special transmission outside the scriptures, independent of words. Taken loosely, that can turn into contempt for the Buddha’s teachings and a belief that Zen needs no study. Dōgen takes it on directly.

He argues that the Buddha’s teaching in the sūtras and the mind of the ancestors, passed teacher to student, aren’t two different things. The ancestors who traveled from India to China brought the same dharma that’s in the sūtras. Saying the two point in different directions misunderstands both.

It matters because it keeps Zen continuous with Buddhism as a whole instead of a clever departure from it. A genuine lineage doesn’t have to disown the teachings to feel distinctive. It stands in the same line and should be able to read the old texts with respect and intelligence. That gives you a test for any modern teaching: does the teacher dismiss the traditional sources, or can they read them well?

In his words, roughly
The sūtras and the ancestors’ mind aren’t two different dharmas.
Zen isn’t an escape from the Buddha’s teaching.

Discernment. A teaching that has to disown its sources to look special is a red flag. Dōgen insists on continuity.

35神JinzūSpiritual Powers

The takeawayThe real marvel is ordinary activity done completely. Powers aren’t proof of awakening.

Traditional Buddhism lists extraordinary abilities tied to advanced meditation, like seeing at a distance, reading minds, or remembering past lives. For many people, these powers are what spiritual attainment means. Dōgen’s fascicle answers that assumption.

He quotes the layman Pang, who wrote that the marvelous power and wondrous activity of an awakened person is simply drawing water and carrying firewood. Dōgen treats the verse as the point of the whole teaching. Real power is ordinary action done wholeheartedly, with nothing held back and nothing left over.

He doesn’t deny that unusual abilities have been reported. He treats them as secondary, and as something that can mislead. A person can have uncanny skills and still be confused, and another can be completely clear and have none. If you take powers as evidence of realization, you’re looking in the wrong place. A teacher who points to displays, special states, or a reputation for miracles is offering the wrong goods. The test is whether the marvel shows up in how they live and work every day.

In his words, roughly
Marvelous power and wondrous activity: drawing water and carrying firewood.
A power that isn’t found in ordinary action isn’t the buddhas’ power.

Discernment. Displays and special abilities aren’t proof. Dōgen sends you back to what’s ordinary and verifiable.

36羅ArakanThe Arhat

The takeawayDon’t sneer at other forms of the dharma. Being precise about what a term means is a form of honesty.

In some Mahāyāna circles, the arhat, the realized person of the early teachings, was treated as inferior to the bodhisattva. Zen writers sometimes piled on, using “arhat” for someone who’d reached a narrow, self-regarding peace. Dōgen disagrees and says so here.

His argument is historical and doctrinal. The ancestors of the Zen line include arhats, and the title means a worthy one, someone truly liberated. Using it as an insult misreads both the early tradition and Mahāyāna. He argues an arhat is a buddha, in the sense that the realized state is the same thing under a different name.

It’s a short, fairly dry fascicle, but it shows his method. He won’t accept a convenient caricature of someone else’s teaching, even when it helps his own side. He checks the term against the sources and corrects the habit. Easy contempt for another tradition, or for an earlier stage of your own, usually signals thin knowledge.

In his words, roughly
An arhat is a worthy one, and the ancestors include arhats.
Don’t use a respected title as an insult.

Discernment. Accuracy in naming what you criticize is part of discernment. Caricature is lazy.

37春ShunjūSpring and Autumn

The takeawayMeet each season fully. There’s no place without cold and heat.

The fascicle turns on a well-known exchange about cold and heat, which in Chan stand for the pressures of ordinary life. A monk asks Dongshan how to avoid cold and heat. Dongshan asks why not go where there’s no cold or heat. The monk asks where that is. Dongshan says: when it’s cold, let the cold kill you; when it’s hot, let the heat kill you.

It’s blunt, but it’s not cruel. The way to deal with a hard condition isn’t to escape it but to meet it so completely that you’re no longer standing off to the side asking how to get out. The suffering of resisting gets stacked on top of the cold. The cold alone, fully met, is just cold.

Dōgen connects this to the changing seasons of the title. Spring and autumn aren’t metaphors. They’re the actual rhythm practice happens in, and whoever waits for good weather to start never starts. Conditions are seldom ideal. Dongshan’s answer closes that exit, and it’s a gentle warning about any teaching that promises a place free of difficulty.

In his words, roughly
When it’s cold, let the cold kill you. When it’s hot, let the heat kill you.
There’s no place without cold and heat. Stop looking for one.

Discernment. Be wary of any teaching that promises a place without difficulty. Dōgen points the other way.

38葛KattōTwining Vines

The takeawayThe relationship between teacher and student is a tangle of transmission, and no part of it is lesser.

Kattō means the vines of wisteria or kudzu winding around each other. In Zen the word often meant entanglement in words, something to cut through. Dōgen turns the image around and treats the vines as the very medium of transmission. The tangle of teacher and student, question and answer, word and word is how the dharma passes from person to person.

He builds the fascicle around Bodhidharma’s test of four disciples, said to have gotten his skin, flesh, bones, and marrow, the last being Huike. Many read that as a ranking from shallow to deep. Dōgen rejects the ranking. Each of the four expressed the whole of Bodhidharma’s teaching, and none expressed less. Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow aren’t levels. They’re all the person.

This matters for anyone wary of hierarchy in practice. A real teaching doesn’t need a ladder of attainment to be authentic, and the idea that some students understand more deeply than others is often step one toward a cult of rank. It reads as a quiet warning against any group that uses grades of insight to keep people in their places.

In his words, roughly
Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow aren’t levels. Each one is the whole teaching.
The tangle of teacher and student is the transmission itself.

Discernment. A ladder of attainments is a red flag. Dōgen refuses to rank understanding into higher and lower.

39嗣ShishoSuccession Record

The takeawayA lineage should be documented, checkable, and honest. Forgery and vague claims are betrayals.

The shisho is the document recording a succession of dharma from teacher to student. It lists the line of ancestors, usually back to Śākyamuni, and ends with the recipient’s name, signed and sealed by the teacher. In Dōgen’s day it was the central proof that someone had received transmission.

Dōgen describes the document and why it matters, having seen examples in China. He explains the form with care: how the names are written, what language is used, how the last name is added. He treats the papers as the concrete expression of an unbroken relationship, not paperwork.

He also worries about abuse. A transmission that’s bought, invented, or handed out casually damages the whole line, because the document is only as honest as the people who write it, and he warns about monks claiming lineages they haven’t received. It’s one of his plainest discussions of authenticity, and the practical takeaway is easy: a real succession can stand up to questions. Who gave transmission? What were they like? Are they still around to be asked?

In his words, roughly
The succession record is the concrete trace of an unbroken line of persons.
A transmission that’s invented or bought breaks the whole line.

Discernment. Dōgen treats traceable lineage as a basic test and forgery as a betrayal of everyone in the chain.

40柏HakujushiThe Cypress Tree

The takeawayThe point isn’t hidden behind the tree. The cypress in the courtyard is the answer.

The famous case: a monk asks Zhaozhou why the first ancestor came from the west, meaning what the point of Zen is. Zhaozhou says, “The cypress tree in the courtyard.” The monk objects that the teacher shouldn’t answer by pointing to an object. Zhaozhou says he isn’t pointing to an object. The monk asks again, and Zhaozhou says the same thing.

Many readers take the cypress as a trick that blocks an abstract question by pointing at something concrete. Dōgen takes it more seriously. He quotes the story and comments carefully on where the monk went wrong: the monk assumed the tree was a symbol for something else and wanted to know what lay underneath. Zhaozhou’s denial that he’s pointing at an object means he isn’t using the tree as a sign. The tree is the matter itself.

Dōgen also criticizes people who treat the case as a cunning shortcut, or who claim to see what it means without having lived anything like it. It’s a demonstration to meet, not a riddle to solve. The truth usually isn’t behind the thing you’re looking at. It’s the thing.

In his words, roughly
Why did the first ancestor come from the west? The cypress tree in the courtyard.
Don’t turn the tree into a symbol. It’s the answer.

Discernment. A clever interpretation is often a way of dodging the plain fact. Dōgen keeps pointing back to it.

41三Sangai yuishinThe Three Worlds Are Only Mind

The takeawayMind isn’t a private screen inside your head. It’s the whole world as you encounter it.

A traditional Buddhist phrase says the three worlds, of desire, form, and formlessness, are only mind. Read fast, it sounds like idealism, as if the world were a projection of a personal mind you could change at will. Dōgen explicitly rejects that reading.

For him, mind isn’t a thing inside a skull producing images. Mind is the whole field of what appears: trees, walls, people, everything. Saying the three worlds are only mind means the world isn’t outside the one experiencing it, not that it’s less real. It’s as solid as ever, and the experiencer isn’t a separate observer.

He ties this to the earlier fascicles on the ancient buddha mind and on “the very mind is Buddha.” They all make the same point from different directions: the teaching is about the unity of self and world in practice, not an interior fact. The implication is serious. The quality of attention you bring to your tasks and to other people isn’t a private matter. The world isn’t a dream to control. It’s a field where responsibility is complete.

In his words, roughly
The three worlds are only mind, and mind isn’t inside you. It’s the world.
Idealism isn’t the teaching. The teaching is the unity of self and world.

Discernment. Dōgen guards against a familiar misreading that turns a deep teaching into wishful thinking.

42説Sesshin sesshōExpounding Mind, Expounding Nature

The takeawayTalk about mind and nature has to be tested against actual practice. Without that, it’s only talk.

The title refers to the teaching of mind and nature, a favorite topic in Chan debates. The question was whether the essence of the Buddha’s teaching lay in a mind or nature underlying all appearances. Some teachers claimed to expound mind and nature as the core of their method, and others dismissed the whole subject as an intellectual trap.

Dōgen takes a position between the two. He accepts that the Buddha’s way concerns mind and nature, but insists on what the words mean. Mind is the whole working of the world, in the manner of the ancient buddha mind. Nature is the actuality of things as they are, not an essence behind them.

He criticizes presenting mind and nature as a doctrine people can hold and defend, because that turns an experience into a position. The way to expound mind and nature is to practice in a way that shows them, not to lecture. It’s a handy reminder around any skilled speaker on spiritual subjects: fluent description isn’t evidence. Check whether the talk lines up with how the person lives and works.

In his words, roughly
To expound mind and nature is to practice them, not to lecture about them.
A doctrine of mind isn’t mind.

Discernment. Fluent talk about deep things isn’t evidence. Check it against how the person actually lives.

43相Shohō jissōThe True Reality of All Dharmas

The takeawayReality isn’t hidden behind appearances. Each thing, as it is, is the real form.

The title comes from a line in the Lotus Sūtra: only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas. It’s a famous sentence, and Dōgen uses it as a lens for asking what’s real.

The usual reading sets up a reality behind the shapes of things that only awakened beings can perceive. Dōgen reverses it. The real form of things isn’t a hidden essence. It’s how things appear, as they are, right now. A flower blooming is true reality. A wall standing is true reality. There’s no further layer to go find.

The “buddha together with a buddha” part matters. Realization isn’t a private possession. It happens between people, in a relationship of recognition and testing, and you can’t certify your own insight without someone who can see it. So the way to check whether you’ve seen something isn’t inward certainty but recognition from someone who’s seen it too. And what’s seen isn’t a secret. It’s the plain world.

In his words, roughly
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
The real form isn’t behind the appearance. It’s the appearance, as it is.

Discernment. Realization is mutual. Without someone who can see what you see, certainty is just feeling sure.

44佛ButsudōThe Way of the Buddha

The takeawayThe Buddha’s way stands on its own. Don’t blend it into a vague unity of all teachings, and don’t rebrand it.

A pointed, somewhat polemical fascicle. In Dōgen’s time a popular idea in East Asia held that Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism were three expressions of one truth and the differences were superficial. Some Chan teachers promoted it, and it appealed because it sounded tolerant.

Dōgen rejects it flatly. His argument is that the Buddha’s way has its own content, not interchangeable with the others. Saying all three teach the same thing means you don’t understand any of them well enough to see where they differ. That isn’t breadth. It’s vagueness.

He also criticizes the name “Zen school” and the habit of setting it up as a separate sect that has moved past the Buddha’s teaching. For him the way is simply the Buddha’s way, handed on by the line of ancestors, and a new label that implies a fresh start only cuts it off from its roots. The result is an early statement of authenticity as fidelity: he wants the actual thing, with its edges, not a smooth synthesis of whatever works.

In his words, roughly
To say the three teachings are one is to understand none of them.
The way is the Buddha’s way, not a new school.

Discernment. A comfortable blend isn’t depth. Dōgen wants the real tradition, edges included.

45密MitsugoIntimate Words

The takeawayWhat’s intimate isn’t hidden. It’s plain, direct, and recognized only by someone who’s actually in it.

Mitsugo literally means “secret language,” and in Chan stories a teacher’s remark can be called an intimate word between teacher and student that others can’t follow. Dōgen uses the idea to ask what a secret even means in a tradition that claims to hide nothing.

His answer: the intimacy is real, but it isn’t concealment. A word is secret not because it’s kept from others but because it can only be received by someone standing in the right place. A plain remark can fly over the heads of everyone in the room still hunting for an answer and land instantly with the one who isn’t.

The text uses traditional cases where observers dismiss a teacher’s words but the student clearly receives them. Dōgen doesn’t explain them away or treat them as occult. They’re examples of real encounter, where saying and hearing are one event. In his version nothing is withheld. If something seems hidden, it’s because we haven’t yet attended to what’s plainly shown. That’s a useful corrective for any group that builds a mystique out of hidden knowledge.

In his words, roughly
The secret isn’t kept from you. It’s plainly shown, and recognized only by whoever is there.
An intimate word needs no mystery. It needs a listener.

Discernment. A teaching built on hidden knowledge is suspect. Dōgen’s secret is the plain thing nobody’s looking at.

46情Mujō seppōThe Insentient Preach the Dharma

The takeawayThe whole world is teaching. Listen with your eyes and your whole body, not only your ears.

The fascicle centers on an exchange between Dongshan and his teacher Yunyan. Dongshan asks who can hear the preaching of the insentient: walls, trees, stones, the non-living world. Yunyan says the insentient can hear it. Dongshan asks if Yunyan hears it. Yunyan says that if he could hear it, Dongshan wouldn’t be able to hear Yunyan’s own preaching. Dongshan asks why he can’t hear Yunyan. Yunyan raises his whisk and asks if he hears that. Dongshan says no. Yunyan says Dongshan can’t even hear a human preach, so how could he hear the insentient?

Dongshan later wrote a verse saying, roughly, that the insentient’s preaching is marvelous and inconceivable. If you listen with your ears, you won’t understand. If you hear with your eyes, you will.

Dōgen reads this as saying the teaching isn’t confined to words and speakers. Every part of the world is expressing the dharma, and what’s needed is a way of attending that doesn’t depend on ordinary hearing. He means whole-body attention, where the listener isn’t separate from what’s heard. It’s a fascicle about receptivity. If you’re closed to anything but explicit speech, you miss most of what’s going on.

In his words, roughly
If you listen with the ears, you won’t understand. Hear it with the eyes.
Walls, trees, and stones are all preaching. The task is learning to listen.

Discernment. Real attention is wider than the obvious channel. Dōgen trains you to notice what everything around you is actually saying.

47經ButsukyōThe Buddhas’ Sutras

The takeawayThe sūtras belong to the buddhas and ancestors alike. Don’t use them as props, and don’t dismiss them as dead paper.

A companion to the earlier fascicle on the Buddha’s teaching, this one treats the sūtras as the buddhas’ own and asks how they relate to the ancestors’ words. The problem is the same: a style of Zen that treats the written teachings as cast-off paper, quoted when convenient and ignored when not.

Dōgen holds that both the sūtras and the words of the ancestors are the buddhas’ expression, and neither can be fully understood without the other. If you treat the sūtras as lifeless, you haven’t understood the ancestors. If you think the ancestors had a different message, you haven’t understood the sūtras.

He also says something about how to receive sūtras. They aren’t just information. Reading and hearing them is practice, and you bring the same attention you’d bring to sitting. Done right, the sūtra changes you. As a way of weighing sources, a tradition that knows its own texts and can speak from them is on stronger ground than one resting only on a charismatic person. Dōgen would want you to check both.

In his words, roughly
The sūtras are the buddhas’ own expression, and the ancestors’ words are the same.
Don’t worship the page, and don’t throw it away.

Discernment. Check the sources. A teaching that can’t be read against its own texts is resting on personality alone.

48法HosshōDharma-Nature

The takeawayDharma-nature isn’t a hidden essence. It’s the way things actually are, right now.

Dharma-nature (hosshō) names the underlying nature of things and shows up all over Mahāyāna thought. The risk, which Dōgen sees clearly, is that people picture it as a substance, an ultimate stuff behind the world of ordinary appearances. Talk of it then becomes a way of saying that what you see isn’t the real world.

This fascicle pushes back. Working through sayings of earlier teachers, Dōgen shows dharma-nature isn’t behind things. It’s the actual character of things, appearing as they appear. A mountain’s nature is the mountain. If you go looking under the surface, you’ve missed what’s at the surface.

The text is short and works mostly by suggestion. It’s related to the longer treatment of buddha-nature and carries the same message: there’s no hidden nature you need to find. You’re part of what’s happening. When you catch yourself thinking the real answer is elsewhere, in a better method, a deeper insight, a more advanced teacher, go back to what’s in front of you.

In his words, roughly
A thing’s nature isn’t behind the thing. It’s the thing as it is.
Looking for the real elsewhere is how you miss it here.

Discernment. A teaching that always points beyond the present is suspect. Dōgen points back to what’s here.

49陀DaraniDharani

The takeawayThe holding power of the teaching isn’t a magic formula. It’s what practice does when it’s complete.

A dhāraṇī is a long incantation of Sanskrit syllables, used across many Buddhist traditions for protection and blessing. The word carries the sense of holding or retaining, and these formulas were believed to preserve and transmit a teaching’s power. In popular practice they could easily turn into a kind of spell.

Dōgen handles the subject with restraint. He doesn’t condemn dhāraṇī and doesn’t treat them as magic. His emphasis is on what the word means at root: the capacity to hold the whole teaching in body and mind, so it’s available in whatever situation comes up. In that sense, a person who has practiced completely is a dhāraṇī. Practice is what holds.

That guards against two opposite errors. One is treating ritual formulas as a technology that works no matter who uses them. The other is dismissing ritual as superstition. Dōgen asks for something different: a person for whom the formula expresses a practice already lived. A form with no practice behind it is empty, and a practice that refuses all forms loses its shape.

In his words, roughly
To hold the teaching completely is itself dhāraṇī.
A formula without practice is empty. Practice without a form has no shape.

Discernment. Ritual is neither a spell nor an embarrassment. It carries weight only when the person using it is actually practicing.

50洗SenmenWashing the Face

The takeawayEven washing your face is the buddha way. Small acts done with care aren’t beneath practice.

This is one of Dōgen’s plainest fascicles and one of the most revealing. It’s a manual for morning ablutions in the monastery: how to clean your teeth, rinse your mouth, and wash your face. He cites the early rules of the Buddhist community, the practice of the Buddha himself, and the habits of the Chinese monasteries he’d seen.

The details are specific: how to use the water, how to hold the basin, how to avoid waste, how to be quiet and unhurried. The tone is practical and quietly devoted. Dōgen treats cleansing the body as a sacred act, and in his view washing the face is the same practice as sitting, in a different posture.

The fascicle also says a lot about attitude. Caring for the body and the immediate environment isn’t separate from caring for the dharma, and a monk who’s sloppy about daily routines isn’t in a position to claim depth. The takeaway is that how someone treats small tasks is a better measure of seriousness than their stated ideals.

In his words, roughly
Washing the face isn’t a chore before practice. It’s the practice.
How you handle small tasks shows how you handle everything.

Discernment. Dōgen tests a claim of depth against the smallest daily habits. If it doesn’t show up there, doubt it.

51面MenjuFace-to-Face Transmission

The takeawayThe dharma passes from person to person, in person. Books alone can’t certify it.

Dōgen wrote this after he’d been teaching for some years. It describes what face-to-face transmission means and how he came to receive it. In Zen the central relationship is between a teacher and a student who actually meet, and the dharma is passed in that meeting. Dōgen insists this isn’t a figure of speech.

He recounts his own experience with his teacher Rujing, the sense of something being recognized and confirmed that books couldn’t give him. He also describes the line of ancestors in terms of meetings, each one standing before the next and being known.

The consequence is a statement about authority. If transmission happens through real meeting, then anyone claiming it must have actually met the person they say they received it from, and that person must have known them. That’s a strong check on self-declared teachers and on lineage claims built on a dream, a vision, or a reading. Dōgen also affirms the student’s own commitment: a teacher can’t do the work for you, but the work isn’t complete without the other person. When deciding whom to trust, ask whom they met, and who met them.

In his words, roughly
The dharma is passed face to face, and nothing replaces the meeting.
A claim of transmission needs a real person who actually knew you.

Discernment. Dōgen treats unverifiable claims of private transmission as a basic warning sign.

52祖BussoBuddhas and Ancestors

The takeawayThe line of ancestors isn’t past history. Each one is present in practice now.

This fascicle is mostly a recitation. It names the seven buddhas of the past, from Vipaśyin to Śākyamuni, then the Indian ancestors starting with Mahākāśyapa, then the Chinese ancestors down to Dōgen’s own teacher. The list is long and ritual in feel, because it was chanted and revered in the monastery.

Dōgen’s introduction explains why it matters. A line of teachers isn’t a pedigree to frame. It’s alive, and reciting the names is a way of meeting the people. Honoring the ancestors means entering the relationship they had with each other and with the dharma.

There’s a subtler claim too. The line carries authority precisely because it’s made of individuals who each had to verify the teaching in their own practice. It doesn’t remove the need to practice. It gives practice a context and a standard. A conviction that runs through all his work shows up here: you don’t invent the way yourself. You stand in a line, and you take responsibility for passing it on cleanly.

In his words, roughly
Reciting the names is meeting the people.
The line gives practice a standard, not a shortcut.

Discernment. A teaching with no line behind it has nothing to be checked against. Dōgen insists on the chain.

53梅BaikaPlum Blossoms

The takeawayThe plum opens in the cold. Realization shows up in hard conditions, not despite them.

The plum blossom opens in late winter while snow’s still on the ground, and it’s a favorite image in East Asian poetry and Chan. It stands for beauty that appears in adversity, and for clear awakening that doesn’t wait until conditions are comfortable. Dōgen draws on that tradition and on the sayings of his teacher Rujing, who loved the image.

His treatment is characteristic. The plum blossom isn’t a metaphor for an awakening that’s somewhere else. It’s the awakening in that form, there on that branch. One flower in the cold is a complete expression of the teaching, and whoever sees it sees the whole.

The image also reflects his situation. He wrote this fascicle in 1243, in a hard stretch, with a small community in remote mountains and little material support. Flowering in the cold may well express what he hoped for his community. For anyone who’s been waiting for better conditions to start, the lesson is direct: practice happens in whatever conditions you have, with what’s available.

In his words, roughly
The plum blossoms in the cold, without waiting for spring.
One flower on a bare branch is the whole teaching.

Discernment. Genuine practice doesn’t need ideal conditions. Be skeptical of any teaching that requires them.

54淨SenjōPurification

The takeawayEven the toilet is a place for practice. Nothing in life is outside the way.

A companion to the fascicle on washing the face, this one takes on a subject most religious writing avoids: using the toilet and cleaning yourself afterward. Dōgen explains the procedures in detail, including preparation, washing with water, washing the hands, and the order of each step, drawing on early Buddhist rules and Chinese monastic practice.

It’s easy to smile at this, but Dōgen was serious. The point is direct: no part of life is outside practice. If the body is a place where awakening happens, then the body’s actions, including the least dignified, deserve attention. A tradition that can talk about emptiness but not hygiene has missed something.

There’s also a point about honesty. The most private, least glamorous parts of life are where character shows. Someone who’s careful only in public hasn’t integrated their practice. What you do when nobody’s watching is the real measure.

In his words, roughly
No part of life is outside the way, the toilet included.
What you do unseen is the real test.

Discernment. Integrity shows in unglamorous details. Dōgen won’t let practice live only in the dignified parts of life.

55十JippōThe Ten Directions

The takeawayThe whole world is here, where you stand. Don’t look for the teaching somewhere far off.

The ten directions are the four cardinal points, the four in-between points, up, and down, which together stand for the whole universe. In Buddhist sources they often come up when talking about the buddhas of the ten directions, who are everywhere. The phrase suggests vastness.

Dōgen’s use of it works against distance. For him the ten directions aren’t a far-off horizon. They’re the place where you’re standing, with every direction opening out from it. Saying the dharma fills the ten directions means it’s fully present at this spot, with no need to go looking.

He backs this up with sayings of earlier teachers and with the logic that runs through his work: nothing is lacking, and no thing is a lesser version of something else. One place is the whole world at that spot, the way the moon in a drop is the whole moon. Someone always looking toward a bigger, better, more advanced situation is discounting the present. The ten directions begin here.

In his words, roughly
The ten directions aren’t far away. They open out from where you stand.
Nothing is lacking at this spot.

Discernment. A teaching that always sends you elsewhere deserves questions. Dōgen keeps returning to where you are.

56見KenbutsuSeeing the Buddha

The takeawaySeeing the Buddha isn’t seeing a figure. It’s seeing forms as they are, and practicing.

A line in the Diamond Sūtra says that if you see all forms as non-forms, you see the Tathāgata. It’s often read as an instruction to look through the world at something behind it. Dōgen takes up what it means to see the Buddha and handles it carefully.

His answer moves away from visions and extraordinary experiences. Seeing the Buddha isn’t an apparition appearing. Plenty of people in the tradition have claimed visions, and he doesn’t brush them off, but he says real seeing is the quiet recognition of how things are in the course of ordinary practice. The Buddha is seen in the one who practices, you included.

He also stresses reverence. Seeing a buddha is an occasion for respect, and the right response is to bow and practice, not to describe or collect the experience. By his standard, the test of any special experience is what it does to how you live. If it deepens care and attention, it may be real. If it breeds pride, it’s a distraction, however vivid.

In his words, roughly
To see the Buddha is not to see a figure. It’s to see forms as they are.
A vision that makes you feel special has gone wrong.

Discernment. Dōgen is cautious about special experiences. What matters is whether they deepen care or inflate pride.

57參HenzanExtensive Study

The takeawayTravel and study with total commitment, and look for a real teacher, not a famous one.

Henzan means visiting many teachers and studying widely. In Chinese Chan, a monk would often travel from monastery to monastery, testing teachers and being tested. Dōgen did exactly this in China, and the fascicle reflects what he learned.

His main point is that extensive study isn’t tourism. A monk who wanders around collecting impressions isn’t studying. Real study is complete engagement with whatever’s in front of you, so even a short stay can be thorough and a long journey is a series of full encounters.

He’s also clear about what you’re looking for. A student should seek a true teacher, someone who’s actually realized the dharma and can transmit it. Fame, community size, and impressive buildings are unreliable guides. In his own travels, he met many prominent teachers who didn’t meet his standard, and one, Rujing, who did. Choosing whom to learn from deserves time and attention to what a teacher actually does, without being dazzled by reputation.

In his words, roughly
Extensive study isn’t collecting impressions. It’s full engagement with each encounter.
Look for a true teacher, not a famous one.

Discernment. Dōgen’s own travels are the model: visit widely, test carefully, and don’t mistake reputation for realization.

58眼GanzeiThe Eye

The takeawayThe eye of the buddhas isn’t an organ. It’s a whole practicing life seeing clearly.

The title means the pupil of the eye, and the fascicle is about the Buddha’s eye, the eye of awakened vision. In Zen literature the eye is often a metaphor for the capacity to see what’s real, as in the title of the Shōbōgenzō itself, the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye.

Dōgen avoids the standard picture of a special inner faculty. Working with old sayings, he shows the eye is the whole body in action, and that talking about seeing means talking about everything a practitioner does. An eye with no conduct behind it isn’t the Buddha’s eye, however sharp it looks.

He’s especially interested in how an authentic eye tells truth from imitation. Someone who has it recognizes a real teacher, a real teaching, and a real practice, and notices when something’s off. That’s not suspicion. It’s clarity. Discernment is a trained capacity, built slowly through looking and practice, and it isn’t separate from how you live.

In his words, roughly
The eye isn’t an organ. It’s the whole body seeing clearly.
An eye that can’t tell the real from the imitation isn’t yet the true eye.

Discernment. Discernment is a trained capacity, not a mood. Dōgen ties it to a person’s whole practice.

59家KajōEveryday Matters

The takeawayTea and rice, plain and daily, are the buddha way. Don’t look past the ordinary.

Kajō means the ordinary round of home life: a meal, a cup of tea, the daily tasks that make up a household. In Chan, the teacher Zhaozhou is famous for answering questions with something as plain as “have a cup of tea.” The suggestion is that the way isn’t an exotic place. It’s the everyday.

Dōgen develops this with some force. People who look for the Buddha’s way in remarkable experiences and dramatic events overlook the one place it can be seen without effort: ordinary eating, drinking, working, and resting. Tea and rice aren’t lesser than visions. They’re the full presence of the way.

The text also gently criticizes a tendency to romanticize monastic life. A monastery is a place of daily routine, and what makes it a place of practice is how the routine gets done. The ordinary isn’t a means to the sacred. The ordinary, done completely, is what the sacred looks like. If you’re trying to fold practice into a job and a creative life, this is good news: you don’t need a special container. The tasks you already have are the place.

In his words, roughly
Tea and rice, simple and daily, are the buddha way.
Don’t look past the ordinary for something better.

Discernment. A teaching that disdains daily life and promises something rarer is suspect. Dōgen sends you back to the table.

60品Sanjūshichihon bodai bunpōThe Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening

The takeawayThe early Buddhist path isn’t a lesser way. Dōgen puts the Buddha’s own practices at the center of Zen.

This is the most detailed treatment of the basic Buddhist teachings in the Shōbōgenzō. The thirty-seven factors are an early list of practices that lead to awakening, drawn from the oldest sources. They include the four foundations of mindfulness (body, feelings, mind, and objects of mind), the four right efforts, the four bases of spiritual power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of awakening, and the eightfold path.

Dōgen goes through each group and comments. He treats them not as beginner material to leave behind but as the substance of the buddhas’ teaching and the content of the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye itself. In his view they’re continuous with sitting, and people who sit practice them fully.

The effect is to put Zen squarely inside the Buddha’s own teaching and to reject the idea that it’s a separate, higher path that no longer needs the basics. The foundations aren’t outgrown. If a teacher claims to be beyond mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom, this fascicle says nobody is. Real depth includes these things and doesn’t leave them.

In his words, roughly
These thirty-seven aren’t a lesser path. They’re the treasury of the true dharma eye.
No one outgrows mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom.

Discernment. A teaching that leaves the basics behind has left the Buddha behind. Dōgen grounds Zen in the earliest practices.

61龍RyūginSong of the Dragon

The takeawayWhat looks dead can sing. When the grasping stops, vitality shows up.

The title comes from a saying attributed to the teacher Xiangyan, who, asked about the way, answered that a dragon sings in a withered tree. The phrase pairs lifeless wood with a mythic creature whose voice is thunder.

Dōgen reads the withered tree as the condition of a person who has dropped attachments, a body and mind no longer pushing in any direction. From outside it looks like nothing, dry and plain. But from that condition a voice arises that’s vast and alive. The song isn’t made by effort. It’s what the tree does when it’s simply there.

He pairs the image with other sayings that use dead wood and cold ashes to describe the silent, unmoving mind, but he’s careful to separate this from deadness. A meditation where nothing happens and nothing is felt isn’t what he means. The tree is dry, but the dragon is singing. If your practice has gone flat, the barrenness may be a condition for something. Don’t force it to look lively. Let it be what it is and listen.

In his words, roughly
A dragon sings in a withered tree.
What looks like nothing isn’t nothing. The song is there.

Discernment. Dōgen separates real stillness from dull numbness, which are easy to confuse. Real stillness has life in it.

62西Soshi seirai iThe Intention of the Ancestor’s Coming from the West

The takeawayThe point of Zen isn’t a hidden doctrine. It’s the plain actuality shown in each answer.

Why did Bodhidharma come from India to China? It’s the most famous question in Chan, a way of asking what the whole teaching is for. Teachers gave many different answers, and the answers became cases for study.

Dōgen collects several and examines them. His repeated claim is that the ancestor’s intention isn’t a separate message these answers point toward. Each answer, from the cypress tree in the courtyard to something even more ordinary, is itself the intention. The ancestor came to show what’s already present in front of everyone.

He also pushes back on treating these answers as clever tricks, as if the goal were surprising statements. They aren’t performances. They’re what the person saw at that moment, and they work because the questioner was meant to look at the thing, not puzzle over the reply. It’s quietly demanding on discernment. You’re asked to notice when a teacher is giving something real and when they’re putting on a show of being enigmatic. The real thing isn’t obscure. It’s plain, often unexpected, and doesn’t need much staging.

In his words, roughly
The ancestor came to show what’s already in front of everyone.
A cryptic answer isn’t deeper than a plain one.

Discernment. Mystification is a common counterfeit of depth. Dōgen’s answers are plain.

63發Hotsu mujō shinRaising the Supreme Mind

The takeawayThe vow to benefit others before yourself is the supreme mind. It begins with a decision, and anyone can make it.

The supreme mind is the aspiration for complete awakening for the sake of all beings, known in Sanskrit as bodhicitta. In Mahāyāna it’s the beginning and root of the whole path, the point where a person takes up the way for something bigger than their own comfort or advantage.

Dōgen discusses what it means to raise this mind and why it matters. He stresses that it isn’t reserved for an elite. It can happen to an ordinary person in unlikely circumstances, and it doesn’t depend on prior achievement. What matters is that it’s a real turning, a decision that changes what you’re working for.

He also separates the aspiration from chasing reputation. Plenty of people take up practice for status, admiration, or an interesting identity, which he calls common and corrosive. The vow to help others corrects it by pointing away from the self. The purpose behind the work matters. Work done for praise bends toward whatever gets praised. Work done for something beyond yourself tends to steady.

In his words, roughly
To raise the supreme mind is to vow to help others cross before oneself.
Anyone can make this turn, whatever their position.

Discernment. Check the motive. Practice taken up for reputation is already off course, and Dōgen says so plainly.

64優Udon geThe Udumbara Flower

The takeawayThe flower isn’t a symbol of the transmission. It is the transmission, as it actually happened.

The udumbara is a legendary flower said to bloom once in a very long time. In the story behind Zen’s claim of a special transmission, the Buddha held up a flower before a gathering. Nobody responded except Mahākāśyapa, who smiled. The Buddha then said he had the treasury of the true dharma eye and entrusted it to him.

Dōgen takes up the story and rejects the standard interpretation, where the flower is a gesture standing for a silent teaching beyond words. For him the holding up of the flower and the smile are the teaching, fully expressed and received. They’re not a code to be decoded. And the story isn’t about magical communication between two special people. The transmission is carried in ordinary activity, and the flower is a flower.

The fascicle belongs with those that guard against turning Zen into a tale of mystical elitism. The tradition isn’t a secret club with special signals. It’s a line of people who’ve shown, in plain actions, what the teaching is. If you’re sifting through claims of hidden knowledge, it’s a useful corrective.

In his words, roughly
The flower held up isn’t a symbol. It’s the teaching, fully expressed.
The transmission begins with an ordinary act, plainly done.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses a mystical elitism around the tradition’s own founding story.

65如Nyorai zenshinThe Whole Body of the Tathagata

The takeawayThe Buddha’s real body is the teaching, which includes the scrolls and the person who practices it.

A phrase in the Lotus Sūtra speaks of the Tathāgata’s relics, and in some places the sūtras themselves are described as the whole body of the Tathāgata. Dōgen takes up the claim and works out what it means.

In an earlier tradition, the Buddha’s remains were enshrined in stūpas and reverence went to the physical relics. Mahāyāna moved the center of reverence to the teaching: the Buddha’s body is the dharma, and the sūtra scrolls are its form. Dōgen goes a step further. The whole body of the Buddha isn’t limited to written texts. It’s present wherever the teaching is practiced.

That changes how you treat both texts and practitioners. A scroll deserves honor, and so does a person living the teaching, since that person is also the Buddha’s body. The fascicle becomes a reflection on what’s worth revering: not a relic as an object of superstition, not a text as a prop, but the living teaching as it shows up in action. It’s a handy way to sort what’s substance from what’s decoration in any tradition.

In his words, roughly
The sūtra scrolls are the whole body of the Tathāgata.
The living practice is also the Buddha’s body.

Discernment. Reverence belongs to the living teaching, not to objects or reputations.

66王Zanmai ō zanmaiThe King of Samadhis Samadhi

The takeawaySitting upright is the king of concentrations. Nothing replaces it, and nothing is above it.

In this fascicle Dōgen makes his most emphatic claim about zazen. Sitting cross-legged, he says, is the king of samādhis, the practice that contains all others, and the whole body in that posture is the form of the buddhas and ancestors.

He goes through the traditional language for the posture, including the old description of the cross-legged form as the skin, flesh, bones, and marrow of the Buddha. In his reading, to sit is to express the whole teaching, with nothing further for sitting to aim at.

He’s pointed about people who treat the posture as optional. He quotes teachers who emphasized sitting and criticizes those who say real Zen can skip it or swap in something more exciting. For him, losing the sitting posture means losing the buddha way. The text reflects his conviction that the practice he brought from China was complete and needed no supplement. Sitting is the one practice that doesn’t become obsolete.

In his words, roughly
Sitting cross-legged is the king of samādhis.
To lose the sitting posture is to lose the buddha way.

Discernment. Dōgen is blunt about practices that claim to replace the core. The simple thing stays the center.

67轉Ten hōrinTurning the Dharma Wheel

The takeawayThe wheel is turning now, wherever the teaching is practiced. You’re both turning it and being turned by it.

Turning the dharma wheel is the traditional description of the Buddha’s first teaching, given to five ascetics at Deer Park. The wheel is the teaching, and its turning is the start of an ongoing activity.

Dōgen reads the image as describing something that isn’t finished. The wheel isn’t a historical act. It’s turning whenever the dharma is practiced, taught, or heard. Whoever sits down to practice is turning the wheel, and the wheel is turning that person too.

The emphasis on mutuality is typical. A teaching isn’t an object you pick up and use. It acts on you as you act with it, and the line between user and used gets blurry. It’s the same relationship as the boat that carries the rider who steers it. The fascicle is brief, and it nudges you to approach the teaching as a participant, not an owner. Something’s already moving, and you join it.

In his words, roughly
The wheel is turning wherever the dharma is practiced.
You turn the wheel, and the wheel turns you.

Discernment. A teaching you own outright isn’t the teaching. Dōgen keeps you inside something larger.

68修Dai shugyōGreat Practice

The takeawayEven the greatly awakened don’t escape cause and effect. Claiming otherwise is a serious mistake.

This fascicle retells the story of Baizhang and the fox. After a lecture, an old man who regularly came to listen stayed behind. He explained that long ago he’d been a teacher on that mountain. A student had asked whether a greatly practiced person falls into cause and effect, and he’d answered that such a person doesn’t. For that answer he’d been reborn as a fox for five hundred lifetimes. He asked Baizhang to answer the question properly. Baizhang said such a person doesn’t obscure cause and effect, and the old man was released.

Dōgen examines the two phrases, “does not fall into” (is exempt from) and “does not obscure” (doesn’t hide from or ignore), with great care. The first suggests realization puts you outside the law of consequence. The second says realization sees consequence clearly and doesn’t hide from it. He holds that the difference between them is the difference between delusion and truth.

What comes out is a firm statement: practice doesn’t lift anyone above the consequences of their actions. Cause and effect is the structure of the world, and an awakened person lives in it with eyes open. It’s among his clearest teachings on discernment. Anyone who claims their realization exempts them from accountability is standing where the old man stood before his release.

In his words, roughly
Does a greatly practiced person fall into cause and effect? Such a person does not obscure cause and effect.
Claiming to stand outside consequence is the fox’s mistake.

Discernment. The most direct test of a claim to realization: does it accept consequence and accountability?

69證Jishō zanmaiThe Samadhi of Self-Verification

The takeawayVerification isn’t private certainty. It’s confirmed by the dharma and by a real teacher.

Self-verification, in Dōgen’s usage, doesn’t mean what it sounds like. It isn’t the experience of being inwardly convinced of your own awakening, and the self involved isn’t a private subject sitting in judgment on itself. It’s the self verified by all things, as in Genjōkōan, where letting the myriad things come forward confirms the self.

He argues that real verification happens in practice and is then recognized by a teacher who’s had the same experience. Someone who says they’ve verified themselves with no reference to a lineage or any outside check has misunderstood the term.

The fascicle includes a warning about a kind of self-assured individualism already around in some circles, where the language of direct realization got treated as a license to bypass teachers and traditions. Dōgen sees the risk and ties verification firmly to relationship. The distinction between feeling certain and being verified is practical. Certainty is easy to have and a poor indicator. A skilled teacher, a careful reading of the tradition, and an honest look at your own conduct can test it, and this fascicle encourages you to submit to that test.

In his words, roughly
Self-verification isn’t self-certification.
Feeling certain isn’t the same as being verified.

Discernment. Private certainty is cheap. Dōgen insists on confirmation by the dharma and by someone who’s actually seen it.

70空KokūEmpty Space

The takeawaySpace isn’t an absence. It’s the room where everything happens, and it’s itself a way the teaching shows up.

Kokū means empty space or sky, and in Buddhist writing it often names the open expanse that holds all things. It can suggest the void, and in some teachings it stands for negation, an absence of everything.

Dōgen doesn’t accept the negative reading. For him space is as real as a mountain and part of the teaching. The sky isn’t a nothing between things. It’s the openness that lets things be what they are. He draws on Chan sayings where space is spoken of as a body with its own function.

He also links space with practice. Someone who sits well is spacious, meaning not stuffed with agendas and opinions, and that openness isn’t vacancy. It’s a capacity to receive what comes without distortion. The same logic applies to a layout, a schedule, or a conversation: leaving room isn’t waste, it’s what lets the other parts work. A cramped mind, like a crowded page, can’t see.

In his words, roughly
Space isn’t a nothing between things. It’s the room in which things are.
An open mind isn’t vacant. It’s available.

Discernment. Emptiness isn’t nihilism. Dōgen makes space a positive condition for seeing clearly.

71鉢HouThe Almsbowl

The takeawayThe bowl is the Buddha’s body. Use the genuine form with care, not a convenient substitute.

The almsbowl is the container a monk uses for food, and from the beginning of Buddhism it’s been one of a monk’s few possessions. Dōgen’s fascicle covers its history, meaning, and use. He traces the bowl back to the Buddha and to the transmission from Mahākāśyapa.

There are practical details on the proper shape, material, and size of the bowl and on how the nested set is arranged. Dōgen had seen how the bowl was used in Chinese monasteries and knew that in many places the proper form was neglected or mixed with other styles. His response is firm: he insists on the traditional bowl, used the way the buddhas and ancestors used it.

His reasoning is that form carries teaching. The bowl isn’t just a utensil. It’s a link to a line of people who used the same vessel the same way, and swapping in something more convenient or fashionable cuts that link. A modern reader may wonder how much of this is essential. Still, the underlying point is useful for discernment. A tradition that’s casual about its own forms, or reshapes them to suit marketing, owes an explanation of what it’s keeping and what it’s giving up.

In his words, roughly
The bowl is the body of the buddhas and ancestors.
A convenient substitute breaks the link with the line.

Discernment. Dōgen holds the tradition’s forms to exact standards. Casual substitution is how a lineage quietly drifts.

72安AngoThe Practice Period

The takeawayCommit to a fixed container of practice and stay inside it. The container does part of the work.

Ango, the practice period, is a retreat of about three months when monks stay in the monastery and focus on training. It goes back to the rainy season in India, when monks stopped wandering and stayed put. In China and Japan it became a fixed part of the year.

Dōgen gives the history and the rules: the dates, the purpose, the obligations. A monk who enters the practice period commits to staying and following the schedule, and leaving early is a serious matter.

There’s a deeper idea here: the container itself is part of the practice. A fixed period, place, and schedule remove the daily question of whether to practice today. The choice has already been made, and practice is what’s left. Dōgen treats this as a gift, not a restriction. It works outside a monastery too. Plenty of people trying to build a practice or a body of work fail because they renegotiate the decision every morning. A fixed period with clear edges changes the game.

In his words, roughly
Within a fixed period, the question of whether to practice today is already answered.
The container does part of the work.

Discernment. A tradition with real forms of commitment is easier to trust than one that offers only inspiration.

73他Tashin tsūPenetration of Other Minds

The takeawayMind-reading powers prove nothing. A genuine teacher can’t be caught out by a trick.

The fascicle is built around a well-known story about National Teacher Huizhong and an Indian monk, Daer Sanzang, said to have the power to read minds. The emperor invited the visitor to the capital, and Huizhong came to test him.

Each time, Huizhong asked the visitor to say where Huizhong was. The first time, the visitor said he was at the West River boat. The second time, the visitor said he was on the bridge watching a monkey show. The third time, the visitor couldn’t find him. Huizhong then said this fox spirit, who claimed to know others’ minds, didn’t know where he was.

Dōgen uses the case to show what the story is really testing. A power to read minds can only locate a mind that’s fixed on something. It can’t find a mind that’s attached to nothing. The third time, Huizhong couldn’t be found because he had no position to be located. A powerful trick isn’t the same as understanding. It pairs well with the fascicle on spiritual powers: impressive displays are never the test, and someone who can do a trick may be less clear than someone who can do none.

In his words, roughly
The third time, the mind-reader couldn’t find the teacher. There was no place to find.
A power to read minds isn’t wisdom.

Discernment. Dōgen shows that a spectacular ability can be completely out-seen by ordinary clarity.

74索Ō saku sendabaThe King Asks for Saindhava

The takeawayDiscernment is responding rightly to what’s actually meant in this moment, not following a rule.

The Sanskrit word saindhava has several meanings: salt, water, a bowl, a horse. The story is about a king whose attendant was sharp enough to know which one the king meant at any given moment. When the king was washing his hands and said saindhava, the attendant brought water. At a meal, salt. After the meal, a cup. When the king was about to go out, his horse.

Dōgen picks up the story as a model of right response. The attendant didn’t have a rule. He knew the situation, read the person, and gave exactly what was needed. For Dōgen, that’s a picture of what a practitioner is supposed to do: see the actual circumstances and act accordingly.

The fascicle makes a few points about teaching and learning. A teacher’s words have to be received in context, and an attendant who brought the wrong item would be a fool no matter how much he knew. A student, likewise, has to learn to read the situation and not hang on to a single meaning. Plenty of fields have rules. The skill that makes the difference is knowing what the moment calls for.

In his words, roughly
The king said saindhava. The wise attendant brought water, salt, a cup, or the horse, as the moment required.
Knowing what’s needed isn’t a matter of knowing a rule.

Discernment. Discernment is the skill of reading the actual situation. No formula substitutes for it.

75出ShukkeLeaving Home

The takeawayLeaving home is where practice begins, and it means leaving the habits of the self as well as a house.

In the Buddhist tradition, leaving home means taking up the life of a monk or nun. The Buddha himself left his family and palace to seek awakening, and the act has been the model for those who follow.

Dōgen’s fascicle is partly a defense of monastic life and partly an examination of what it means. He argues that leaving home is the fundamental act of the buddhas and ancestors, the thing they all did, and stresses that it isn’t an escape from responsibility but the taking on of a larger one.

He also makes clear that the outward act isn’t enough. A person can leave home in the outer sense and stay tied to every habit and ambition that home represented. The real departure is inner: letting go of the picture of yourself as someone who has to protect and enlarge a position. Without that, the robe and the shaved head are costume. Even if you’ll never be a monastic, the question lands: has turning toward practice actually changed what you’re living for?

In his words, roughly
Leaving home is the act of all the buddhas and ancestors.
A robe worn over unchanged ambitions is only a costume.

Discernment. The outward form isn’t the proof. Dōgen asks whether the inward turn has really happened.

Part two: the late twelve

The collection he was still writing near the end of his life, ending with his last fascicle.

01功Shukke kudokuThe Merit of Leaving Home

The takeawayCommitment makes practice real. A serious turn costs something and takes a formal shape.

Among the last texts Dōgen wrote, this fascicle praises ordination, the formal step from lay life onto the monastic path. He gathers statements from the sūtras and the ancestors on the great benefit of leaving home and holding the precepts, and says plainly that the merit outweighs any ordinary good.

You can read it as a simple recommendation of monasticism, but the real subject is seriousness. Dōgen had watched plenty of people treat practice as an interest to fit around an otherwise unchanged life. In his late writing he recalls what he thought the way required: a decision, a vow, and a form that makes the decision public and hard to take back.

He also wants to dispel the idea that the outward act is empty ritual. In his view, form and intention hold each other up. The ceremony expresses a commitment already begun in the heart, and the commitment gets stronger by being made concrete. For non-monastics, the principle still works. Anything you take up seriously benefits from a visible point of commitment, because vague intention rarely lasts.

In his words, roughly
The merit of leaving home can’t be matched by ordinary good deeds.
A vow made in the open is harder to abandon than a private wish.

Discernment. Dōgen distrusts casual interest. In his late work, the test of seriousness is a commitment that isn’t easy to undo.

02戒JukaiReceiving the Precepts

The takeawayThe precepts are how the awakened live. You take them up by decision and ceremony, and renew them daily.

Another of Dōgen’s late texts, this fascicle describes the formal reception of the sixteen bodhisattva precepts: the three refuges in Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha; the three pure precepts, which are to avoid evil, to do good, and to benefit all beings; and the ten grave precepts. The grave precepts forbid killing, stealing, misusing sexuality, lying, dealing in intoxicants, speaking of others’ faults, praising yourself while criticizing others, stinginess, anger, and disparaging the three treasures.

Dōgen presents receiving the precepts as entering the Buddha’s way. They aren’t a rulebook delivered from outside. They’re the content of awakened conduct, handed on from the buddhas and made your own through the ceremony. He also stresses repentance as part of the process, an honest acknowledgment of wrongs done.

He’s careful about how the ceremony happens. It needs a proper teacher who’s received the precepts in an unbroken line, and a sincere student. Casual or commercial conferral doesn’t count. The fascicle pulls together much of what he says about authenticity: ethics isn’t decoration. It’s the shape of practice, and it has to be handed on by someone who can be held to the same standard.

In his words, roughly
To receive the precepts is to enter the ranks of the buddhas.
Precepts handed out casually aren’t precepts.

Discernment. The ethical core of the tradition isn’t optional. Dōgen ties it to a traceable transmission and an accountable teacher.

03袈Kesa kudokuThe Merit of the Robe

The takeawayThe robe isn’t decoration. It’s made and worn correctly, and its form is part of the teaching.

Dōgen’s long essay on the kāṣāya, the Buddhist robe, is one of the more extended treatments in the Shōbōgenzō. He explains where it comes from, what it means, how it should be made, and why it deserves reverence. The robe is a patchwork garment made from discarded cloth, a deliberate rejection of luxury, and the mark of someone who has left home.

He’s insistent on correct form. The stitching pattern, the choice of cloth, and the manner of wearing are all part of what makes the robe what it is, and he criticizes mixing styles and swapping in fancier or more convenient materials as a loss of the tradition’s meaning. There’s a strong note of devotion running through the text. Dōgen cites many accounts of the power of the robe and the merit of making, wearing, and honoring it, some of them miraculous, which a modern reader can take as expressions of reverence rather than literal claims.

The theme that matters for discernment is fidelity. A tradition’s forms aren’t arbitrary, and dropping them for taste or marketing has consequences. Dōgen’s severity can seem excessive, but it comes from a consistent conviction that the way is carried in the details, not only in ideas.

In his words, roughly
The robe is the buddhas’ own garment. Make it and wear it as they did.
Changing the form for convenience loses what the form carries.

Discernment. Dōgen is strict about form because he treats fidelity as a test of seriousness.

04菩Hotsu bodai shinArousing the Aspiration for Awakening

The takeawayThe path begins with the vow to awaken for others’ sake. Without it, practice drifts into self-interest.

The aspiration for awakening, bodhicitta, is the starting point of the bodhisattva path. Dōgen takes it up in a late fascicle that seems aimed at people inside the monastery as much as at a wider audience.

His central claim is that the aspiration is more than a feeling of inspiration. It’s a commitment to awaken in order to help all beings, and it requires willingness to put others first. He warns against taking up practice for personal gain, reputation, comfort, or even the satisfaction of a spiritual identity, which he treats as common and corrosive motives.

He’s encouraging too. Anyone can raise the aspiration, in any condition, even when sick, ashamed, or distracted, and it doesn’t require a clean record or a stable situation. It’s renewed whenever you remember it, and you can raise it again after forgetting. The text is sober and warm at once: sober about purpose, warm about who’s allowed to begin. The question it poses, who the effort is for, shapes everything that follows.

In his words, roughly
Awaken for the sake of others, and don’t look for fame or profit in it.
The aspiration can be raised again at any time.

Discernment. Practice undertaken for reputation is already off course. The check is the purpose behind the effort.

05供Kuyō shobutsuMaking Offerings to the Buddhas

The takeawayOffering is a practice of generosity. A sincere small gift is worth more than an extravagant empty one.

Making offerings means presenting incense, flowers, light, food, and other gifts to the buddhas, and by extension to teachers and the community. Dōgen gives the practice serious attention in a late fascicle that gathers stories and sayings about its merit.

He’s clear that an offering’s value isn’t in what it costs. A handful of grass, a bowl of water, or a single flower offered in good faith is complete, while an elaborate offering made out of vanity or a wish to be seen is empty. The act trains the mind away from stinginess and toward giving.

He also ties the practice to reverence for the Three Treasures and for the living line of teachers. Making an offering means recognizing that you’ve received something, which counters the habit of treating teaching and tradition as a service you pay for and then own. The principle travels well: give without a hidden bill, and acknowledge what you’ve been given. Dōgen treats those as part of the way, not extras.

In his words, roughly
A single flower offered sincerely is complete.
An offering made to be seen is only a display.

Discernment. Sincerity is the test. Dōgen measures an act by the intention behind it, not its size or visibility.

06歸Kie buppōsōbōTaking Refuge in the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha

The takeawayRefuge is the doorway. Without it, nothing else in the way stands on solid ground.

Taking refuge in the Three Treasures, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, is the formal act that marks someone as a Buddhist. It’s the first of the precepts and the first thing a student does in the ordination ceremony.

Dōgen’s late fascicle treats it with great seriousness. He cites teachers and sūtras to show taking refuge is no small matter. It’s the point where a person turns from seeking shelter in worldly securities, like reputation, wealth, and power, to relying on the way the buddhas walked. He also stresses that people who take refuge sincerely receive the support of the Three Treasures.

One important point is that he treats the three as objects of real respect. The Buddha isn’t an abstract ideal, the Dharma isn’t a set of ideas, and the Saṅgha isn’t a social club. Each is a living reality, and the community, flaws and all, is part of the refuge. The fascicle makes a plain observation: everyone relies on something, and the question is whether what you rely on will hold. For the Three Treasures, Dōgen’s answer fits all his writing: the way that a line of people has tested over time.

In his words, roughly
To take refuge is to turn from shaky securities to the way the buddhas walked.
The community, flaws included, is part of the refuge.

Discernment. Everyone relies on something. Dōgen asks what has actually held up over time.

07因Jinshin ingaDeep Faith in Cause and Effect

The takeawayDon’t deny cause and effect. People who do are outside the buddha way, however advanced they look.

Cause and effect, in Buddhist terms, means actions have consequences and that’s part of how the world is built. In some Chan circles of Dōgen’s day, the language of emptiness and non-attachment was used to suggest the awakened were beyond such things and talk of consequence was for beginners.

Dōgen rejects this view strongly. The fascicle opens with a well-known story about the Chinese teacher Baizhang and a fox (it also appears in Dai shugyō). An old man tells Baizhang that he was once a teacher on this mountain. A student had asked him whether a person of great practice is still subject to cause and effect. He answered, “Such a person does not fall into cause and effect,” meaning a realized person is exempt from consequences. For that answer he was reborn as a fox, and lived as a fox for five hundred lifetimes.

He asks Baizhang to answer the question for him. Baizhang says, “Such a person does not obscure cause and effect,” meaning a realized person doesn’t hide from consequences or pretend they don’t apply. Hearing this, the old man was freed from the fox’s body. The two answers sound alike but say opposite things. The first says realization lifts you out of cause and effect. The second says a realized person still lives inside it and sees it clearly. Dōgen treats the first answer as the mistake.

He then lines up a long set of arguments and quotations to show that denying cause and effect is heresy. The tone is severe, and he says plainly that people who hold this view are outside the way. It’s among his most direct statements on the link between realization and ethics: a teaching that leads people to think their actions don’t matter, or that realization excuses harm, is false.

In his words, roughly
Don’t deny cause and effect.
A teaching that excuses harm in the name of emptiness isn’t the buddha way.

Discernment. The clearest lesson in the late work: no realization puts anyone above consequence.

08業Sanji gōKarma of the Three Times

The takeawayActions bear fruit in this life, the next, or later. Don’t assume anything escapes.

In the traditional account, karma ripens at different times. Some actions produce results in this very life, some in the next, and some in lives further off. Dōgen’s short fascicle explains this and presents it as an essential teaching.

The reason he stresses it has to do with apparent injustice. A good person suffers while a bad person thrives, and an observer concludes actions don’t matter. Dōgen’s answer is that results aren’t always visible on a short timescale. The fruit is certain, though the timing isn’t.

He also uses it as a caution against complacency. Someone who’s done something harmful and isn’t suffering for it yet shouldn’t conclude nothing will follow, and someone who’s done good and received no reward shouldn’t give up. It isn’t meant as mechanical or punitive. It’s a way of taking responsibility seriously over the long run. For work that matters, the sensible approach is to do it properly whatever the immediate reaction.

In his words, roughly
Some karma ripens now, some next, some later.
Not yet seeing the result isn’t the same as there being no result.

Discernment. Dōgen asks for a long view of conduct. Short-term outcomes are a poor guide to what’s right.

09馬ShibaFour Horses

The takeawayRespond at the shadow of the whip. Don’t wait for the pain before you begin to practice.

A passage in the early sūtras describes four kinds of horses. The best starts moving at the mere shadow of the whip. The second moves when the whip touches its hair, the third when it touches the skin, and the fourth only when the pain reaches the bone. The image was used for different kinds of students, from those who respond to the faintest hint to those who need a serious crisis.

Dōgen applies the image to practice. The teaching of impermanence is the whip, and the question is how soon you respond. If you see that life is passing and death can come at any time, you can start practicing right away. If you wait for illness, loss, or disaster, you’re the fourth horse.

His emphasis is both encouraging and urgent. All four horses can run, and nobody’s excluded, but the sooner you start the better. Delay is dangerous because it assumes there’s always another day. Responding to the shadow of the whip isn’t anxiety. It’s being awake to how short the time is.

In his words, roughly
The best horse moves at the shadow of the whip.
Don’t wait for the pain to reach the bone.

Discernment. A teaching that advises starting later is suspect. Dōgen’s urgency is plain and kind.

10禪Shizen bikuThe Monk of the Fourth Dhyana

The takeawayA deep meditative state isn’t realization. Mistaking one for the other is a catastrophic error.

The fascicle retells a story from Buddhist sources about a monk who reached the fourth dhyāna, a state of deep meditative absorption where breathing seems to stop and the mind goes extremely still. The monk took it as proof he’d reached the final fruit of the path and told others so.

At death, according to the story, he saw signs of rebirth that didn’t match his expectations. Confused and angry, he concluded the Buddha’s teaching was false, and that mistake led to a descent into a terrible realm. His error was double. He’d mistaken a high state of concentration for liberation, and then denied the truth when reality didn’t line up.

Dōgen uses the story as a warning for practitioners and teachers. Intense experiences, however profound, don’t validate themselves. Skillful practice can produce them and they can still be something other than what they seem. The only protection is accurate knowledge of the tradition and willingness to be corrected. If a claim of attainment rests mostly on an experience, especially one the claimant can’t test against the teachings or a reliable teacher, treat it with caution.

In his words, roughly
A state of deep stillness isn’t the same as liberation.
Claiming an attainment you don’t have endangers you and others.

Discernment. The work’s most direct warning against mistaking an experience for realization, and against teachers who claim attainments they haven’t tested.

11百Ippyakuhachi hōmyō monThe One Hundred and Eight Gates of Illumination

The takeawayThere are many entrances to the one way, and each gate is the whole of it.

This text is distinctive in the Shōbōgenzō and was lost for centuries. It survives in a single manuscript, discovered only in the twentieth century, which is why older editions sometimes leave it out. It’s built as a litany, working through one hundred and eight gates of illumination.

The number has a long history in Buddhism, tied to the one hundred and eight defilements, the varied forms of delusion. Here it’s turned around, and each gate is a way of entering clarity. Dōgen treats the list as a recitation in which each item is a gate that opens onto the whole.

Because the text is so rarely studied, this summary stays brief. It expresses a belief that runs through his work: the way doesn’t have one narrow entrance. It can be entered through many doors, each complete, and standing at one gate isn’t a lesser position than standing at another. The attitude is generous about method and strict about practice. It doesn’t endorse every approach, but it recognizes the same thing can be approached from many sides.

In his words, roughly
There are many gates, and each is the whole way.
No gate is a lesser place.

Discernment. Dōgen is open about entry points and strict about what has to be done once you’re inside.

12八Hachi dainin gakuThe Eight Awakenings of Great Beings

The takeawayWant little, be content, seek quiet, be diligent, keep mindfulness, practice concentration, cultivate wisdom, and avoid idle talk.

This is the last fascicle Dōgen wrote, in 1253, the year he died. It’s based on a teaching traditionally attributed to the Buddha’s final instructions to his disciples, which Dōgen presents as the essence of the way. It describes eight qualities that awakened beings practice and anyone hoping to awaken can cultivate.

The first is having few desires. The second is contentment with what you have. The third is enjoying quiet, preferring solitude and stillness to agitation. The fourth is diligence, steady, unhurried effort that keeps going. The fifth is not forgetting mindfulness, staying with what’s present. The sixth is practicing concentration, the settled mind of samādhi. The seventh is practicing wisdom, the clear seeing of how things are. The eighth is avoiding idle talk, refusing to scatter your attention in frivolous chatter and argument.

Dōgen’s closing note has the force of a farewell. He stresses these aren’t preliminary teachings. They’re the whole path. If you practice these eight, you’ve understood the Buddha’s teaching and nothing’s left out. The text works well as a daily checklist: want less, be content, protect quiet, persevere, pay attention, concentrate, see accurately, and speak less.

In his words, roughly
Few desires, contentment, quiet, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, and no idle talk.
People who want many things have much trouble. People who want little are at ease.

Discernment. Dōgen’s last word is practical and checkable. You can tell within a day whether you’re doing it.

Part three: printed alongside

Texts traditional editions include with the Shōbōgenzō.

01辨BendōwaA Talk on Endeavoring in the Way

The takeawaySit, and let the sitting itself be practice and realization together. This one gate is complete.

Dōgen wrote Bendōwa in 1231, shortly after coming back from China. It’s his earliest major statement and is often printed with the Shōbōgenzō. It’s set up as a series of questions and answers, with an imagined questioner pressing him on the unusual claims he’s making.

He opens by saying what he’s brought: the buddha dharma transmitted in an unbroken line from Śākyamuni, and above all the practice of zazen, which he calls the right gate. He describes zazen as a self-fulfilling samādhi, an activity complete in itself that aims at nothing outside the sitting. When a person sits upright, even briefly, he says, they express the Buddha’s posture with the whole body, and the entire world joins in.

The questions pursue the obvious objections. Why only sitting? Why not chanting, incense, bowing, or reading? Does one have to be a monk? Can laypeople awaken? What about the view that the mind is permanent while the body perishes? He gives firm answers to each. He rejects the idea of an eternal inner spirit, and he says people who think they can skip sitting for other practices haven’t understood what’s on offer. The text also includes his account of searching for a true teacher. It’s as much an argument for authenticity as for a method: the way has to be received from someone who has it, then tested in practice.

In his words, roughly
Practice and realization are one. Sitting isn’t a means to awakening. It’s awakening taking place.
Even a single period of upright sitting expresses the buddhas’ posture, and the whole world joins in.

Discernment. The whole treatise argues for verified transmission and complete practice, against casual blending and shortcuts.

02生ShōjiBirth and Death

The takeawayDon’t flee birth and death, and don’t cling to them. Let go into the buddha’s way and follow it.

A short text on the problem of birth and death, the most basic human concern. It isn’t part of Dōgen’s own arrangement of the Shōbōgenzō, but it appears in traditional Sōtō editions and has long been read alongside it. Some scholars question how much of it comes directly from him.

Its main idea is that birth and death, the cycle of ordinary existence, is itself the life of the Buddha. Trying to escape birth and death to find the Buddha somewhere else means losing the Buddha’s life, and clinging to birth and death as if they were solid and final means getting lost in them. The way runs through the middle of what’s happening.

The text ends with a famous instruction: throw body and mind into the house of the Buddha and let the Buddha act on them. Follow along without effort of body or mind and without grasping, and you leave birth and death behind and become a buddha. The message is trust in practice, not in a plan. Together with the fascicle on total dynamism, it forms a pair on how to face mortality: neither avoiding nor fixating, but taking part fully in what’s happening.

In his words, roughly
Birth and death is itself the buddha’s life.
Don’t hate birth and death, and don’t cling to them.

Discernment. Dōgen refuses both escapism and fixation. The honest posture toward mortality is full participation.

03唯Yuibutsu yobutsuOnly a Buddha Together with a Buddha

The takeawayRealization is mutual. It’s verified by those who have it, and nobody confirms themselves alone.

The title comes from the Lotus Sūtra’s statement that only a buddha together with a buddha can fully fathom the true reality of all things. Dōgen returns to this line again and again, and here he takes it as his main subject.

His claim is that realization of the dharma can’t be assessed from outside. Someone who hasn’t realized it can’t judge it, and someone who has realized it isn’t alone. The knowledge is shared and recognized between people. A buddha knows a buddha, and transmission is carried in that recognition. A student can’t decide alone that they have it, and a teacher can’t give it to someone unready or unwilling. Both are part of a mutual process that takes time and honesty.

The fascicle also carries some humility. If only buddhas fully know buddhas, then anyone still learning should be slow to pass final judgment on others and quick to accept the check of a teacher who knows more. This isn’t a doctrine of mystique. It’s about the limits of self-assessment. Along with the fascicles on self-verification and face-to-face transmission, it forms the core of Dōgen’s view of how authenticity gets established.

In his words, roughly
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
No one verifies their own realization alone.

Discernment. The limits of self-assessment are built into the tradition. Authenticity is established between people, over time.

1Four noble truths

The Buddha’s first teaching: a diagnosis, a cause, a prognosis, and a treatment plan. Pick a truth to read it.

Ordinary life comes with dukkha, usually translated as “suffering” but closer to a pervasive sense that things don’t quite satisfy. Birth, aging, illness, and death are unavoidable. So is losing what you value and dealing with what you don’t like. Even the good stuff is impermanent, so it can’t give you lasting security.

This isn’t pessimism. It’s a diagnosis. Before anyone proposes a remedy, the first truth asks you to describe your experience accurately.

In PracticeZen practitioners begin by sitting with discomfort instead of managing it away. The aching knees, restlessness, and boredom of zazen are treated as direct information about dukkha, observed without commentary.

In DōgenBirth and death aren’t enemies or refuges. Meet each fully. Shōji, Zenki

Dukkha comes from craving (taṇhā): for pleasure, for more existence, for getting rid of what you don’t want. Craving rests on ignorance (avijjā) of three facts, impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self, and on the belief in a separate self that has to be defended and fed.

In PracticeIn zazen, practitioners watch wanting, planning, and self-commentary arise and let each pass without following it. The practice is observation of craving rather than its suppression.

In DōgenMind can’t be grasped, and flowers fall even when we cling to them. Shin fukatoku, Genjōkōan

When craving fully stops, so does dukkha. That’s nirvana (nibbāna). It isn’t annihilation. It’s freedom from compulsive grasping, and the early texts say it can happen within a single lifetime.

In PracticeSōtō practice treats cessation as something enacted in each period of sitting, not reached once and kept. Monastic routines such as bowing, chanting, silent meals, and work practice rehearse that release in ordinary activity.

In DōgenTo study the self is to forget the self, and body and mind go into the house of the Buddha. Genjōkōan, Shōji

The way out is the Noble Eightfold Path, a training in wisdom, ethical conduct, and concentration. It’s meant to be practiced and tested in daily life, not just believed.

In PracticeZen communities express the path through shared forms: daily zazen, retreats (sesshin), precept ceremonies, and work practice. Each is a way of testing the eight factors in conduct rather than in belief.

In DōgenThe path is the buddhas’ own practice, and sitting is the way in. Sanjūshichihon bodai bunpō, Bendōwa

2The eightfold path

The fourth truth in eight parts, grouped under wisdom, ethical conduct, and concentration. Pick a factor to read it.

  • Wisdom
  • Ethical conduct
  • Concentration

1Wisdom

Right View

Sammā diṭṭhi

Seeing reality accurately, especially the four truths, impermanence, and cause and effect. It’s the orientation the other seven factors depend on.

In PracticeStudy of the sūtras and the ancestors’ sayings sits alongside zazen, and in private interviews (dokusan) a teacher checks a student’s understanding against actual experience.

In DōgenHe won’t accept any denial of cause and effect. Jinshin inga

2Wisdom

Right Intention

Sammā saṅkappa

The motive behind your actions: letting go, goodwill, and not causing harm.

In PracticeThe bodhisattva vows, including the vow to save all beings, are chanted regularly in Sōtō communities and at ordination, recited as an intention renewed rather than a goal achieved.

In DōgenAim the aspiration for awakening at others’ benefit, not your reputation. Hotsu bodai shin

3Ethical conduct

Right Speech

Sammā vācā

Speech that’s truthful, free of malice and divisiveness, and not frivolous.

In PracticeRetreats are often held in silence, and monastic training limits idle conversation. Speech in the dharma hall and at formal meals follows set forms meant to keep it deliberate.

In DōgenExpression should come from your own realization, and idle talk should go. Dōtoku, Hachi dainin gaku

4Ethical conduct

Right Action

Sammā kammanta

Conduct that avoids killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct. In general, no physical harm.

In PracticeZen formalizes ethics through the sixteen bodhisattva precepts, received in the jukai ceremony and recited at regular precept-renewal services.

In DōgenAvoiding evil is the natural expression of awakening. Shoaku makusa

5Ethical conduct

Right Livelihood

Sammā ājīva

Earning a living without harming others or relying on deception.

In PracticeMonastic training treats work as practice. The tenzo (head cook) and the daily work period (samu) are classic examples, and lay practitioners commonly treat their livelihoods as the main testing ground of the precepts.

In DōgenWant little, be content, and treat daily activity as the way itself. Hachi dainin gaku, Kajō

6Concentration

Right Effort

Sammā vāyāma

Preventing unwholesome states before they start, dropping ones that have started, cultivating wholesome states, and maintaining them. Sustained and moderate, neither forced nor lax.

In PracticeSōtō training is built on regularity: sitting at fixed hours, annual practice periods (ango), and seasonal retreats. Effort is expressed as consistency rather than intensity.

In DōgenContinuous practice is unbroken and unadvertised. Gyōji

7Concentration

Right Mindfulness

Sammā sati

Steady, non-reactive attention to the body, feelings, mind, and the contents of mind.

In PracticeZen cultivates mindfulness through formal activities done with deliberate attention, such as walking meditation (kinhin), eating from nested bowls (oryōki), and the physical forms of bowing and entering the meditation hall.

In DōgenThe four foundations of mindfulness are among the buddhas’ practices. Sanjūshichihon bodai bunpō

8Concentration

Right Concentration

Sammā samādhi

Unifying and steadying the mind. The early texts describe four absorptions (jhāna). In Dōgen, the matching practice is zazen.

In PracticeIn Sōtō Zen, concentration takes the form of shikantaza (“just sitting”), seated meditation without a particular object, usually practiced facing a wall in the meditation hall (zendō).

In DōgenSitting is the king of samādhis. Zazen gi, Zanmai ō zanmai

Shōbōgenzō

Principal founder of Japanese Sōtō Zen.

  • Early life
    • Born in Kyoto in 1200 to an aristocratic family and orphaned young
    • Ordained on Mount Hiei in the Tendai school
    • Stuck on one question: if everyone already has buddha-nature, why practice?
  • Study in China
    • Studied at Kennin-ji, then went to Song China in 1223
    • Visited several monasteries and found that many teachers had rank but not insight
    • Met Rujing at Tiantong monastery and trained under him
  • The turning point
    • In 1225, during intensive meditation, he went through what he called the dropping away of body and mind
    • Rujing confirmed it, and Dōgen received dharma transmission
  • Return and work
    • Came back to Japan in 1227, teaching that seated meditation is itself the practice of awakening
    • Taught near Kyoto until 1243, then moved to the mountains of Echizen and founded Eihei-ji in 1244
    • Died in 1253 at age fifty-three
  • The Shōbōgenzō
    • Written between 1231 and 1253 in everyday Japanese rather than classical Chinese
    • Dōgen put together a seventy-five-fascicle collection and started a later one of twelve
    • Modern editions run to ninety-five fascicles, counting variant versions and extra texts

5Authenticity

How to tell truth from delusion in teachers, in teachings, and in yourself.

Authenticity

Discernment
Question
What’s true, and what’s delusion?
Hardest case
The self
Method
Check the claim, its results, and the person checking
Test
Conduct over time

Here, authenticity isn’t a personal brand or a feeling of being genuine. It’s the ongoing work of telling truth from delusion.

  • What it means
    • The work of telling truth from delusion in teachers, in teachings, and above all in yourself
    • Yourself is the hardest case, because the mind keeps building a story of a unified “me,” revising it as events unfold, and presenting it as direct experience
    • Sustained sitting lets you watch that construction happen, including noticing that the watcher is part of what’s being built
    • Delusion isn’t a personal flaw. It’s the default setting. The Shōbōgenzō keeps prescribing the same discipline: check the claim, check its results, and check the one doing the checking
  • Truth and delusion
    • Both are present in every situation, so discernment is never finished
    • Test a claim by what it does in conduct, not by how persuasive it sounds
    • Genjōkōan: carrying the self forward to verify the myriad things is delusion, while letting the myriad things verify the self is awakening
  • How the mind builds a self
    • The story of a continuous self gets produced over and over, and feels most convincing when it’s loudest
    • The feeling of having arrived at understanding is one more construction, which is why Dōgen treats it as a warning sign
    • Muchū setsumu (a dream within a dream), Kūge (sky flowers), Gabyō (painted cakes)
  • The three poisons
    • GreedtonCraving more, including a better version of yourself.
    • AngershinPushing away whatever threatens your self-image.
    • DelusionchiNot seeing these processes, and not seeing that you’re not seeing.
    • Related reading: Ippyakuhachi hōmyō mon, and Shoaku makusa on avoiding harm.
  • Compassion for the deluded
    • Dōgen, paraphrased from Bodaisatta shishōbōSpeak to beings the way you’d speak to an infant in your care. Praise those who have virtue, and feel for those who don’t.
    • Read next to the three poisons, this suggests most people stuck in greed, anger, and delusion can’t see them, so they can’t see that they’re suffering. Blame treats an inability to see as a choice. Pity treats it as a condition
    • The same goes for your own mind. It deserves the same pity

How it runs through the Shōbōgenzō

The same discernment, traced fascicle by fascicle.

  • Verified transmission
    • Real teaching passes between identifiable people whose claims can be checked
    • Shisho, Menju, Den e, Busso, Jishō zanmai, Yuibutsu yobutsu
  • No exemption from consequence
    • No attainment excuses harm, and deep meditative states aren’t the same as realization
    • Dai shugyō, Jinshin inga, Sanji gō, Shoaku makusa, Shizen biku
  • The ordinary over the spectacular
    • Powers, secrecy, and display don’t count as proof. Ordinary conduct does
    • Jinzū, Tashin tsū, Hakujushi, Mitsugo, Kajō, Kobutsushin
  • Honoring what’s real, whatever the title
    • The dharma deserves respect wherever it shows up, and criticism should be accurate
    • Raihai tokuzui, Henzan, Arakan, Butsudō, Kankin
  • Form carries the teaching
    • Precise forms and small actions are where a practice shows how serious it is
    • Zazen gi, Hou, Senmen, Senjō, Kesa kudoku
  • Never finished
    • Awakening isn’t a fixed status, and the feeling of having arrived is a warning
    • Genjōkōan, Daigo, Bukkōjōji, Gyōji, Keisei sanshoku
