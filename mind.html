Dogen

A monk who refused to take anything on trust, including his own experience.

  • Early life
    • Born in Kyoto in 1200 into the aristocracy, and orphaned young
    • Ordained on Mount Hiei in the Tendai school
    • Left with one nagging question: if everyone already has buddha-nature, why practice?
  • The journey
    • Studied at Kennin-ji, then sailed to China in 1223
    • Visited monasteries and was unimpressed by teachers who had titles but not clarity
    • Met Rujing at Tiantong and trained under him
  • The turning point
    • In 1225, during intensive sitting, he experienced the dropping away of body and mind
    • Rujing confirmed it, and Dogen received transmission
  • Return and work
    • Came back to Japan in 1227 with a single message: just sit
    • Taught near Kyoto, then founded Eihei-ji in the mountains in 1244
    • Died in 1253, aged 53
  • The Shōbōgenzō
    • Written between 1231 and 1253, in vernacular Japanese rather than classical Chinese
    • Dogen arranged a 75-fascicle collection and began a late collection of twelve
    • Modern editions run to 95 fascicles, counting variant versions and added texts

Shōbōgenzō

The Treasury of the True Dharma Eye. A full outline, one entry per fascicle. Open any entry for the synopsis, the main lesson, and paraphrased lines.

Part one: the seventy-five

Dogen’s own arrangement, in the order of the 75-fascicle collection.

01GenjōkōanActualizing the Fundamental Point

Main lessonAwakening is not a possession. It is practice that leaves no trace and has no end.

The most widely read piece Dogen wrote, composed in 1233 as a letter-essay for a lay student. It is short and every line carries weight. It opens with a map: seen from one side, there are delusion and awakening, practice, life and death, buddhas and ordinary beings; seen from another, none of those things has a fixed self. The way is neither of these, and yet flowers fall while we cling to them and weeds grow while we resent them.

Then comes the central reversal. Trying to carry the self out to verify the ten thousand things is delusion; letting the ten thousand things come forward and verify the self is awakening. Dogen gives images rather than arguments: a person in a boat who thinks the shore is moving, firewood that does not become ash but has its own before and after, the moon lodged whole in a dewdrop without wetting or breaking the water.

Near the end he warns against stopping. A fish and a bird each go as far as their element allows and nothing stops them, but if either tried to find the end of water or sky, it would have nowhere to stand. The closing story is of a monk fanning himself who is told that the nature of wind is permanent, and asked why he fans. The master’s answer is simply to fan. Practice is what realization looks like when it is actually happening.

Paraphrased lines
Studying the way means studying the self. Studying the self means forgetting it. Forgetting it, you are confirmed by everything that arises.
When the teaching has not yet filled you, you think you already have enough. When it does, you notice something is missing.

Discernment. The second line is a built-in test for any claim of understanding: the feeling of being finished is the warning sign.

02Maka hannya haramitsuThe Great Perfection of Wisdom

Main lessonWisdom is not an idea about emptiness. It is a whole body that rings equally in any wind.

Dogen takes the opening of the Heart Sutra, in which the bodhisattva practicing deep wisdom sees that the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, volition, consciousness) are empty. The usual reading makes emptiness a verdict on the aggregates. Dogen reads the line the other way around: the five aggregates are themselves the wisdom, and seeing them clearly is practice, not a conclusion reached afterwards.

He is attacking a quiet habit of scholars who treat the Heart Sutra as a teaching to be understood and filed. For him, wisdom has no location in the head. The whole body-mind is prajna, and so is the world it moves through. Emptiness is not nothing; it is what allows each thing to be fully what it is.

The famous image comes from his teacher Rujing: a bell hung in the sky that does not care whether the wind comes from east, west, south, or north, and rings the same prajna for everyone. The bell is not smart about the wind. It is simply hung, open, and responsive.

This is the fascicle to read when you are tempted to turn practice into a philosophy. The test is not whether you can explain emptiness. It is whether you respond to what comes without favoring one direction and refusing another.

Paraphrased lines
The whole body is like a bell hung in empty sky. Whatever wind comes, it simply rings wisdom.
The five aggregates are not in the way of wisdom. They are wisdom, practicing.

Discernment. Dogen rereads a famous sutra against the habit of treating it as an idea. A real understanding shows up in how you respond, not in how well you can explain it.

03BusshōBuddha-Nature

Main lessonBuddha-nature is not something you have or lack. It is the whole of what is, including change.

Among the longest fascicles, and one of the three most central to the Sōtō school. Dogen takes the famous line “all beings have Buddha-nature” and breaks it open. Read the usual way, it sounds like every being possesses a hidden essence. Dogen reads it as “all beings, the whole of being, is Buddha-nature.” Nothing is added to a being; the being itself is the nature.

He works through old cases. When the Fifth Ancestor told a newcomer from the south that people from that region have no Buddha-nature, the young Huineng answered that a person may be from north or south but Buddha-nature has no north or south. In another famous exchange, a monk asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature and gets different answers at different times, and Dogen shows neither answer is a position but each is a living response.

The passage most often quoted is Huineng’s teaching that impermanence is itself Buddha-nature. Dogen refuses the picture of an unchanging soul under the flow of things. Grass, trees, and change are not obstacles to nature; they are nature.

He also says that if you want to understand Buddha-nature you should watch the right conditions of time, and when the time comes, it is simply there. That ties directly into his work on being-time: you are not waiting for it, you are inside it.

Paraphrased lines
Buddha-nature is not having and not lacking. It is the whole being itself.
Impermanence is not the enemy of buddha-nature. Impermanence is buddha-nature.

Discernment. Dogen guards against the oldest misreading, the idea of a permanent inner essence, by insisting that realization is the actual, changing present rather than a private possession.

04Shinjin gakudōLearning the Way with Body and Mind

Main lessonStudy the way with the body and the mind together. Neither one is the road alone.

Dogen asks what it means to study the way and answers that it has two sides, body and mind, which cannot be pulled apart. Learning with the mind begins with the aspiration to practice and keeps being renewed by it. Learning with the body means sitting, posture, and the physical forms of training. He insists neither is optional and neither is the real one hiding behind the other.

A recurring warning runs through the text: do not take “mind” to mean only the thinking, discriminating mind that sits in the head and has opinions. In his usage mind includes the world, so studying mind is not an inward retreat. The body, in turn, is not an obstacle to be overcome but the actual place where practice happens.

For anyone who works for a living this is an unusually practical fascicle. The mind that reads, plans, and worries is real, but it is not the whole of you. Posture, breath, hands, and work are also the way. A day spent mostly in thought is not a day of practice, and a day of mere motion without mind is not either.

Dogen’s tone is firm. Studying the way is not a hobby to be fitted in once everything else is settled. It is the thing everything else is arranged around.

Paraphrased lines
Learning the way with the mind is not following the chatter of thought. Learning it with the body is not ignoring the mind.
Body and mind are not two roads. Walk the one road with both.

Discernment. He rules out two easy escapes: a purely mental spirituality and a purely physical technique. Authentic practice has to involve the whole person.

05Sokushin zebutsuThe Very Mind Is Buddha

Main lessonA slogan misread is a trap. “Mind is Buddha” means the whole activity of practice, not a private inner self.

The phrase “this very mind is Buddha” is often repeated in Zen, and Dogen treats it as dangerous when taken carelessly. One common reading says that your ordinary thinking mind already is Buddha and needs no practice. Another says there is an eternal spirit inside you that survives the death of the body. Dogen rejects both, and he cites the old teacher Nanyang Huizhong, who criticised those who taught a permanent mind inside a perishing body as a heresy.

What he puts in its place is an account of the mind as the whole movement of practice: arousing the aspiration, training, realizing the way, entering nirvana. Each stage is “the very mind,” and each is the Buddha. The phrase does not point to something inside you. It points to the entire activity of buddhas practicing.

This is a good fascicle to keep nearby whenever a fashionable idea about the mind starts sounding like a shortcut. Any teaching that tells you that you are already complete and need only believe it is a candidate for this critique. Dogen would say that the phrase is true, but it is true in the doing, not in the belief.

The fascicle is brisk and argumentative. It is Dogen defending a phrase from its own popularity.

Paraphrased lines
The very mind is not a thing inside the head. It is the whole of arousing, practicing, realizing, and entering nirvana.
Believing in an eternal inner spirit is not the buddha way.

Discernment. A clean example of discernment: a true teaching can become a false one the moment it is turned into a comforting slogan.

06Gyōbutsu igiThe Dignified Conduct of the Practicing Buddha

Main lessonAwakening is enacted in concrete, dignified action. There is no buddha apart from practice.

The title means something like the deportment, or dignified activity, of the buddha who is practicing. Dogen is making a point that comes up everywhere in his writing: there is no Buddha who has finished and stepped out of practice. Buddhas are the ones who are practicing, and practice is not preparation for something else. It is what buddhahood looks like from the outside and the inside.

The word igi, dignity in conduct, matters. In monastic training the forms of walking, sitting, standing, eating, and bowing are not decoration. They are where realization takes physical shape. A buddha does not float above the body. A buddha stands in a particular way, picks up a bowl in a particular way, and the manner is part of the teaching.

Dogen is also pushing against a picture in which awakening happens once, in a flash, and everything afterward is anticlimax. In his view that flash, if it occurs, is an event within practice and not its conclusion. After it, practice continues, and the dignity of that continuing is what he is describing.

For modern readers the lesson translates easily. The quality of a person’s work shows in how they do small physical things, not in how they describe their values.

Paraphrased lines
Buddhas do not stop practicing. Practice is what a buddha is doing.
The way of walking, sitting, and standing is not decoration. It is where the teaching takes shape.

Discernment. Look at conduct, not claims. In Dogen’s view, how a person actually moves through ordinary tasks is the evidence.

07Ikka myōjuOne Bright Pearl

Main lessonThe whole world is one bright pearl, and no explanation of it is needed to be it.

The fascicle is built on a short exchange. A teacher, Xuansha, says that the entire universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl. A monk asks how to understand it. Xuansha replies, in effect, what use is understanding? The next day he asks the monk how he now understands, and when the monk repeats the same answer back, Xuansha tells him he is living in a ghost cave in a black mountain.

Dogen does not read this as a trick. He reads it as a lesson about the difference between holding an idea and being the thing. The monk repeats the words correctly but has turned the pearl into a concept. Xuansha’s rebuke is not that the words are wrong, but that they are dead.

The bright pearl, for Dogen, is not a metaphor for the totality of everything; it is each thing, fully itself, with nothing hidden. A pearl does not have to be understood to be bright. A mountain does not need your analysis to be a mountain.

The fascicle is a good reminder that a correct statement and a living realization are different things, and that a good teacher can tell the difference quickly. The difference is not in the vocabulary. It is in whether the person is speaking from the pearl or about it.

Paraphrased lines
The whole universe in the ten directions is one bright pearl. What use is understanding?
Repeating the right words from the wrong place is living in a cave of ghosts.

Discernment. A sharp lesson in telling real understanding from a good imitation: the correct answer, repeated, can still be dead.

08Shin fukatokuThe Mind Cannot Be Got

Main lessonYou cannot hold onto the mind, past, present, or future. Let go of the grasping and the mind is simply what is happening.

The title comes from a line in the Diamond Sutra: past mind cannot be got, present mind cannot be got, future mind cannot be got. The fascicle revolves around the story of Deshan, a scholar famous for his knowledge of that very sutra, who traveled south carrying his commentaries in a bundle on his back. On the road he stops and asks an old woman selling cakes for a snack to refresh his mind (a pun on “tenshin,” a light meal and a point of the mind). She asks which mind he proposes to refresh, since the sutra he knows so well says none can be got.

Deshan, who has written books on the subject, cannot answer. The old woman sends him on to a teacher, and the traditional telling is that he went on to burn his commentaries.

Dogen takes the “cannot be got” seriously and makes it practical. Mind is not an object you can hold up to examine, because by the time you point to it, it has changed. The wish to capture a state of mind, a good meditation or a clear moment, is itself a form of grasping. Letting go of the wish is not the loss of mind; it is mind unobstructed.

The story is also an early lesson in humility. A person who knows the text perfectly is stopped cold by a woman who sells cakes.

Paraphrased lines
The past mind cannot be got. The present mind cannot be got. The future mind cannot be got.
What mind will you refresh? The one who has written the commentary has no answer.

Discernment. Scholarly knowledge and lived understanding are tested against each other here, and the scholar has to admit the gap.

09KobutsushinThe Old Buddha Mind

Main lessonThe mind of the ancients is not a secret inner state. It is fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles.

Presented to students in 1243, this fascicle starts with a monk asking the National Teacher Nanyang Huizhong what the old buddha mind is. The answer is blunt: fences, walls, tiles, and pebbles. A monk who expected something refined and interior hears a list of ordinary objects.

Dogen takes this at face value and also pushes it further. If the old buddha mind were hidden inside, it would be a mystery to be unlocked. Instead it is what is plainly in front of you. The wall is not a symbol of mind; the wall is the mind in action. The tile, the pebble, and the fence are not lower than awareness. They are the form awareness takes.

He is careful about the word “old.” It does not mean antique. The old buddha mind is old in the sense of being complete, with nothing to be added by a later improvement. It is also fully present, which means it cannot be preserved in a book or inherited like property. Each person has to meet it as it is.

For anyone who works with materials, wood, paper, ink, metal, pixels, the fascicle offers a compact encouragement. The thing in your hands is not a distraction from the real work. It is the work, and the attention it deserves is the practice.

Paraphrased lines
What is the old buddha mind? Fences, walls, tiles, pebbles.
Not a symbol of mind. The wall itself, doing what a wall does, is the mind.

Discernment. Dogen refuses the glamorous version of awakening in favor of the ordinary things in front of you, which is the opposite of a performance.

10DaigoGreat Awakening

Main lessonAwakening is not a one-time event. There is awakening within awakening and practice that goes on after it.

Dogen takes up the phrase great awakening and treats it with suspicion toward anyone who thinks of it as a finish line. In his view, awakening is not a peak experience you reach and keep. It has no fixed shape you could point to, and the one who is awake does not stand outside delusion as a spectator.

A major thread in the fascicle is that awakening and delusion are not simple opposites. A person can awaken within delusion, and can be deluded within awakening, which sounds paradoxical but matches what any serious practitioner reports. A moment of clarity does not guarantee a clear day. Dogen wants his students to hold both facts without drama.

He also criticises the use of the term as a status. If someone presents themselves as having had a great awakening and therefore as above the common run, Dogen’s whole approach says the claim should lower your trust, not raise it. The real thing is quiet, continues in ordinary activity, and does not need an audience.

This fascicle works well as a counterweight to any teaching that dramatizes awakening or sells it as a result. If it can be packaged and delivered, it is probably not what Dogen is talking about.

Paraphrased lines
Great awakening is not finished. There is awakening upon awakening.
An awakened person who stands above the world has misunderstood what awakened means.

Discernment. The strongest warning in the fascicle is against status. Someone who presents awakening as a credential is a red flag.

11Zazen giPrinciples of Zazen

Main lessonSit upright, think without thinking, and let sitting itself be the practice.

The practical heart of Dogen’s teaching. The fascicle is a set of instructions on how to sit, and it reads as concrete and unromantic. Find a quiet room. Use a thick mat and a cushion. Put aside involvements and let the body-mind settle. Sit in full or half lotus, with the left foot on the right thigh, or the reverse. Place the hands one in the other, thumbs lightly touching. Keep the spine straight, ears in line with shoulders and nose in line with the navel, tongue against the palate, lips and teeth closed, eyes open, breathing quietly through the nose.

After the body is arranged, the mind is addressed with a famous exchange. What do you think about as you sit still? You think of not-thinking. How do you think of not-thinking? Non-thinking. This is the essential art of zazen.

Dogen goes further than technique. Zazen, he says, is not meditation practice as a means to something else. It is the dharma gate of great ease and joy, the practice and realization of complete awakening in one act. The sitting does not lead you to awakening. It is awakening, taking this particular shape.

There is also advice on how to get up. Move slowly and calmly, without hurry, and carry the settled quality of sitting into the rest of the day. The practice does not end when the bell rings.

Paraphrased lines
Think of not-thinking. How do you think of not-thinking? Non-thinking. This is the art of zazen.
Zazen is not a practice for gaining awakening. It is the gate of great ease and joy.

Discernment. The details matter. Dogen gives exact form because he distrusts a practice that is only an attitude.

12Zazen shinThe Lancet of Zazen

Main lessonNon-thinking is not a blank. It is the free activity of fish in water and birds in sky.

A short, dense, partly poetic text. A lancet is a small blade used to lance a boil, and Dogen’s point is that this fascicle is meant to cut cleanly through confusion about sitting. It builds on an earlier poem by the Chinese teacher Hongzhi and offers a poem of his own at the end.

At the center is the old exchange of Yaoshan, who is asked what he thinks about while sitting so steadily. He says he thinks of not-thinking. Asked how, he says: beyond thinking. Dogen spends most of the fascicle on this. Not-thinking is not a mental dullness or a trance in which thought has been turned off. It is also not thinking about a certain thing. It is a way of being awake that includes thinking passing without being grasped.

The images are physical. The fish swims, and however far it goes there is no end to the water. The bird flies, and however far it goes there is no end to the sky. The sitting person is like both. They do not travel toward a goal; they move completely within their element.

The text warns against two mistakes. One is thinking sitting is a technique to produce a result. The other is thinking it is passive. Dogen says it is neither, and that the only way to learn the difference is to sit.

Paraphrased lines
Thinking of not-thinking. How? Beyond thinking.
The fish swims and there is no end to the water. The bird flies and there is no end to the sky.

Discernment. Dogen refuses both a technique that promises results and a passivity that avoids work. The difference can only be tested by doing it.

13Kaiin zanmaiThe Ocean Seal Samadhi

Main lessonThe whole world appears at once and at full depth in each moment of settled practice.

The title draws on a Mahayana image of a samadhi, or deep concentration, in which everything is reflected the way a calm ocean holds the sky, the shore, and every passing cloud at once. The word “seal” is the sense of a stamp: the single impression that fits all things.

Dogen takes this teaching and ties it directly to ordinary sitting. The samadhi is not a special state far from daily practice, and the ocean it describes is not far away. The same activity that holds a spine upright and a mind quiet holds the whole world in view, because that world is not outside the one sitting. It shows up in the sitting.

He also uses the image to correct a common mistake, which is imagining the ocean as a single huge thing and each wave as small and separate. In his view the ocean is not more real than the wave. Each wave is the ocean entirely, at that spot, in that moment. A single moment of practice is not a fragment of some larger enlightenment to come. It is the whole sea.

The fascicle is not easy to summarise, since it works by accumulation and echo. Readers often find it more rewarding to read a page at a time, slowly, and let the repeated image do its work.

Paraphrased lines
The ocean is not larger than the wave. Each wave is the whole ocean, right where it is.
One moment of settled practice is not part of enlightenment. It is the whole of it.

Discernment. The seal metaphor is a reminder that what is genuine carries the same imprint everywhere and does not need a special stage or setting.

14KūgeSky Flowers

Main lessonWhat seems like an illusion is also the actual flowering of things. Do not dismiss appearance, and do not cling to it.

Sky flowers are the spots a person with a diseased eye sees floating in clear air, an old Buddhist image for delusion. They look like flowers, but there are no flowers there, and the point of the image is usually that deluded seeing creates things that are not real.

Dogen takes up the image and refuses the easy conclusion. If sky flowers are simply illusions to be dismissed, then the person who dismisses them has set up a picture of a real, flowerless sky behind the appearance and is clinging to that. He asks his listeners to look more carefully. The flowers bloom in the sky, they fall, and the whole world is in this activity of blooming and falling.

This is not an argument for naive belief in whatever one perceives. It is an argument against a lazy kind of skepticism that takes pride in seeing through everything. Dogen wants the seeing to be accurate, which includes seeing that appearance has a way of being that is not simply a mistake.

The fascicle is a good one to read when you catch yourself reaching for easy dismissals, whether of a person’s work, a tradition, or an experience. Dogen’s habit is to slow down and look at what is actually there before deciding what to call it.

Paraphrased lines
The flowers in the sky bloom and fall. To call them mere illusion is only another way of clinging.
See through appearance without turning your seeing into a new appearance.

Discernment. Cynicism is a counterfeit of discernment. Dogen wants accurate seeing, not reflexive disbelief.

15KōmyōIlluminating Wisdom

Main lessonLight is not a glow in the mind. It is the actual world seen without obstruction.

Dogen builds this fascicle on a teaching of the master Yunmen, who said that everyone has their own light but when they look for it they cannot see, and all is dark. A monk asked what everyone’s light is, and Yunmen answered with the monks’ hall, the kitchen, and the gate. The reply is deliberately flat.

Dogen’s reading is that illumination, in the Zen sense, is not a luminous inner experience. If you sit hoping to see a light, you are looking for the wrong thing, which is why the looking only finds darkness. The light is the ordinary world, the hall, the kitchen, the gate, shown plainly. It is available at any moment because it is not hidden. What gets in the way is the effort to find it somewhere else.

There is a clear caution here for anyone who has absorbed popular ideas about spiritual light, energy, or auras. Dogen is not denying that people have vivid experiences. He is saying that a vivid experience is not what the teaching is about, and that treating it as the goal is the dark.

The fascicle is also a quiet commentary on responsibility. If the light is the kitchen and the gate, then the work done in those places is the illumination, and the quality of attention you bring to them is the practice.

Paraphrased lines
Everyone has their own light. When they look for it, they cannot see it. What is it? The hall, the kitchen, the gate.
If you are waiting for a glow, you are looking in the dark.

Discernment. A pointed correction to anyone selling inner light. The illumination is the plain, ordinary world seen clearly.

16GyōjiContinuous Practice

Main lessonPractice is not your personal project. It is the unbroken activity of the whole line of ancestors, and it runs through you.

The longest and most moving of the fascicles about practice, often printed in two parts. Dogen walks through the lives of the ancestors and shows what continuous practice looked like for each of them. They are not heroes who had a breakthrough and coasted. They are people who kept at it, day after day, often in obscurity.

The best-known story is of Baizhang, an old master who kept working in the fields with the monks even when he was very old. The monks, worried about him, hid his tools. He refused to eat until they returned them, saying a day without work is a day without eating. For Dogen this is not about farming. It is about the refusal to hold practice as an optional extra once you are senior or tired.

He insists that continuous practice cannot be assigned to the self. The merit of it belongs to the whole world. You do not practice for your own credit, and you cannot see where it starts or stops. When you do it, you are doing something that has been done for a very long time, and that will continue.

The text is also a quiet standard. Authentic practice, in Dogen’s picture, does not announce itself. Look for the person who is still doing the plain work without fanfare.

Paraphrased lines
Continuous practice is not for yourself. It is the practice of the whole world, passing through you.
A day without work is a day without eating.

Discernment. The test is persistence without display. Quiet, unbroken, unadvertised practice is what Dogen holds up as the real thing.

17InmoThus, What Is It?

Main lessonThe real thing cannot be named like an object, but it is not absent. Practice and realization are not defiled by grasping.

The fascicle turns on one of the most important exchanges in Zen. The Sixth Ancestor, Huineng, asks a newcomer named Nanyue what it is that thus comes. Nanyue cannot answer and stays with the question for eight years. When he finally speaks, he says that to say it is like a thing would miss the mark.

Huineng then asks whether there is practice and realization. Nanyue says it is not that there is none, but they cannot be defiled. Huineng replies that this very non-defilement is what the buddhas protect and keep in mind.

Dogen takes each line slowly. The question “what is it?” is not asking for an answer to be filed. It is a way of keeping a person in the actual. The eight years matter: Nanyue did not produce a clever reply but lived with the question until it was part of him.

The word “defiled” is also important. Practice and realization are not absent, which would make Zen a negation. They are also not something you can grab and own, because the grabbing is the defilement. What the ancestors protect is the freedom of practice from the habit of making it into an object or an achievement.

This is a fascicle for anyone who has noticed their own practice or their own craft turning into a self-image. The remedy is not to drop it, but to keep it clean.

Paraphrased lines
What is it that thus comes? To say it is like a thing misses the mark.
It is not that there is no practice and realization. It is only that they cannot be defiled.

Discernment. Naming and grasping are the contaminants. A real practice stays free of both.

18KannonAvalokiteshvara

Main lessonCompassion is a whole-body, unplanned response, like reaching for a pillow in the dark. And a good teacher says honestly when the answer is only eight-tenths.

The fascicle is about the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara, whose images often show a thousand hands each with an eye. Dogen uses an exchange between two brothers in the dharma, Yunyan and Daowu. Yunyan asks what the great compassionate bodhisattva does with so many hands and eyes. Daowu says it is like someone reaching behind them in the night for the pillow.

Yunyan says he understands. Daowu asks what he understands. Yunyan says the whole body is hands and eyes. Daowu answers that this is well said, but only eight-tenths of it. Yunyan asks how he would say it. Daowu says the whole body is hands and eyes throughout.

Dogen’s interest is both in the image and in the exchange. The image of reaching for the pillow in the dark says that compassion does not involve planning, deliberating, or checking a rulebook. It is the body responding to a need it knows without thinking. There is no helper and no one helped, only the action.

The second half of the exchange is just as important. Daowu does not praise a good answer. He says it is eight-tenths of the truth and pushes for the rest. A real teacher or friend does not flatter. The text is a model of how to be honest with someone you respect.

Paraphrased lines
It is like reaching behind you in the night for the pillow.
Well said, but only eight-tenths.

Discernment. Honest evaluation is itself a kindness. Daowu does not flatter a good answer, and Dogen records it approvingly.

19KokyōThe Old Mirror

Main lessonA mirror shows each thing exactly as it is. Realization is that kind of clarity, not a fixed possession.

The fascicle uses the old image of the mirror to talk about the nature of awareness, and Dogen works through several traditional sayings. A famous one says that when a foreigner comes before the mirror, a foreigner appears, and when a Chinese person comes, a Chinese person appears. The mirror does not pick or prefer. It simply shows what is there.

Dogen takes this further. The mirror is not a mind that stores images, and the images are not impressions inside it. The mirror and the thing reflected are one event. An old mirror is old because it is not new, which is to say it has no beginning point. It is the clarity that has always been present, the way a clear eye is always a clear eye.

He is also critical of readings of the mirror that make it a passive device with a clean surface that needs polishing. In an earlier Chinese tradition, a poem about polishing the mirror to remove dust was set against another about there being no mirror to polish. Dogen does not choose one side. He points to practice that is neither polishing to get somewhere nor dismissing polishing.

The practical sense is that clarity is a way of meeting things, not a state to guard. When you meet each thing as it comes, without preference or distortion, the mirror is working.

Paraphrased lines
When a foreigner comes, a foreigner appears. When a Chinese comes, a Chinese appears.
The mirror is not a storehouse of images. The images and the mirror are one event.

Discernment. Clear seeing means not bending the picture toward what you prefer to see.

20UjiBeing-Time

Main lessonYou are not in time. You are time. Each moment is complete and the whole of existence.

One of the best-known fascicles and the most philosophically daring. Dogen takes the ordinary expression uji, which means “sometimes,” and splits it into its parts: being and time. All being is time, and all time is being. A mountain, a pot, a person each is a moment of time.

He begins with a poem about the different forms a person might take at different times: at times standing on a mountain peak, at times at the bottom of the deepest sea, at times with three heads and eight arms, at times a sixteen-foot golden Buddha. Dogen reads this not as a sequence of changes but as a series of whole beings, each complete in its own moment.

The image he uses most is firewood and ash. We say that firewood becomes ash, but Dogen says firewood does not turn into ash. Firewood is firewood, with its before and after. Ash is ash, with its before and after. Each is a full dharma position, cut off from the other and yet continuous.

The teaching dissolves the picture of time as a stream that carries you from past toward future. The past is not behind you waiting to be revisited. It is the way this moment is. When you do whatever you are doing now, you are doing the whole of time.

This is the fascicle for anyone who feels that practice or work is a means to a later state. The only time to do it is now.

Paraphrased lines
Time does not fly past you. Each being is a moment of time, whole in itself.
Firewood does not become ash. Firewood is firewood, with its before and after. Ash is ash, with its before and after.

Discernment. The hardest test of authenticity is the present moment. Dogen refuses any practice that defers its meaning to later.

21JukiConferring Predictions

Main lessonReal confirmation is given face to face and lived, not claimed.

In Buddhist tradition a prediction (vyakarana) is the moment a buddha assures a practitioner that they will awaken. It appears across the sutras, especially in the Lotus Sutra, where whole groups of disciples are told what kind of buddhas they will become. Dogen takes this ritual of assurance and asks what it means in the Zen transmission.

His answer moves it out of the future and into the present. A prediction is not a forecast about later. It is the recognition, in the middle of practice, that the person is already inside the way, and it is made by someone who can actually see that. The teacher confirms; the student does not appoint themselves.

That emphasis on who is doing the confirming is characteristic. For Dogen, the line of ancestors is a chain of persons who verified each other. A claim to be awakened that has no teacher behind it, no lineage and no one who has watched you practice, is not a prediction at all. It is only a story you are telling yourself.

The fascicle belongs to the part of his work that is about trust. It is a reminder that a real recognition is humbling and external, and that it is wise to be wary of anyone, including yourself, who hands it out without checking.

Paraphrased lines
A prediction is not a forecast of later. It is a recognition, given now, by one who can see.
You cannot certify yourself.

Discernment. The thread here is verification. Dogen ties awakening to being seen by a real teacher, not to a private claim.

22ZenkiTotal Dynamism

Main lessonLife and death are each the whole function. Meet each completely and do not flee from either.

Zenki means the full activity of a thing, the whole working of what is happening right now. Dogen applies it to the most basic subject of all, life and death. His proposal is that life is not a stretch of time that death will later cut off, and death is not what follows life. Each is a complete and independent function of the whole.

His image is a person riding a boat. The rider raises the sails, works the oar, steers with the pole. Yet the boat carries the rider. Without the boat there is no rider, and the rider is the one who makes the boat a boat. Life is like that. You are living, and life is also living you, and you cannot take yourself out and look at it from outside.

He goes on to say that birth does not come from somewhere and death does not go somewhere. In birth you are completely birth. In death you are completely death. There is nothing left over to be afraid of, because there is no second self standing apart to be threatened.

This is not meant as consolation. Dogen is doing something harder than reassuring. He is asking you to stop treating your life as a project that might be spoiled, and instead to see that each moment is already complete. That tends to change how you work.

Paraphrased lines
Life is like riding in a boat. You work the sails and the oar, yet the boat carries you.
In life, be entirely life. In death, be entirely death.

Discernment. Dogen refuses comfort that is not true. He gives a harder, more accurate way to look at what scares us.

23TsukiThe Moon

Main lessonThe moon is not a symbol standing in for the Buddha. It is the whole thing, present at each place it appears.

Dogen takes the traditional Buddhist image of the moon, which stands for the awakened nature, and in typical fashion declines to treat it as a metaphor. The moon is not like the Buddha. In this fascicle the moon is the Buddha’s body, shown in the sky and in the water, whole in each place.

The old image of the moon reflected in many waters has an obvious weakness. It can suggest that there is one real moon far away and many small copies. Dogen blocks that reading. The moon in a puddle is the whole moon. The moon in a dewdrop is the whole moon. Each appearance is complete, and none is lesser. This is the same logic he uses for the ocean and the wave.

He also draws on traditional sayings in which a teacher is asked about the moon and answers with something practical or plain. The teaching is not found in a clever interpretation but in how the person reacts to what is actually in front of them.

The fascicle is gentle and short on argument. It works by repeating a few images until they sink in. For someone doing creative or technical work, the idea that a single small piece of work can hold the whole of the practice is encouraging. You do not need a grand project for it to be real.

Paraphrased lines
The moon in the dewdrop is the whole moon, not a piece of it.
There is not one true moon and many copies.

Discernment. The moon is whole wherever it appears, which means no one gets to claim a bigger or more real share of the teaching.

24GabyōPainted Cakes

Main lessonThere is no real world behind the painted one. Only a painted cake can satisfy hunger.

The fascicle starts from a saying attributed to the old Chinese teacher Xiangyan: a painted rice cake does not satisfy hunger. It sounds like common sense, and in much Buddhist teaching it was used to warn that words, pictures, and doctrines are only paintings of the real thing. Dogen turns it over.

His claim is that there is no real cake somewhere else that the painted one is a poor copy of. Everything we encounter is, in a certain sense, painted: formed, composed, presented. The buddhas and ancestors, the mountains and rivers, the sutras, the self are all part of the same painted world. If you decide that the painting is worthless and the real is elsewhere, you have not seen through anything. You have simply invented another painting.

So Dogen says that only a painted cake can satisfy hunger. The medicine for hunger is not outside the world of appearances. It is the very practice of using what is present, including words, images, and teachings, with full attention.

This is a useful antidote to a certain high-minded dismissal of art, text, and craft. A visual designer or a writer might hear the old saying and feel their work is only a shadow. Dogen says it is the actual way the dharma shows up. The cake is real, and the point is to make it well.

Paraphrased lines
A painted cake cannot satisfy hunger, says the old teacher. Dogen answers: only a painted cake can.
Do not look for a real world behind the painted one.

Discernment. Contempt for forms is a counterfeit of insight. Dogen makes a place for craft, art, and words as the actual medium of practice.

25Keisei sanshokuSounds of the Valley, Forms of the Mountain

Main lessonAwakening can be triggered by anything, but a single experience is not the end. Keep practicing and stay humble.

Written in 1240, this fascicle begins with a poem by Su Dongpo, a poet and official who was a layman and not a monk. He had been studying with a Chan teacher and, in the night, heard a stream in a valley. Afterward he wrote that the sound of the valley stream is the long broad tongue of the Buddha and the form of the mountain is his pure body. Through the night he heard eighty-four thousand verses, and he wondered how he could ever explain them to others the next day.

Dogen treats the poem as a real expression of realization, and he uses it to make several points. First, awakening does not belong only to monks. A layman writing a poem can see clearly, and Dogen will honor that. Second, the sound and the form are not metaphors. The stream is the teaching, and the mountain is the body.

Third, and most important, he warns against taking a vivid event as a conclusion. Two other famous figures, Xiangyan who heard a pebble strike bamboo and Lingyun who saw peach blossoms, are discussed as well. They had striking experiences, and Dogen holds that what matters is that they kept practicing afterward. An isolated experience is not proof of anything.

The fascicle ends with an unusually personal note about humility. Even a good practitioner should be cautious and keep going.

Paraphrased lines
The sound of the valley stream is the Buddha’s long broad tongue. The mountain’s form is his pure body.
Do not mistake one vivid moment for the end of the road.

Discernment. Dogen honors what is real wherever it appears, in a layman, in a poem, and also refuses to let a single experience become a credential.

26BukkōjōjiWhat Is Beyond the Buddha

Main lessonThere is no resting place called Buddha. Even realization keeps going.

The title is a phrase from the Chan tradition meaning something like going beyond the Buddha, or the matter that is beyond buddhahood. It is a provocative expression, because it seems to suggest there is a level above awakening. Dogen takes up the phrase and uses it to talk about continued practice.

If you think awakening is a final state, a place where the work stops, then you have made buddhahood into a seat and settled on it. Dogen’s point is that those who are truly awakened do not rest there. They take another step, and another, within the way. Going beyond the Buddha does not mean moving up to a higher location. It means refusing to stay put in any attainment.

This is consistent with his insistence that practice and realization are one. If realization were a finish line, practice would be over after crossing it. Instead, realization is a way of continuing.

The fascicle is a good check for anyone inclined to feel they have arrived, whether in a field of craft, in some practice, or in a career. The point is not to feel inadequate. It is to keep the freshness of someone who is still learning, since the moment you decide you are finished, the learning stops. For Dogen, that is the real danger.

Paraphrased lines
Do not make a seat out of buddhahood and settle in it.
To go beyond the Buddha is to keep walking, not to climb.

Discernment. Authentic practice does not turn its own achievements into a throne. Resting on attainment is how it dies.

27Muchū setsumuExpounding a Dream Within a Dream

Main lessonThe teaching is given inside this world, which is itself dream-like. Do not look for solid ground outside it.

The phrase describes buddhas teaching the dharma within a dream, and a dream within a dream. In Buddhist sources the world is often compared to a dream, and the point is usually that it is not as solid as it seems. Dogen takes this further by refusing to set up a waking reality outside the dream where things are finally firm.

The classic picture goes like this. We are dreaming, which is to say we are living in a world of unstable appearances. A buddha comes and says that all of this is a dream. We nod and think we have understood. Dogen says that this is only one dream explaining another. There is no outside from which the dream could be described.

The practical consequence is that the teaching is not a message from beyond. It is spoken from within the same world of confusion and appearance that the listeners are in. The buddha is in the dream with us, not hovering over it. Practice happens where we are.

For readers, the effect can be steadying. You do not need to wait for a perfect, clear view of things before acting. The one who acts well is the one who uses what is present, right in the middle of the uncertainty. That includes the work in front of you today.

Paraphrased lines
Even the awakened teach from inside the dream. There is no outside from which to explain it.
One dream explaining another is not yet seeing.

Discernment. Dogen refuses a vantage point above the world. Real teaching is done in the middle of it.

28Raihai tokuzuiGetting the Marrow by Doing Obeisance

Main lessonHonor the dharma wherever it is found, whoever carries it. Rank, age, and sex are irrelevant.

The title refers to a famous story of Bodhidharma, who asked each of four students to show their understanding. After the last of them, Huike, bowed three times and stood in his place, Bodhidharma said that Huike had gotten his marrow. Dogen takes the bowing seriously. Obeisance is not servility. It is how you receive the teaching from a person who has it.

The core of the fascicle is about whom to bow to. Dogen says that if a person has realized the dharma, you should bow to them, whatever their appearance, age, or station. He names the cases explicitly: a young child, a woman, a person the world looks down on. He is sharply critical of the idea that women should be kept out of sacred places or excluded from instruction, and he ridicules those who put their own rank above the dharma. The teaching is unequivocal and unusual for its time.

The deeper teaching is that authority comes from realization, not from title. If someone is a famous abbot but has not realized the dharma, there is no real reason to bow to them. If someone is a girl of seven who has it, bow.

This is one of the clearest statements of Dogen’s attitude toward authenticity. Look at what the person actually has, not what they are called.

Paraphrased lines
If a person has the dharma, bow to them, whether woman or man, old or young.
Bowing to a title is not bowing to the dharma.

Discernment. The sharpest test of discernment: respect what is real wherever it appears, and do not defer to what is only titled.

29Sansui kyōThe Mountains and Waters Sutra

Main lessonMountains and waters are the teaching, walking and flowing in plain view. Learn to see their activity.

The fascicle opens with a line often quoted out of context: the mountains are constantly walking. Dogen takes a verse from the old master Furong Daokai, who said that blue mountains are always walking and that a stone woman gives birth to a child in the night. He builds on this without trying to explain it away.

His claim is that the mountains do not merely stand there waiting to be admired. They are always doing what mountains do, which includes walking. We do not see it because we assume that walking requires legs and a destination. If we doubted that mountains walk, he says, it would be because we do not understand our own walking.

The fascicle also discusses water. Different beings see water differently. A fish sees a palace, a heavenly being sees jewels, a hungry ghost sees fire, and a human sees water. Dogen is not saying that reality is only a matter of perspective. He is making a point about how much of what we call real is bound up with our situation, and he wants us to hold that lightly while still taking the world seriously.

He ends with encouragement to train in the mountains. Mountains are not a retreat from the world. They are the sutra, the teaching itself, written in the actual landscape.

Paraphrased lines
The blue mountains are constantly walking.
To doubt that the mountains walk is to not yet know your own walking.

Discernment. Dogen trains attention on what is actually present and refuses to let a familiar assumption stand in for looking.

30KankinReading Sutras

Main lessonRead the teachings with your whole body and without contempt. Neither worship the words nor dismiss them.

A short fascicle about how to read the sutras. A common Zen attitude of the time held that the scriptures were only words and that real practice was outside them. Dogen pushes back, though not in the way a scholar would.

He tells a story about Yaoshan, a master who would not let his monks read the sutras. Yet the master read them himself. When asked about it, he said he was only trying to cover his eyes. Dogen does not read this as hypocrisy. He sees it as a remark about the kind of reading that matters: not reading to gather opinions, but reading as a way of seeing the world with the eyes of the teaching, so that the page becomes transparent.

The real sutra, he says, is not limited to the scroll. The whole universe is a sutra, and the scroll is part of it. When you read well, you read with the whole body, and you are also being read. He asks the reader to leave neither the written text nor the unwritten world out of account.

For anyone who studies, the fascicle is a gentle correction. It does not tell you to stop reading. It tells you to read as though your life depended on it, and to let the reading change what you do.

Paraphrased lines
Reading the sutra is not only done with the eyes. It is done with the whole body.
The whole world is a sutra, and the scroll is one part of it.

Discernment. Dogen refuses both extremes: worshiping text and dismissing it. Real reading changes the reader.

31Shoaku makusaNot Doing Evil

Main lessonNot doing harm is not a rule imposed from outside. It is how an awakened life naturally moves.

The fascicle takes up an exchange between the poet-official Bai Juyi and the teacher Niaoke, who lived in a tree. Bai Juyi asked what the great meaning of the Buddha’s teaching was. Niaoke answered that it is to do no evil and to practice all good. Bai Juyi said that even a three-year-old child could say that. Niaoke replied that a three-year-old can say it, but an eighty-year-old cannot do it.

Dogen’s reading is the center of the fascicle. The traditional verse is usually read as an instruction: do not do evil, do good. Dogen reads the grammar differently, so that “do not do evil” becomes a description. When awakening is actually happening, evil is simply not done, because it is not what that way of being does. The precept is not a fence around the way. It is what the way looks like when it is moving.

He is careful, though, not to turn this into complacency. The old man cannot do it, which means the words are easy and the doing is hard. A person who says they have realized and then causes harm has not realized.

This is a core passage for Dogen’s view of ethics and authenticity. The test of understanding is conduct, observed over time. The fascicle does not allow a gap between the two.

Paraphrased lines
A three-year-old can say it. An eighty-year-old cannot do it.
Not doing evil is not a command from outside. It is how awakening acts.

Discernment. Conduct is the proof. If understanding allows harm, it was not understanding.

32Den eTransmitting the Robe

Main lessonThe robe stands for an actual, verified transmission. It is not a costume or a trophy.

In Zen history the robe, the kesa, is passed from teacher to student as a sign that the teaching has been handed on. The legend reaches back to the first ancestor, Mahakasyapa, who was said to have received the robe from the Buddha. In the story of the Sixth Ancestor, the robe is a physical object with real consequences.

Dogen treats the robe as an expression of the thing it stands for. The transmission of the robe is not a ceremony added on top of the teaching, and it is not a certificate. It is how the teaching continues from one person to another. The robe is the dharma in a form you can hold.

The fascicle belongs with a group of his writings that insist on authenticity of lineage. He had traveled to China precisely to find a teacher whose transmission was genuine, and he returned with a robe and a certificate of his own. What he took from this was that practice should be linked to a real line of persons who recognized one another, rather than to a self-declared status.

For the reader today the specific object matters less than the principle. A real transmission can be traced, is given by someone who has it, and carries obligations. Be wary of any teaching that gives you the robe without the lineage.

Paraphrased lines
The robe is not a costume. It is the way the teaching is passed hand to hand.
A robe with no lineage behind it is only cloth.

Discernment. Verify the line. Dogen traveled across the sea for exactly this reason.

33DōtokuAble to Speak

Main lessonTo express the way is not to repeat sayings. It is to speak or act from your own realization.

The word dotoku means being able to say, or the expression of the way. In Chan literature a teacher often presses a student to say something about their understanding, and the student’s reply shows how deep it goes. Dogen picks up the idea and makes it central.

His main claim is that dotoku is not the same as speaking. Words are one form of it, but so are silence, a gesture, or the manner of doing a task. What matters is that the expression is alive and comes from realization rather than from memory. He also notes that a teacher’s phrase is not available for borrowing. If you simply repeat what an ancestor said, you have not expressed anything.

He is critical of both extremes. Someone who covers a lack of understanding with a flood of clever phrases is not expressing the way. Someone who takes silence as a pose and hides behind it is not either. Both are avoiding the risk of actually being seen.

For artists and professionals the fascicle has a plain, useful meaning. Your own work is your expression, and you cannot hand it over to a template or to someone else’s phrases. If it is going to be real, it will have to come from what you actually see, and it will have to be tested in the open.

Paraphrased lines
Repeating an ancestor’s words is not expressing the way.
Silence used as a pose is another way of not speaking.

Discernment. Both borrowed eloquence and performed silence are counterfeits. Real expression is risky and your own.

34BukkyōThe Buddha’s Teaching

Main lessonDo not set the sutras against the ancestors. The teaching and the mind that transmits it are one.

A frequent claim in Zen is that it is a special transmission outside the scriptures, not dependent on words. Taken in a loose way, this can become contempt for the teachings of the Buddha, and it leads to the idea that Zen has no need for study. Dogen addresses this directly.

He argues that the Buddha’s teaching, found in the sutras, and the mind of the ancestors, passed from teacher to student, are not two different things. The ancestors who traveled from India to China brought the same dharma that is in the sutras. To say that the sutras and the ancestors point in different directions is to misunderstand both.

This matters to him because it makes Zen continuous with the whole of Buddhism rather than a clever departure from it. A genuine lineage does not need to disown the teachings in order to feel special. It stands in the same line, and it should be able to read the old texts with respect and understanding.

For someone assessing a modern teaching, the fascicle suggests a test. Does the teacher dismiss the traditional sources, or can they read them with intelligence and care? A practice that has to throw out the whole tradition to sustain itself is on weak ground. A practice that stands in the line can afford to read.

Paraphrased lines
The sutras and the ancestors’ mind are not two different dharmas.
Zen is not an escape from the Buddha’s teaching.

Discernment. A teaching that must disown its sources to look special is a red flag. Dogen insists on continuity.

35JinzūSpiritual Powers

Main lessonThe real marvel is ordinary activity done completely. Powers are not the proof of awakening.

Traditional Buddhism lists a set of extraordinary abilities, such as seeing at a distance, reading minds, or remembering past lives, associated with advanced meditation. For many people these powers are what spiritual attainment means. Dogen’s fascicle is a response.

He quotes the layman Pang, who wrote that the marvelous power and wondrous activity of an awakened person is simply drawing water and carrying firewood. Dogen treats this verse as the point of the whole teaching. The real power is ordinary action when it is done wholeheartedly, with nothing held back and nothing left over.

He does not deny that unusual abilities have been reported. He treats them as secondary, and as something that can mislead. A person can have uncanny skills and still be confused, and a person can be completely clear and have none. If you take the powers as evidence of realization, you are looking in the wrong place.

The topic comes up for anyone who has been around spiritual communities. A teacher who points to displays, to special states, or to a reputation for miracles is, by this standard, offering the wrong goods. The fascicle gives you a simple test to apply: if the marvel could not be found in how they live and work every day, be cautious.

Paraphrased lines
Marvelous power and wondrous activity: drawing water and carrying firewood.
A power that is not found in ordinary action is not the buddha’s power.

Discernment. Displays and special abilities are not proof. Dogen sends you back to what is ordinary and verifiable.

36ArakanThe Arhat

Main lessonDo not sneer at other forms of the dharma. Precision about what a term means is a form of honesty.

In some Mahayana circles the arhat, the realized person of the early teachings, was treated as inferior to the bodhisattva. Zen writers sometimes joined in, using arhat as a term for someone who has attained a narrow, self-regarding peace. Dogen disagrees with this and says so in the fascicle.

His point is historical and doctrinal. The ancestors of the Zen line include arhats, and the title means a worthy one, a person who is truly freed. To treat the word as an insult is to misunderstand both the early tradition and the Mahayana. He argues that an arhat is a buddha, in the sense that the realized state is the same thing under a different name.

It is a short and fairly dry fascicle, but it is revealing about his method. He does not accept a convenient caricature of someone else’s teaching, even when the caricature serves his own side. He checks the term against the sources and corrects the habit.

For discernment, that attitude is useful. Easy contempt for another tradition, or for an earlier stage of one’s own, is usually a sign of weak knowledge. The person who can state accurately what the other side holds is the one whose judgments are worth trusting.

Paraphrased lines
An arhat is a worthy one, and the ancestors include arhats.
Do not use a respected title as an insult.

Discernment. Accuracy in naming what you criticize is part of discernment. Caricature is lazy.

37ShunjūSpring and Autumn

Main lessonMeet each season fully. Do not look for a place without cold and heat.

The fascicle turns on a well-known exchange about cold and heat, which in the Chan tradition stands for the pressures of ordinary life. A monk asks the teacher Dongshan how to avoid cold and heat. Dongshan asks why not go where there is no cold and heat. The monk asks where that is. Dongshan says that when it is cold, let the cold kill you, and when it is hot, let the heat kill you.

The reply is blunt and not meant as cruelty. It says that the way to deal with a hard condition is not to escape it but to meet it so completely that you are no longer standing off to the side asking how to get out. The suffering that comes from resisting is added to the cold; the cold alone, fully met, is only cold.

Dogen draws the connection to the changing seasons in the title. Spring and autumn are not metaphors; they are the actual rhythm in which practice happens. The practitioner who waits for the right weather to start never starts.

This is a helpful fascicle for working life. Conditions are seldom ideal, and there is a long tradition of putting off serious effort until things are calmer. Dongshan’s answer closes that exit. It also gently warns against teachings that promise a place free of difficulty, since a place like that does not exist.

Paraphrased lines
When it is cold, let the cold kill you. When it is hot, let the heat kill you.
There is no place without cold and heat. Stop looking for it.

Discernment. Be wary of any teaching that promises a place without difficulty. Dogen points the other way.

38KattōTwining Vines

Main lessonThe relationship between teacher and student is a tangle of transmission, and no part of it is lesser.

Kattō means the vines of wisteria or kudzu that wind around each other. In Zen, the word has often been used to mean entanglement in words, a hindrance to be cut through. Dogen turns the image around and treats the vines as the very medium of transmission. The tangle of teacher and student, of question and answer, of word and word, is how the dharma passes from one person to another.

He builds the fascicle around Bodhidharma’s famous test of four students. The first is told that he has Bodhidharma’s skin, the second his flesh, the third his bones, and the fourth, Huike, his marrow. Many people have read this as a ranking from shallow to deep. Dogen rejects the ranking. Each of the four expressed the whole of Bodhidharma’s teaching, and none expressed less. Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow are not levels. They are all the person.

The point is important for anyone who worries about hierarchy in practice. Dogen is saying that a real teaching does not need a ladder of attainment to be authentic. The idea that some students have deeper understanding than others is often the first step toward a cult of rank.

It is a quietly radical fascicle. It also reads as a warning against any group that relies on grades of insight to keep people in their places.

Paraphrased lines
Skin, flesh, bones, and marrow are not levels. Each one is the whole teaching.
The tangle of teacher and student is the transmission itself.

Discernment. A ladder of attainments is a red flag. Dogen refuses to rank understanding into higher and lower.

39ShishoSuccession Record

Main lessonA lineage should be documented, checkable, and honest. Forgery and vague claims are betrayals.

The shisho is the document that records a succession of dharma from teacher to student. It lists the line of ancestors, usually going back to Shakyamuni, and ends with the name of the person receiving it. It is signed and sealed by the teacher. In Dogen’s day it was the central proof that a person had actually received transmission.

The fascicle describes the document and why it matters. Dogen had seen examples in China, and he explains their form with care: how the names are written, what the language is, how the last name is added. He treats these papers as the concrete expression of an unbroken relationship, not as mere paperwork.

He is also concerned with what happens when the process is abused. A transmission that is bought, invented, or handed out casually damages the whole line. The document is only as honest as the people who write it, and he warns about monks who claim lineages they have not received.

This is one of his plainest discussions of authenticity. It shows how seriously he took the question of who is actually entitled to teach. For a modern reader, the lesson is practical. Ask for the line. Ask who gave transmission and what they were like. Ask whether they are still alive and still willing to be asked. A real succession can bear that kind of questioning.

Paraphrased lines
The succession record is the concrete trace of an unbroken line of persons.
A transmission that is invented or bought breaks the whole line.

Discernment. Dogen treats traceable lineage as a basic test, and treats forgery as a betrayal of everyone in the chain.

40HakujushiThe Cypress Tree

Main lessonThe point is not hidden behind the tree. The cypress tree in the courtyard is the answer.

The famous case: a monk asks the teacher Zhaozhou why the first ancestor came from the west, meaning what is the point of Zen. Zhaozhou says the cypress tree in the courtyard. The monk replies that the teacher should not point to an object in order to answer. Zhaozhou says he is not pointing to an object. The monk asks again, and Zhaozhou says the same thing.

Many readers take the cypress as a trick, a way of blocking an abstract question by directing attention to something concrete. Dogen takes it more seriously. He quotes the story and then comments carefully on how the monk misunderstood. The monk assumed the tree was a symbol of something else, and he wanted to know what was underneath. Zhaozhou’s refusal to point at an object means he is not using the tree as a sign. The tree is the matter.

Dogen also criticizes people who treat the case as a cunning shortcut, or who claim to see what it means without having lived anything like it. The exchange is not a riddle to be solved but a demonstration to be met.

For someone trying to see clearly, the lesson is to look at what is there. The truth is not usually behind the thing you are looking at. It is the thing, and it is shown to anyone who looks.

Paraphrased lines
Why did the first ancestor come from the west? The cypress tree in the courtyard.
Do not turn the tree into a symbol. It is the answer.

Discernment. A clever interpretation is often a way of avoiding the plain fact. Dogen keeps pointing back to it.

41Sangai yuishinThe Three Worlds Are Only Mind

Main lessonMind is not a private screen inside the head. It is the whole world as it is encountered.

A traditional Buddhist phrase says that the three worlds, of desire, form, and formlessness, are only mind. Read quickly, it sounds like idealism, as though the world were a projection of a personal mind that could be changed at will. Dogen explicitly rejects that reading.

For him, mind is not a thing inside a skull producing images. Mind is the whole field of what appears, trees, walls, people, and all. To say that the three worlds are only mind is therefore to say that the world is not outside the one who experiences it, not that it is less real. It is as solid as ever, and the one experiencing it is not a separate observer.

He ties this to earlier fascicles on the old buddha mind and the very mind that is Buddha. All of them make the same point from different directions: that the awakened teaching is not about an interior fact, but about the unity of self and world in practice.

The implication is serious. If the world is mind, then the way you meet it, the quality of attention you bring to your tasks and to other people, is not a private matter. It shapes what exists. That does not make life a dream to be controlled. It makes it a field where responsibility is complete.

Paraphrased lines
The three worlds are only mind, and mind is not inside you. It is the world.
Idealism is not the teaching. The teaching is the unity of self and world.

Discernment. Dogen guards against a familiar misreading that turns a deep teaching into wishful thinking.

42Sesshin sesshōExpounding Mind, Expounding Nature

Main lessonTalk about mind and nature must be tested against actual practice. Without that, it is only talk.

The title refers to the teaching of mind and nature, which was a favorite topic in Chan debates. The question was whether the essence of the Buddha’s teaching lay in a mind or nature underlying all appearances. Some teachers claimed to expound mind and nature as the core of their method, and others dismissed the whole subject as an intellectual trap.

Dogen takes a middle path that is distinctly his own. He accepts that the Buddha’s way has to do with mind and nature but insists on what these words actually mean. Mind is the whole working of the world, in the manner of the old buddha mind. Nature is the actuality of things as they are, not an essence behind them.

He criticizes the habit of presenting mind and nature as a doctrine that people can hold and defend. Such a teaching is already off course, because it turns an experience into a position. The way to expound mind and nature is to practice in a way that shows them, not to lecture about them.

The fascicle is a useful reminder when listening to a skilled speaker on spiritual subjects. Fluent description is not evidence. Look for whether the talk lines up with how the person actually lives and works, and whether they can say plainly what practice they do.

Paraphrased lines
To expound mind and nature is to practice them, not to lecture about them.
A doctrine of mind is not mind.

Discernment. Fluent talk about the deep things is not evidence. Check it against how the person actually lives.

43Shohō jissōThe True Reality of All Dharmas

Main lessonReality is not hidden behind appearances. Each thing, as it is, is the real form.

The title comes from a line in the Lotus Sutra: only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas. It is a famous sentence, and Dogen uses it as a lens for thinking about what is real.

The usual reading sets up a reality behind the shapes of things, which only awakened beings can perceive. Dogen reverses it. For him, the real form of things is not a hidden essence. It is the way the things appear, as they are, just now. A flower blooming is the true reality. A wall standing is the true reality. There is no further layer to go find.

The part about a buddha together with a buddha is important. He means that realization is not a private possession. It happens between people, in a relationship of recognition and testing. You cannot certify your own insight without someone who can see it.

This leads to a clear conclusion about authenticity. The way to verify whether you have seen something is not to be sure of it inwardly but to have it recognized by someone who has seen it too. And what is seen is not a secret but the plain world.

Paraphrased lines
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
The real form is not behind the appearance. It is the appearance, as it is.

Discernment. Realization is mutual. Without someone who can see what you see, certainty is just feeling sure.

44ButsudōThe Way of the Buddha

Main lessonThe Buddha’s way is its own thing. Do not blend it into a vague unity of all teachings, and do not rebrand it.

A pointed and somewhat polemical fascicle. In Dogen’s time, a popular idea in East Asia held that Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism were three expressions of one truth, and that differences among them were superficial. Some Chan teachers promoted this view, and it appealed because it seemed tolerant.

Dogen rejects it strongly. His argument is that the Buddha’s way has its own content that is not interchangeable with the others. To say that all three teach the same thing is to say that you do not understand any of them well enough to see where they differ. It is not breadth; it is vagueness.

He also criticizes the very name Zen school, along with the habit of setting it up as a separate sect that has gone beyond the Buddha’s teaching. For him the way is simply the Buddha’s way, handed on by the line of ancestors. A new label that implies a fresh start only serves to separate it from its roots.

The result is an early statement about authenticity as fidelity. Dogen does not want blending, rebranding, or a smooth synthesis of everything. He wants the actual thing, with its edges, and a clear understanding of where it stands. In modern terms, he is skeptical of any approach that makes spirituality a comfortable mixture of whatever works.

Paraphrased lines
To say the three teachings are one is to understand none of them.
The way is the Buddha’s way, not a new school.

Discernment. A comfortable blend is not depth. Dogen wants the real tradition with its actual edges.

45MitsugoIntimate Words

Main lessonWhat is intimate is not hidden. It is plain, direct, and recognized only by one who is actually in it.

The word mitsugo literally means secret language, and in Chan stories a teacher’s remark may be called an intimate word between teacher and student that others cannot follow. Dogen uses the concept to ask what is meant by a secret in a tradition that claims to hide nothing.

His answer is that the intimacy is real but is not about concealment. A word is secret not because it is kept from others but because it can only be received by someone standing in the right place. A plain remark can pass over the heads of everyone in the room who is still looking for an answer, and land immediately with the one who is not.

The text uses a number of the traditional cases in which a teacher’s words are dismissed by observers but clearly received by the student. Dogen does not try to explain them away, and he does not treat them as occult. They are examples of what happens in genuine encounter, where saying and hearing are one event.

This is a helpful correction for anyone who has encountered spiritual groups that make a mystique out of hidden knowledge. In Dogen’s version, nothing is withheld. If it seems hidden, it is because we are not yet attending to what is plainly shown.

Paraphrased lines
The secret is not kept from you. It is plainly shown, and only recognized by one who is there.
An intimate word needs no mystery. It needs someone listening.

Discernment. A teaching built on hidden knowledge is suspect. Dogen’s secret is the plain thing no one is looking at.

46Mujō seppōThe Insentient Preach the Dharma

Main lessonThe whole world is teaching. Listen with your eyes and your whole body, not only your ears.

The fascicle centers on a story about Dongshan and his teacher’s teacher Yunyan. Dongshan asks who can hear the preaching of the insentient, meaning walls, trees, stones, and the rest of the non-living world. Yunyan answers that the insentient can hear. Dongshan asks if Yunyan himself hears it. Yunyan says that if he did, Dongshan could not hear his own preaching. Dongshan asks why he cannot hear Yunyan. Yunyan raises his whisk and asks whether he hears that. Dongshan says no. Yunyan says Dongshan cannot even hear a human preach, so how could he hear the insentient.

Dongshan later wrote a verse that says, roughly, that it is marvelous, the insentient preaching is inconceivable, and if you listen with your ears you will not understand but if you hear with your eyes you will know.

Dogen takes this as a statement that the teaching is not confined to words and speakers. Every part of the world is expressing the dharma, and what is needed is a way of attending that does not depend on ordinary hearing. He means whole-body attention, in which the listener is not separate from what is heard.

It is a fascicle about receptivity. A person who is closed to anything except explicit speech misses most of what is happening. Learning to notice the way materials, tools, places, and ordinary tasks speak is a core part of the practice, and of any craft.

Paraphrased lines
If you listen with the ears, you will not understand. Hear it with the eyes.
Walls, trees, and stones are all preaching. The task is to be able to listen.

Discernment. Real attention is wider than the obvious channel. Dogen trains you to notice what is actually being said by everything around you.

47ButsukyōThe Buddhas’ Sutras

Main lessonThe sutras belong to the buddhas and ancestors alike. Do not use them as mere props or dismiss them as dead paper.

A companion to the earlier fascicle on the Buddha’s teaching, this one looks at the sutras as the buddhas’ own and asks how they relate to the ancestors’ words. The problem it addresses is the same: a style of Zen that treats the written teachings as cast-off paper, to be quoted when convenient and ignored when not.

Dogen holds that both the sutras and the words of the ancestors are the buddhas’ expression, and that neither can be fully understood without the other. A person who treats the sutras as lifeless has not understood the ancestors, and one who treats the ancestors as having a different message has not understood the sutras.

He also makes a point about the way sutras should be received. They are not simply information. Reading and hearing them is a form of practice, and the reader is to bring the same attention as in sitting. Done right, the sutra changes the reader.

As a way of thinking about sources, the fascicle is helpful. A tradition that knows its own texts and can speak from them is in a stronger position to say what it holds than one that depends only on a charismatic person. Dogen would want you to check both.

Paraphrased lines
The sutras are the buddhas’ own expression, and the ancestors’ words are the same.
Neither worship the page nor throw it away.

Discernment. Check the sources. A teaching that cannot be read against its own texts is resting on personality alone.

48HosshōDharma-Nature

Main lessonDharma-nature is not a hidden essence. It is the way things actually are, right now.

Dharma-nature names the underlying nature of things, a topic that appears in much of Mahayana thought. A common risk, which Dogen sees clearly, is that it comes to be pictured as a substance, a kind of ultimate stuff behind the world of ordinary appearances. Talk of it then becomes a way of saying that what you see is not the real world.

Dogen’s fascicle counters this tendency. He works through sayings of earlier teachers to show that dharma-nature is not behind things but is the actual character of things, appearing as they appear. A mountain’s nature is the mountain. The practitioner who goes looking for something under the surface has missed what is at the surface.

The text is short and mostly works by suggestion. It is related to the longer treatment of Buddha-nature and carries the same message: you do not have a hidden nature that must be found. You are what is happening.

For the reader trying to apply this, it means something like a practice of looking again. When you catch yourself thinking that the real answer is elsewhere, in a better method, a deeper insight, a more advanced teacher, return to the thing in front of you. Dogen would ask whether you have really looked at it.

Paraphrased lines
The nature of a thing is not behind the thing. It is the thing as it is.
Looking for the real elsewhere is how you miss it here.

Discernment. A teaching that always points to something beyond the present is suspect. Dogen points back to what is here.

49DaraniDharani

Main lessonThe holding power of the teaching is not a magic formula. It is what practice does when it is complete.

A dharani is a long incantation of Sanskrit syllables, used in many Buddhist traditions for protection and blessing. Its name carries the sense of holding or retaining, and it was believed to preserve and transmit the power of a teaching. In popular practice it could easily become a kind of spell.

Dogen’s fascicle takes up the subject in a restrained way. He neither condemns the use of dharani nor treats it as magic. His emphasis is on what dharani means at root: the capacity to hold the whole of the teaching in the body and mind, so that it is available in whatever situation arises. In that sense, a person who has practiced completely is a dharani. The practice is what holds.

The point guards against two opposite errors. One is treating ritual formulas as a technology that works regardless of the person who uses them. The other is dismissing ritual altogether as superstition. Dogen asks for something different, a person for whom the formula is an expression of a practice already lived.

It is a useful frame for thinking about any inherited form, whether a chant, a method, or a studio routine. A form that is not backed by practice is empty, and a practice that refuses all forms loses its shape. The two are meant to go together.

Paraphrased lines
To hold the teaching completely is itself dharani.
A formula without practice is empty. Practice without a form has no shape.

Discernment. Ritual is neither a spell nor an embarrassment. It carries weight only when the person using it is actually practicing.

50SenmenWashing the Face

Main lessonEven washing the face is the buddha way. Small acts done with care are not beneath practice.

This is one of the plainest of Dogen’s fascicles and one of the most revealing. It is a manual for morning ablutions in the monastery: how to clean the teeth, rinse the mouth, and wash the face. He cites the early rules of the Buddhist community, the practice of the Buddha himself, and the habits of the Chinese monasteries he had seen.

The details are specific. How to use the water, how to hold the basin, how to avoid waste, how to be quiet and unhurried. The tone is practical and quietly devoted. Dogen treats the cleansing of the body as a sacred act, and this is not exaggeration. In his view the washing of a face is the same practice as sitting, expressed in a different posture.

The fascicle also says a good deal about attitude. Care for the body and for the immediate environment is not separate from care for the dharma. A monk who is sloppy about daily routines is not in a position to claim depth.

The modern equivalent might be that how someone treats the small tasks of their workday, tidying a desk, preparing a file, cleaning a tool, is a better measure of their seriousness than their stated ideals. This fascicle holds that line, with a quiet insistence that nothing is too ordinary to be practice.

Paraphrased lines
Washing the face is not a chore before practice. It is the practice.
How you handle small tasks shows how you handle everything.

Discernment. Dogen tests the claim of depth against the smallest daily habits. If it does not show there, doubt it.

51MenjuFace-to-Face Transmission

Main lessonThe dharma passes from person to person, in person. Books alone cannot certify it.

Written after Dogen had been teaching for some years, this fascicle describes the meaning of face-to-face transmission and recounts how he came to receive it. In Zen the central relationship is between a teacher and a student who actually meet, and the dharma is passed in that meeting. Dogen insists that this is not a figure of speech.

He recounts his own experience with his teacher Rujing, and the sense of something being recognized and confirmed that was not available from books alone. He also speaks of the line of ancestors in terms of meetings, each one standing before the next and being known.

The practical consequence is a statement about authority. If transmission occurs through real meeting, then someone who claims it must have actually met the person they claim to have received it from, and that person must have actually known them. This puts a strong check on self-declared teachers and on claims of lineage that depend on a dream, a vision, or a reading.

Dogen also affirms the importance of a student’s own commitment. A teacher cannot do the work. But the work is not complete without the other person, and no amount of private effort replaces it.

It is a good fascicle to keep in mind when deciding whom to trust. Ask whom they met, and who met them.

Paraphrased lines
The dharma is passed face to face, and nothing replaces the meeting.
A claim of transmission needs a real person who actually knew you.

Discernment. Dogen treats unverifiable claims of private transmission as a basic warning sign.

52BussoBuddhas and Ancestors

Main lessonThe line of ancestors is not past history. Each one is present in practice now.

The fascicle is largely a recitation. It names the seven buddhas of the past, beginning with Vipasyin and ending with Shakyamuni, then the Indian ancestors beginning with Mahakasyapa, and then the Chinese ancestors down to Dogen’s own teacher. The list is long and has a ritual quality, since it was chanted and revered in the monastery.

Dogen’s introduction explains why the list matters. A line of teachers is not a pedigree to be framed. It is a living thing, and the act of reciting the names is a way of meeting the people. To honor the ancestors is to enter the relationship they had with each other and with the dharma.

The fascicle also has a subtler claim. The line is a source of authority precisely because it is made of individuals who had to verify the teaching in their own practice. Each person on the list had their own difficulty and their own realization. The line does not remove the need to practice. It gives practice a context and a standard.

For a modern reader, the text might seem dry. But it illustrates a conviction that runs throughout his work: you do not invent the way yourself. You stand in a line, and you take responsibility for passing it on cleanly.

Paraphrased lines
To recite the names is to meet the people.
The line gives practice a standard, not a shortcut.

Discernment. A teaching that has no line behind it has no one to check it against. Dogen insists on the chain.

53BaikaPlum Blossoms

Main lessonThe plum opens in the cold. Realization appears in hard conditions, not despite them.

The plum blossom, which opens in late winter while snow is still on the ground, is a favorite image in East Asian poetry and in Chan. It stands for beauty that appears in adversity, and for a kind of clear awakening that is not delayed until conditions are comfortable. Dogen draws on this tradition and on the sayings of his teacher Rujing, who was fond of the image.

His treatment is characteristic. The plum blossom is not a metaphor for awakening that is elsewhere. It is the awakening in that form, there in that branch. A single flower in the cold is a complete expression of the teaching, and one who sees it sees the whole.

There is also a connection with Dogen’s own situation. He wrote these fascicles in a period of difficulty, with a small community in remote mountains and little material support. The image of flowering in the cold is also an expression of what he hoped for the practice at Eiheiji.

The lesson for anyone who has put off work waiting for better conditions is direct. The plum does not wait for spring. If the thing is going to be done, it is done in the cold, with what you have, in the time that is actually available.

Paraphrased lines
The plum blossoms in the cold, without waiting for spring.
One flower on a bare branch is the whole teaching.

Discernment. Genuine practice does not need ideal conditions. Be skeptical of any teaching that requires them.

54SenjōPurification

Main lessonEven the toilet is a place for practice. Nothing in life is outside the way.

A companion piece to the fascicle on washing the face, this one deals with a subject that most religious writing avoids: how to use the toilet and clean yourself afterward. Dogen explains the procedures in detail, including how to prepare, how to wash with water, how to wash the hands, and the order of each step. He draws on the early Buddhist rules and on Chinese monastic practice.

It is easy to be amused by this, but Dogen was serious. The fascicle makes a direct claim that there is no part of life outside practice. If the body is a place where awakening happens, then the actions of the body, including the least dignified, deserve attention. A tradition that can talk about emptiness but not about hygiene has missed something.

There is also an implicit statement about honesty. The most private and least glamorous parts of life are where a person’s character is revealed. Someone who is careful only in public has not integrated their practice.

The same applies in professional life. The quality of work is often shown in unseen details: the back of a drawing, the file nobody opens, the part of the job that will not be praised. Dogen holds that what you do when no one is watching is the real measure.

Paraphrased lines
There is no part of life outside the way, including the toilet.
What you do unseen is the real test.

Discernment. Integrity shows in the unglamorous details. Dogen refuses to let practice live only in the dignified parts.

55JippōThe Ten Directions

Main lessonThe whole world is here, where you stand. Do not look for the teaching somewhere far off.

The ten directions are the four cardinal points, the four intermediate points, up, and down, which together stand for the whole universe. In Buddhist sources they often appear when speaking of the buddhas of the ten directions, who are everywhere. The expression suggests vastness.

Dogen’s use of the phrase works against the sense of distance. For him the ten directions are not a faraway horizon but the place where you are standing, with every direction opening out from it. To say that the dharma fills the ten directions is to say that it is fully present at this spot. There is no need to go looking.

He supports this with sayings of earlier teachers and with the logic that runs through his work: nothing is lacking, and no thing is a lesser version of something else. A single place is the whole world at that spot, as the moon in a drop is the whole moon.

The practical effect is steadying. A person who is always looking toward a bigger, better, more advanced situation is, in Dogen’s view, discounting the present. The ten directions begin here. It is a good reminder to anyone who feels that serious work happens elsewhere, in another city, with other people, once everything is in place.

Paraphrased lines
The ten directions are not far away. They open out from where you stand.
Nothing is lacking at this spot.

Discernment. A teaching that always sends you elsewhere should be questioned. Dogen keeps returning to the place you are.

56KenbutsuSeeing the Buddha

Main lessonTo see the Buddha is not to see a figure. It is to see forms as they are and to practice.

A line in the Diamond Sutra says that if you see all forms as non-forms, you see the Tathagata. It is often read as an instruction to look through the world to something behind it. Dogen takes up the question of what it means to see the Buddha and treats it carefully.

His answer moves away from visions and extraordinary experiences. Seeing the Buddha is not a matter of having an apparition appear. Many people in the tradition have claimed such visions, and he does not dismiss them outright, but he says that the real seeing is the quiet recognition of how things are, in the course of ordinary practice. The Buddha is seen in the one who practices, including yourself.

He also stresses reverence. Seeing a buddha is an occasion for respect, and the right response is to bow and to practice, not to describe or to collect the experience. A vision that leaves you feeling special has gone wrong somewhere.

It is a useful perspective on any spiritual claim based on a special experience. The test, in Dogen’s terms, is what the experience does to your way of living. If it deepens care and attention, it may be real. If it produces pride, it is a distraction, however vivid.

Paraphrased lines
To see the Buddha is not to see a figure. It is to see forms as they are.
A vision that makes you feel special has gone wrong.

Discernment. Dogen is cautious about special experiences. What matters is whether they deepen care or inflate pride.

57HenzanExtensive Study

Main lessonTravel and study with total commitment, and look for a real teacher rather than a famous one.

The word henzan means to visit many teachers and study widely. In the Chinese Chan tradition, a monk would often travel from monastery to monastery, testing teachers and being tested. Dogen did exactly this when he was in China, and the fascicle reflects what he learned.

His main point is that extensive study is not tourism. A monk who wanders from place to place collecting impressions is not studying. Real study is complete engagement with whatever is in front of you, so that even a short stay can be thorough, and a long journey is a series of full encounters.

He is also clear about the object of the search. A student should look for a true teacher, someone who has actually realized the dharma and can transmit it. Fame, size of the community, and impressive buildings are not reliable guides. In his own travels he found many prominent teachers who did not meet his standard, and one, Rujing, who did.

The fascicle belongs with those that treat discernment as a skill. Choosing whom to learn from is among the most important decisions a person makes. It should be done slowly, with attention to what a teacher actually does, and without being dazzled by reputation.

Paraphrased lines
Extensive study is not collecting impressions. It is full engagement with each encounter.
Look for a true teacher, not a famous one.

Discernment. Dogen’s own travels are the model: visit widely, test carefully, and do not mistake reputation for realization.

58GanzeiThe Eye

Main lessonThe eye of the buddhas is not an organ. It is the whole of a practicing life seeing clearly.

The title means the pupil of the eye, and the fascicle deals with the Buddha’s eye, the eye of awakened vision. In Zen literature the eye is often a metaphor for the capacity to see what is real, as in the phrase for the Shobogenzo itself, the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye.

Dogen’s treatment avoids the standard picture of a special inner faculty. He works with old sayings to show that the eye is the whole body in action, and that to speak of seeing is to speak of everything the practitioner does. An eye that is not backed by conduct is not the Buddha’s eye, however sharp it may seem.

He has a particular interest in the way an authentic eye can tell the difference between truth and imitation. A person who has it recognizes a real teacher, a real teaching, and a real practice, and also notices when something is off. It is not a matter of being suspicious. It is a matter of being clear.

For those who work with images, this is a good fascicle. The eye that discerns what is real and what is decorative is trained slowly, by looking and by practice. It is not given by talent alone, and it is not independent of how you live.

Paraphrased lines
The eye is not an organ. It is the whole body seeing clearly.
An eye that cannot tell the real from the imitation is not yet the true eye.

Discernment. Discernment is a trained capacity, not a mood. Dogen ties it to the whole of a person’s practice.

59KajōEveryday Matters

Main lessonTea and rice, plain and daily, are the buddha way. Do not look past the ordinary.

Kajō is a term for the ordinary round of home life: a meal, a cup of tea, the daily tasks that make up a household. In Chan, a famous teacher is said to have responded to nearly every question with something as plain as an invitation to have tea. The suggestion is that the way is not an exotic place but the everyday.

Dogen develops this theme with some force. He argues that people who look for the Buddha’s way in remarkable experiences and dramatic events overlook the one place where it can be seen without effort, the ordinary activity of eating, drinking, working, and resting. Tea and rice are not lesser than visions. They are the full presence of the way.

The text is also a gentle criticism of a tendency to romanticize monastic life. A monastery is a place of daily routine, and what makes it a place of practice is the manner in which the routine is carried out. It is not that the ordinary is a means to the sacred. It is that the ordinary, done completely, is what the sacred looks like.

For people trying to integrate practice with a job and a creative life, this is a helpful statement. You do not have to find a special container for it. The tasks you already have are the place.

Paraphrased lines
Tea and rice, simple and daily, are the buddha way.
Do not look past the ordinary for something better.

Discernment. A teaching that disdains daily life and promises something rarer is suspect. Dogen sends you back to the table.

60Sanjūshichihon bodai bunpōThe Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening

Main lessonThe old Buddhist path is not a lesser way. Dogen puts the Buddha’s own practices at the center of Zen.

This is the most detailed treatment in the Shobogenzo of the basic Buddhist teachings. The thirty-seven factors are an early list of practices that lead to awakening, drawn from the oldest sources. They include the four foundations of mindfulness, in which one pays attention to the body, to feelings, to mind, and to the objects of mind; the four right efforts; the four bases of spiritual power; the five faculties; the five powers; the seven factors of awakening; and the eightfold path.

Dogen goes through each group and comments on it. He treats them not as beginner’s material to be left behind but as the very substance of the buddhas’ teaching, and as the content of the Treasury of the True Dharma Eye itself. In his view, they are continuous with sitting, and they are practiced fully by those who sit.

The effect is to place Zen squarely within the Buddha’s own teaching and to refuse the idea that it is a separate, higher path that no longer needs the basics. There is a kind of insistence in this: the foundations are not outgrown.

It is a valuable text for readers who want a check on modern teachings. If a teacher claims to be beyond the basics of mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom, this fascicle says that nobody is. Real depth includes these things and does not depart from them.

Paraphrased lines
These thirty-seven are not a lesser path. They are the treasury of the true dharma eye.
No one outgrows mindfulness, effort, ethics, and wisdom.

Discernment. A teaching that leaves the basics behind has left the Buddha behind. Dogen grounds Zen in the earliest practices.

61RyūginSong of the Dragon

Main lessonWhat looks dead can sing. When the grasping stops, vitality appears.

The title comes from a saying attributed to the teacher Xiangyan, who was asked about the way and answered that a dragon sings in a withered tree. The phrase combines an image of lifeless wood with a mythic creature whose voice is the thunder.

Dogen reads the withered tree as the state of a person who has dropped attachments, a body and mind that are no longer pushing in any direction. It looks like nothing from outside, dry and unadorned. But from that condition a voice arises that is vast and alive. The song is not made by effort. It is what the tree does when it is simply there.

He combines the image with other sayings that use dead wood and cold ashes to describe the silent, unmoving mind. He is careful, however, to separate this from a state of deadness. A meditation in which nothing happens and nothing is felt is not what he means. The tree is dry, but the dragon is singing.

It is an encouraging image for anyone who has felt that practice has gone flat. The apparent barrenness may be the condition for something. The task is not to push it into color but to let it be what it is and listen.

Paraphrased lines
A dragon sings in a withered tree.
What looks like nothing is not nothing. The song is there.

Discernment. Dogen separates real stillness from dull numbness, which are easy to confuse. Real stillness has life in it.

62Soshi seirai iThe Intention of the Ancestor’s Coming from the West

Main lessonThe point of Zen is not a hidden doctrine. It is the plain actuality shown in each answer.

The question of why Bodhidharma came from India to China is the most famous question in Chan. It is a way of asking for the point of the whole teaching. Teachers gave many different answers, and the answers became cases for study.

Dogen collects a number of these and examines them. His repeated claim is that the intention of the ancestor is not a separate message that these answers point toward. Each answer, from the cypress tree in the garden to something even more ordinary, is itself the intention. The ancestor came to show what is already present in front of everyone.

The fascicle also pushes back against the habit of treating these answers as clever tricks, as if the aim were to produce surprising statements. The answers are not performances. They are what the person saw at that moment, and they work because the questioner was meant to look at the thing, not to puzzle over the reply.

As a text on discernment, it is quietly demanding. It asks you to notice when a teacher is giving something real and when they are putting on a show of being enigmatic. The real thing is not obscure. It is plain and often unexpected, and it does not need a lot of staging.

Paraphrased lines
The ancestor came to show what is already in front of everyone.
A cryptic answer is not deeper than a plain one.

Discernment. Mystification is a common counterfeit of depth. Dogen’s answers are plain.

63Hotsu mujō shinRaising the Supreme Mind

Main lessonThe vow to benefit others before yourself is the supreme mind. It begins with a decision, and anyone can make it.

The supreme mind is the aspiration for complete awakening for the sake of all beings, known in Sanskrit as bodhicitta. In Mahayana it is the beginning and the root of the whole path, and the point where a person takes up the way for something larger than their own comfort or advantage.

Dogen’s fascicle discusses what it means to raise this mind and why it matters. He emphasizes that it is not reserved for an elite. It can occur in an ordinary person, in unlikely circumstances, and it does not depend on prior achievement. What matters is that it is a real turning, a decision that changes what you are working for.

He also draws attention to the difference between the aspiration and the pursuit of reputation. Many people take up practice for the sake of status, admiration, or an interesting identity. Dogen notes that this motive is common and that it poisons the work. The vow to help others is a corrective, because it points away from the self.

For professional and creative life, the lesson is that the purpose behind the work matters. A craft done for the sake of praise tends to bend toward what is praised. A craft done for the sake of something beyond oneself tends to steady.

Paraphrased lines
To raise the supreme mind is to vow to help others cross before oneself.
Anyone can make this turn, regardless of position.

Discernment. Check the motive. Practice taken up for reputation is already off course, and Dogen says so plainly.

64Udon geThe Udumbara Flower

Main lessonThe flower is not a symbol of the transmission. It is the transmission, as it actually happened.

The udumbara is a legendary flower that blooms only once in a very long time. In the story that stands behind Zen’s claim of a special transmission, the Buddha held up a flower before a gathering, and no one responded except Mahakasyapa, who smiled. The Buddha then said that he had the treasury of the true dharma eye and entrusted it to Mahakasyapa.

Dogen takes up this story and refuses the standard interpretation, in which the flower is a gesture that stands for a silent teaching beyond words. For him the holding up of the flower and the smile are the teaching, fully expressed and received. It is not a code to be decoded.

He also stresses that the story is not about a magic communication between two special people. The transmission is carried in ordinary activity, and the flower is a flower. The man holding it and the man smiling are doing what they do, and the line starts there.

The fascicle belongs with those that guard against turning Zen into a story of mystical elitism. The tradition is not a secret club with special signals. It is a line of people who have shown, in plain actions, what the teaching is. For someone sifting through claims of hidden knowledge, it is a helpful corrective.

Paraphrased lines
The flower held up is not a symbol. It is the teaching, fully expressed.
The line begins with an ordinary act, plainly done.

Discernment. Dogen refuses a mystical elitism around the tradition’s own founding story.

65Nyorai zenshinThe Whole Body of the Tathagata

Main lessonThe Buddha’s real body is the teaching, which includes the scrolls and the one who practices it.

A phrase in the Lotus Sutra speaks of the Tathagata’s relics, and in some places the sutras themselves are described as the whole body of the Tathagata. Dogen takes up this claim and works through what it means.

In an earlier tradition the Buddha’s remains were enshrined in stupas, and reverence was directed to the physical relics. Mahayana moved the center of reverence to the teaching. The body of the Buddha is the dharma, and the sutra scrolls are its form. Dogen goes a step further by saying that the whole body of the Buddha is not limited to written texts. It is present wherever the teaching is practiced.

This has a consequence for how to treat both texts and practitioners. A scroll is to be honored, but so is a person who is living the teaching, since that person is also the body of the Buddha. The two are not different in kind.

The fascicle ends up being a reflection on what is worth revering. Not a relic as an object of superstition, not a text as a prop, but the living teaching as it appears in action. It is a useful way of sorting what in a tradition is substance and what is decoration, and of recognizing that some of the most authentic things are the least showy.

Paraphrased lines
The sutra scrolls are the whole body of the Tathagata.
The living practice is also the Buddha’s body.

Discernment. Reverence should be directed at the living teaching, not at objects or reputations.

66Zanmai ō zanmaiThe King of Samadhis Samadhi

Main lessonSitting upright is the king of all concentrations. Nothing replaces it, and nothing is above it.

In this fascicle Dogen makes his most emphatic claim about zazen. Sitting with crossed legs, he says, is the king of samadhis, the practice that contains all others. The whole body, in that posture, is the form of the buddhas and ancestors.

He goes through the traditional language for this posture, including the old description that the cross-legged form is the skin, flesh, bones, and marrow of the Buddha. In his reading, to sit is to express the entire teaching. There is nothing further that sitting is aiming toward.

He is also pointed about those who treat the posture as optional. He quotes teachers who emphasized sitting, and he criticizes those who say that real Zen can dispense with it, or who replace it with something more exciting. He treats the loss of the sitting posture as a loss of the buddha way itself.

It is a strong text, and it reflects his conviction that the practice he brought from China was complete and did not need to be supplemented. For anyone who has seen sitting dismissed as a beginner’s tool, it says the opposite. It is the one practice that does not become obsolete. Everything else, including study, work, and art, rests on it.

Paraphrased lines
Sitting cross-legged is the king of samadhis.
To lose the sitting posture is to lose the buddha way.

Discernment. Dogen is blunt about practices that claim to replace the core. The simple thing remains the center.

67Ten hōrinTurning the Dharma Wheel

Main lessonThe wheel is turning now, wherever the teaching is practiced. You are both turning it and turned by it.

Turning the dharma wheel is the traditional description of the Buddha’s first teaching, delivered to five ascetics at Deer Park. The wheel is the teaching, and its turning is the beginning of a continuing activity.

Dogen reads the image as a description of an event that is not finished. The wheel is not a historical act. It is turning whenever the dharma is practiced, taught, or heard. The person who sits down to practice is turning the wheel, and the wheel is also turning that person.

The emphasis on mutuality is typical. A teaching is not an object that you pick up and use. It acts on you as you act with it, and the line between the one who uses it and the one who is used becomes unclear. This is the same relation as in the boat that carries the rider who is steering.

The fascicle is brief and works as a gentle reminder to approach the teaching with a sense of participating, not owning. It is not a possession or a credential. Something is already moving, and the practitioner joins it. For someone feeling that their work or practice is entirely their own project, the image of a wheel that includes them can be a relief.

Paraphrased lines
The wheel is turning wherever the dharma is practiced.
You turn the wheel, and the wheel turns you.

Discernment. A teaching you own outright is not the teaching. Dogen keeps you inside something larger.

68Dai shugyōGreat Practice

Main lessonEven the greatly awakened do not escape cause and effect. Claiming otherwise is a serious mistake.

The fascicle retells the old story of Baizhang and the fox. After a lecture, an old man who regularly came to listen stayed behind. He explained that long ago he had been a teacher on that mountain. A student had asked him whether a greatly practiced person falls into cause and effect, and he had answered that such a person does not fall into cause and effect. For that answer he had been reborn as a fox for five hundred lifetimes. He asked Baizhang to answer the question properly. Baizhang said that such a person does not obscure cause and effect. The old man was released.

Dogen examines the two phrases, not falling into and not obscuring, with great care. The first suggests that realization places you outside the law of consequences. The second says that realization sees consequence clearly and does not hide from it. He holds that the difference between them is the difference between a delusion and the truth.

What emerges is a firm statement that practice does not lift anyone above the consequences of their actions. Cause and effect is the structure of the world, and an awakened person lives in it with open eyes. Great practice is not outside this. It is a way of living it fully.

This is among his clearest teachings on discernment. Anyone who claims their realization exempts them from accountability is the old man before his release.

Paraphrased lines
Does a greatly practiced person fall into cause and effect? Such a person does not obscure cause and effect.
Claiming to stand outside consequence is the fox’s mistake.

Discernment. The most direct test of a claim to realization: does it accept consequence and accountability?

69Jishō zanmaiThe Samadhi of Self-Verification

Main lessonVerification is not private certainty. It is confirmed by the dharma and by a real teacher.

Self-verification, in Dogen’s usage, does not mean what it might seem to mean. It is not the experience of being convinced inwardly of your own awakening. The self in question is not a private subject sitting in judgment on itself. It is the self verified by all things, in the manner of the Genjokoan, where letting the ten thousand things come forward confirms the self.

He argues that real verification takes place in practice and is then recognized by a teacher who has had the same experience. A person who says that they have verified themselves, without reference to a line or to any outside check, has misunderstood the term.

The fascicle includes a note of warning about a kind of self-assured individualism that was already present in some circles. Some people took the language of direct realization as license to bypass teachers and traditions. Dogen sees this as a risk and responds by tying verification firmly to relationship.

For present readers, the distinction between feeling certain and being verified is a practical one. A feeling of certainty is easy to have and is a poor indicator. It is the kind of thing that a skilled teacher, a careful reading of the tradition, and an honest look at one’s own conduct can test. This fascicle encourages submitting yourself to that test.

Paraphrased lines
Self-verification is not self-certification.
Feeling certain is not the same as being verified.

Discernment. Private certainty is cheap. Dogen insists on confirmation by the dharma and by someone who has actually seen it.

70KokūEmpty Space

Main lessonSpace is not an absence. It is the room in which everything happens, and it is itself a way the teaching shows up.

The word kokū means empty space or sky, and in Buddhist writing it is often used for the open expanse that contains all things. It can suggest the void, and in some teachings it stands for a negation, an absence of everything.

Dogen does not accept the negative reading. For him space is as real as a mountain, and it is part of the teaching. The sky is not a nothing between things. It is the openness that lets things be what they are. He draws on Chan sayings in which space is spoken of as a body, with its own function.

He also connects space with practice. A person who sits well is spacious, in the sense of not being filled up with agendas and opinions. That openness is not a vacancy. It is a capacity to receive what comes without distortion.

There is a lesson for any kind of work that requires attention. Leaving room, in a layout, in a schedule, in a conversation, is not wasted. It is what allows the other parts to work. A cramped mind, like a crowded page, cannot see. The fascicle encourages an appreciation of space as active, not empty.

Paraphrased lines
Space is not a nothing between things. It is the room in which things are.
An open mind is not vacant. It is available.

Discernment. Emptiness is not nihilism. Dogen makes space a positive condition of seeing clearly.

71HouThe Almsbowl

Main lessonThe bowl is the buddha’s body. Use the genuine form with care, not a convenient substitute.

The almsbowl is the container a monk uses for food, and from the beginning of Buddhism it has been one of the monk’s few possessions. Dogen’s fascicle is about its history, meaning, and use. He traces the bowl back to the Buddha and to the transmission from Mahakasyapa.

The fascicle includes practical details on the shape, material, and size of the proper bowl, and on how the nested set should be arranged. Dogen had visited Chinese monasteries and had seen the way the bowl was used. He also knew that in many places the proper form was being neglected or mixed with other styles. His response is firm. He insists on the traditional bowl and on using it as the buddhas and ancestors did.

His reasoning is that the form carries the teaching. A bowl is not just a utensil. It is a link with a line of people who used the same vessel in the same way. Substituting a different form to be more convenient or more fashionable cuts that link.

There is some tension in his stance, and a modern reader may wonder how much of this is essential. Still, the underlying point is a useful one for discernment. A tradition that is sloppy about its own forms, or that adapts them to suit marketing, has to explain what it is keeping and what it is giving up. Dogen asks for that kind of care.

Paraphrased lines
The bowl is the body of the buddhas and ancestors.
A convenient substitute breaks the link with the line.

Discernment. Dogen holds the forms of the tradition to exact standards. Casual substitution is how a lineage quietly drifts.

72AngoThe Practice Period

Main lessonCommit to a fixed container of practice and stay inside it. The container does the work.

Ango, the practice period, is a retreat of about three months in which the monks remain in the monastery and devote themselves to training. It goes back to the rainy season in India, when monks stopped wandering and stayed in one place. In China and Japan it became a fixed part of the year.

Dogen’s fascicle gives the history and the rules. He explains the dates, the purpose, and the obligations. A monk who enters the practice period commits to staying and to following the schedule. Leaving early is a serious matter.

There is a deeper idea in the text, which is that the container itself is part of the practice. A fixed period, a fixed place, and a fixed schedule remove the daily question of whether to practice today. The choice has been made, and the practice is what is left. Dogen treats this as a gift, not a restriction.

It is a model that holds up outside a monastery. Many people who want to build a practice, or a body of work, fail because the daily decision is renegotiated every morning. A fixed period with clear boundaries changes the situation. For someone with creative or professional goals, the structure is the practical lesson, whatever one thinks of the religious context.

Paraphrased lines
Within a fixed period, the question of whether to practice today is already answered.
The container does part of the work.

Discernment. A tradition with real forms of commitment is easier to trust than one that offers only inspiration.

73Tashin tsūPenetration of Other Minds

Main lessonMind-reading powers prove nothing. A genuine teacher cannot be found out by a trick.

The fascicle is built around a well-known story about the National Teacher Huizhong and an Indian monk named Daer Sanzang, who was said to have the power to read minds. The emperor invited the visitor to the capital, and the National Teacher came to test him.

The first time, Huizhong asked where he was, and the visitor said he was at the West River boat. The second time, he asked again, and the visitor said he was on the bridge, watching a monkey play. The third time Huizhong asked, the visitor could not find him. The National Teacher then said that this fox spirit, who claimed to know the mind of others, did not know where he was.

Dogen takes up the case to say what the story actually tests. A power to read what is in the minds of others, he says, can only find a mind that is somewhere. It cannot find a mind that is not fixed anywhere. The third time Huizhong was not found because he had no position to be located. The test shows that a powerful trick is not the same as understanding.

It fits with the fascicle on spiritual powers. For an observer of teachers, the practical conclusion is that impressive displays are never the test, and a person who can do a trick may be less clear than someone who can do none.

Paraphrased lines
The third time, the mind-reader could not find the teacher. There was no place to find.
A power to read minds is not wisdom.

Discernment. Dogen shows that a spectacular ability can be completely out-seen by an ordinary clarity.

74Ō saku sendabaThe King Asks for Saindhava

Main lessonDiscernment is responding rightly to what is actually meant in this moment, not following a rule.

The word saindhava has several meanings in Sanskrit: salt, water, a bowl, or a horse. The story is about a king who had an attendant clever enough to know which meaning the king intended at any moment. When the king was washing his hands, he said saindhava, and the attendant brought water. When the king sat down to eat, he said saindhava and the attendant brought salt. After the meal, when the king said saindhava, the attendant brought a cup. When the king was about to go out, he said it, and the attendant brought his horse.

Dogen picks up the story as a model of right response. The attendant did not have a rule. He knew the situation, read the person, and gave exactly what was needed. For Dogen this is a picture of what a practitioner is supposed to do: see the actual circumstances and act accordingly.

The fascicle makes several points about teaching and learning. A teacher’s words have to be received in context, and an attendant who answered with the wrong item would be foolish no matter how knowledgeable. Equally, a student has to learn to read the situation and not hang on to a single meaning.

It is a quiet text about discernment and judgment. Many fields have rules. The skill that makes the difference is knowing what the moment calls for.

Paraphrased lines
The king said saindhava. The wise attendant brought water, salt, a cup, or the horse, as the moment required.
Knowing what is needed is not a matter of knowing a rule.

Discernment. Discernment is the skill of reading the actual situation. No formula substitutes for it.

75ShukkeLeaving Home

Main lessonLeaving home is the beginning of practice, and it means leaving the habits of the self as well as a house.

In the Buddhist tradition, leaving home means taking up the life of a monk or nun. The Buddha himself left his family and his palace to seek awakening, and the act has been the model for those who follow.

Dogen’s fascicle on the subject is partly a defense of monastic life and partly an examination of what it means. He argues that leaving home is the fundamental act of the buddhas and ancestors, the thing they all did. He stresses that it is not an escape from responsibilities but a way of taking up a larger one.

He also makes it clear that the outer act is not enough. A person can leave home in the outward sense and remain tied to every habit and ambition that home represented. The real departure is internal, a letting go of the picture of oneself as someone who has to protect and enlarge a position. Without this, the robe and the shaved head are costume.

For readers who are not monastics, the fascicle can still speak to an experience of turning toward practice and giving it priority. The question it asks is whether this has actually changed what you are living for. The outward forms matter, but they are meant to serve something that has to be done in the mind and in conduct.

Paraphrased lines
Leaving home is the act of all the buddhas and ancestors.
A robe worn over unchanged ambitions is only a costume.

Discernment. The outward form is not the proof. Dogen asks whether the inward turn has actually happened.

Part two: the late twelve

The collection he was still writing near the end of his life, ending with his last fascicle.

01Shukke kudokuThe Merit of Leaving Home

Main lessonCommitment is what makes practice real. A serious turn has to cost something and has to be formal.

Among the last texts Dogen wrote, this fascicle praises the act of ordination, the formal step from lay life into the monastic path. He gathers statements from the sutras and from the ancestors about the great benefit that comes from leaving home and holding the precepts, and he states plainly that the merit is greater than any ordinary good.

It would be easy to read this as a simple recommendation of monasticism, but its main concern is about seriousness. Dogen watched many people treat practice as an interest, something to be fitted around a life that was otherwise unchanged. His late writing is a reminder of what he thought the way required: a decision, a vow, and a form that makes the decision public and hard to retract.

He also tries to dispel the notion that the outer act is an empty ritual. In his view, the form and the intention support each other. The ceremony stands for a commitment that has already begun in the heart, and the commitment is strengthened by being made concrete.

For people who do not become monastics, there is still something useful here. Whatever you take up seriously, a craft, a practice, a profession, benefits from a point at which you commit in a way that others can see. Vague intention rarely lasts. A clear step, with consequences, tends to.

Paraphrased lines
The merit of leaving home cannot be matched by ordinary good deeds.
A vow made in the open is harder to abandon than a private wish.

Discernment. Dogen distrusts casual interest. In his late work the test of seriousness is a commitment you cannot easily undo.

02JukaiReceiving the Precepts

Main lessonThe precepts are the way the awakened live, taken up by a decision and a ceremony, and renewed daily.

Another of Dogen’s late texts, this one describes the formal reception of the sixteen bodhisattva precepts. These are the three refuges in Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha; the three pure precepts, which are to avoid evil, to do good, and to benefit all beings; and the ten grave precepts. The grave precepts forbid killing, stealing, misusing sexuality, lying, dealing in intoxicants, speaking of others’ faults, praising oneself while criticizing others, stinginess, anger, and disparaging the three treasures.

Dogen presents the reception of the precepts as entry into the Buddha’s way. They are not a rulebook that arrives from outside. They are the content of awakened conduct, handed on from the buddhas and made one’s own through the ceremony. He also stresses repentance as part of the process, an honest acknowledgment of what has been done wrong.

He is careful about the manner in which this is done. The ceremony needs a proper teacher who has himself received the precepts in an unbroken line, and a sincere student. A casual or commercial handing out of precepts does not count.

The fascicle ties together a good deal of what he says about authenticity. Ethics is not decoration, it is the shape of practice, and it must be transmitted by someone who can be held to the same standard.

Paraphrased lines
To receive the precepts is to enter the ranks of the buddhas.
Precepts handed out casually are not precepts.

Discernment. The ethical core of the tradition is not optional. Dogen links it to a transmission that can be traced and a teacher who is accountable.

03Kesa kudokuThe Merit of the Robe

Main lessonThe robe is not decoration. Make it and wear it correctly, and treat the form as part of the teaching.

Dogen’s long essay on the kasaya, the Buddhist robe, is one of the more extended treatments in the whole work. He explains its origins, its meaning, how it should be made, and why it deserves reverence. The robe is described as a patchwork garment made from cast-off cloth, a deliberate rejection of luxury, and it was the mark of one who had left home.

He insists on the correct form. The pattern of the stitching, the choice of cloth, and the manner of wearing are all part of what makes the robe what it is. He criticizes the mixing of styles and the substitution of fancier or more convenient materials, which he sees as a loss of the tradition’s meaning.

There is also a strong note of devotion. Dogen cites many accounts of the power of the robe and of the merit that comes from making, wearing, and honoring it. Some of these stories are miraculous, and a modern reader may take them as expressions of reverence rather than literal claims.

The theme that matters for discernment is fidelity. A tradition’s forms are not arbitrary, and abandoning them for taste or marketing has consequences. Dogen’s severity on this point is hard to miss. It can feel excessive, but it comes from a consistent conviction that the way is carried in the details, not only in the ideas.

Paraphrased lines
The robe is the buddhas’ own garment. Make it and wear it as they did.
Changing the form to suit convenience loses what the form carries.

Discernment. Dogen is strict about form because he treats fidelity as a test of seriousness.

04Hotsu bodai shinArousing the Aspiration for Awakening

Main lessonThe beginning of the path is the vow to awaken for the sake of others. Without it, practice drifts into self-interest.

The aspiration for awakening, bodhicitta, is the starting point of the bodhisattva path. Dogen takes it up in a late fascicle that seems directed to those inside the monastery as much as to a broader audience.

His central claim is that the aspiration is more than a feeling of inspiration. It is a commitment to awaken in order to help all beings, and it requires a willingness to put others first. He warns against taking up the practice for personal gain: reputation, comfort, even the satisfaction of a spiritual identity. Those motives are common and are corrosive.

He also encourages. The aspiration can be raised by anyone, in any condition. It can arise when a person is sick, ashamed, or distracted. It does not require a clean record or a stable situation. The point is that it is renewed whenever it is remembered, and it can be raised again after being forgotten.

It is a sober and warm text together. The sobriety is in the insistence on purpose. The warmth is in the assurance that anyone may start. For people in demanding work, the question it poses is simple and useful: who is this for? Dogen suggests that the answer shapes the quality of everything that follows.

Paraphrased lines
Awaken for the sake of others, and do not seek fame or profit by it.
The aspiration can be raised again at any time.

Discernment. Practice for reputation is already off course. The check is the purpose behind the effort.

05Kuyō shobutsuMaking Offerings to the Buddhas

Main lessonOffering is a practice of generosity. A sincere small gift counts for more than an extravagant empty one.

To make offerings is to present incense, flowers, light, food, and other gifts to the buddhas, and by extension to teachers and the community. Dogen gives it serious attention in a late fascicle that gathers stories and sayings about the merit of the practice.

He is clear that the value of the offering is not in the cost. A handful of grass, a bowl of water, or a single flower offered in good faith is complete. An elaborate offering made out of vanity or the desire to be seen is empty. The act is a way of training the mind away from stinginess and toward giving.

He also ties the practice to reverence for the Triple Treasure and for the living line of teachers. To make an offering is to recognize that one has received something. It is a counter to the habit of treating teaching and tradition as a service one pays for and then owns.

For readers outside a monastery, the point can translate into a general principle about generosity in work and relationships. Giving without a hidden bill, doing something carefully for its own sake, and acknowledging what you have been given are all forms of the same practice. Dogen treats them as part of the way, not as extras.

Paraphrased lines
A single flower offered sincerely is complete.
An offering made to be seen is only a display.

Discernment. Sincerity is the test. Dogen measures an act by the heart behind it, not by its size or visibility.

06Kie buppōsōbōTaking Refuge in the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha

Main lessonRefuge is the doorway. Without it, nothing else in the way stands on solid ground.

Taking refuge in the Three Treasures, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, is the formal act that marks someone as a Buddhist. It is the first of the precepts and the first thing a student does in the ordination ceremony.

Dogen’s late fascicle speaks of the practice with great seriousness. He cites teachers and sutras to show that taking refuge is no small matter. It is the point at which a person turns from taking shelter in worldly securities, reputation, wealth, power, to relying on the way that the buddhas walked. He also stresses that those who take refuge sincerely are met by the power of the Three Treasures.

An important point is that he treats the three as objects of genuine respect. The Buddha is not an abstract ideal. The Dharma is not a set of ideas. The Sangha is not a social club. Each is a living reality, and the community, with all its flaws, is part of the refuge.

For those thinking about what to rely on, the fascicle is a plain statement. Everyone relies on something. The question is whether what you rely on will hold. Dogen answers for the Triple Treasure, and the answer is the same as in everything else he wrote: the way that has been proven, by a line of people, over time.

Paraphrased lines
To take refuge is to turn from shaky securities to the way the buddhas walked.
The community, with its flaws, is part of the refuge.

Discernment. Everyone relies on something. Dogen asks what has actually held up over time.

07Jinshin ingaDeep Faith in Cause and Effect

Main lessonDo not deny cause and effect. Those who do are not in the buddha way, however advanced they seem.

Cause and effect, in Buddhist terms, means that actions have consequences, and that this is part of the structure of the world. In some Chan circles of Dogen’s day, the language of emptiness and non-attachment was used to suggest that the awakened were beyond such concerns, and that talk of consequence was for beginners.

Dogen rejects this strongly. The fascicle opens with the story of Baizhang and the fox, the same story that appears in Great Practice, and uses it to make the point as sharply as possible. A teacher who said that a person of great practice does not fall into cause and effect was reborn for five hundred lifetimes as a fox. The correction, that such a person does not obscure cause and effect, ended the suffering.

He then goes through a long list of arguments and quotations to show that denying cause and effect is a heresy. The tone is severe, and he says plainly that people who hold this view are outside the way.

It is among the most direct statements of the link between realization and ethics. If a teaching leads people to believe that their actions do not matter, or that realization excuses harm, this fascicle says that it is a false teaching. For anyone evaluating a spiritual claim, it is an easy line to remember.

Paraphrased lines
Do not deny cause and effect.
A teaching that excuses harm in the name of emptiness is not the buddha way.

Discernment. The clearest lesson in the late work: no realization can place anyone above consequence.

08Sanji gōKarma of the Three Times

Main lessonActions bear fruit in this life, the next, or later. Do not assume that anything escapes.

Karma, in the traditional account, ripens at different times. Some actions produce their results in this very life, some in the next, and some in lives further off. Dogen’s short fascicle explains this and presents it as an essential teaching.

The reason he stresses it is related to the problem of apparent injustice. A good person suffers while a bad person prospers, and an observer concludes that actions do not matter. Dogen’s answer is that the results are not always visible on a short timescale. The fruit is certain, though its timing is not.

He also uses the teaching to caution against complacency. A person who has done something harmful and is not yet suffering for it should not conclude that nothing will come of it. A person who has done good and received no reward should not give up.

This is not meant to be mechanical or punitive. It is a way of taking responsibility seriously over the long run. In a culture that rewards quick results, the idea that effort and consequence play out over a long time is a useful corrective. For work that matters, whether creative or professional, the right approach is to do it properly regardless of the immediate reaction.

Paraphrased lines
Some karma ripens now, some next, some later.
Not seeing the result yet is not the same as there being no result.

Discernment. Dogen asks for a long view on conduct. Short-term outcomes are a poor guide to what is right.

09ShibaFour Horses

Main lessonRespond at the shadow of the whip. Do not wait for pain to start practicing.

A passage in the early sutras describes four kinds of horses. The best starts moving at the mere shadow of the whip. The second moves when the whip touches its hair. The third when it touches the skin. The fourth only when the pain reaches the bone. The image was used to describe different kinds of students, ranging from those who respond to the faintest hint to those who need a serious crisis.

Dogen’s fascicle takes up the image and applies it to practice. The teaching of impermanence is the whip, and the question is how soon one responds. A person who sees that life is passing, and that death can come at any time, can begin practicing at once. A person who waits for illness, loss, or disaster before starting is the fourth horse.

His emphasis is encouraging and urgent at the same time. All four horses are capable of running, and no one is excluded. But the sooner one begins, the better. Delay is dangerous because it assumes there will always be another day.

This is a good fascicle to think about whenever work is being postponed until things settle down. The usual state of things is that they do not. A person who responds to the shadow of the whip is not anxious. They are simply awake to how short the time is.

Paraphrased lines
The best horse moves at the shadow of the whip.
Do not wait for the pain to reach the bone.

Discernment. A teaching that tells you to start later is suspect. Dogen’s urgency is plain and kind.

10Shizen bikuThe Monk of the Fourth Dhyana

Main lessonA deep meditative state is not realization. Mistaking one for the other is a catastrophic error.

The fascicle retells a story from the Buddhist sources about a monk who reached the fourth dhyana, a state of deep meditative absorption in which breathing seems to stop and the mind becomes extremely still. The monk took this as proof that he had attained the final fruit of the path, and he told others so.

At the time of his death, according to the story, he saw the signs of rebirth that did not match what he had expected. In confusion and anger he concluded that the Buddha’s teaching was false, and the consequence of that mistake was a descent into a terrible realm. His error was a double one: he had taken a high state of concentration for liberation, and he had then denied the truth when reality did not conform.

Dogen uses the story as a warning to practitioners and teachers. Intense experiences, however profound, are not self-validating. They can be produced by a skilled practice and still not be what they seem. The only protection is accurate knowledge of the tradition and a willingness to be corrected.

This is one of his sharpest teachings on discernment, and it applies to everyone who pursues unusual states. Beware of any claim of attainment that rests mainly on an experience, especially one that the claimant cannot test against the teachings or a reliable teacher.

Paraphrased lines
A state of deep stillness is not the same as liberation.
Claiming an attainment you do not have is a danger to yourself and others.

Discernment. The most direct warning in the work against mistaking an experience for realization, and against teachers who claim attainments they have not tested.

11Ippyakuhachi hōmyō monThe One Hundred and Eight Gates of Illumination

Main lessonThere are many entrances to the one way. Each gate is the whole of it.

The text is distinctive in the Shobogenzo, and it was lost for centuries. It survives in a single manuscript discovered only in 1936, which is why it is sometimes left out of older editions. It is built as a litany, working through one hundred and eight gates of illumination.

The number has a long association in Buddhism with the hundred and eight defilements, or the varied forms of delusion. Here the number is turned around, and each gate is a way of entering clarity. Dogen treats the list as a recitation, with each item being a gate that opens onto the whole.

Because it is rarely discussed, a short summary has to stay modest. What can be said is that the text expresses a belief that appears throughout his work: that the way does not have a single narrow entrance. It can be entered through many doors, and each of them is complete. A person who is at one gate is not at a lesser place than a person at another.

The implied attitude is one of generosity in method and strictness in practice. It does not endorse every approach. But it recognizes that the same thing can be approached from many sides, and that insisting on a single entrance as the only legitimate one is unnecessary.

Paraphrased lines
There are many gates, and each is the whole way.
No gate is a lesser place.

Discernment. Dogen is open about entry points and strict about what has to be done once inside.

12Hachi dainin gakuThe Eight Awakenings of Great Beings

Main lessonWant little, be content, seek quiet, be diligent, stay mindful, concentrate, cultivate wisdom, and do not waste time in idle talk.

This is the last fascicle Dogen wrote, in 1253, the year he died. It is based on a teaching traditionally attributed to the Buddha’s final instruction to his disciples, and Dogen presents it as the essence of the way. It describes eight qualities that awakened people practice and that those who want to awaken can cultivate.

The first is having few desires, and the second is being content with what one has. The third is enjoying quiet, a preference for solitude and stillness over agitation. The fourth is diligence, the steady, unhurried effort that keeps going. The fifth is not forgetting mindfulness, staying with what is present. The sixth is practicing concentration, the settled mind of samadhi. The seventh is practicing wisdom, the clear seeing of how things are. The eighth is not engaging in idle talk, a refusal to dissipate attention in frivolous chatter and argument.

Dogen’s closing note carries the force of a farewell. He emphasizes that these are not preliminary teachings but the whole path. If a person practices these eight, they have understood the Buddha’s teaching, and nothing is left out.

For anyone organizing a working life around clarity, this is one of the most usable texts in the collection. It reads like a checklist that can be applied every day. Want less, be content, protect quiet, keep going, pay attention, concentrate, see accurately, and talk less.

Paraphrased lines
Few desires, contentment, quiet, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom, and no idle talk.
Those who want many things have much trouble. Those who want little are at ease.

Discernment. Dogen’s last word is practical and checkable. You can see in a day whether you are doing it.

01BendōwaA Talk on Endeavoring in the Way

Main lessonSit, and let the sitting itself be practice and realization together. This one gate is complete.

Written in 1231, shortly after Dogen’s return from China, Bendowa is his earliest major statement and is often printed alongside the Shobogenzo. It is cast as a series of questions and answers, in which an imagined questioner presses him on the unusual claims he is making.

He opens with a statement of what he is bringing: the buddha dharma transmitted in an unbroken line from Shakyamuni, and above all the practice of zazen, which he calls the right gate. He describes zazen as a self-fulfilling samadhi, an activity that is complete in itself and that does not aim at anything outside the sitting. When a person sits upright, even for a short while, he says, they express the Buddha’s posture with the whole body, and the entire world joins in.

The questions pursue obvious objections. Why only sitting? Why not chanting, incense, bowing, or reading? Do you need to be a monk? Can laypeople awaken? What about the idea that the mind is permanent and the body perishes? To each he gives a firm answer. He rejects the notion of an eternal inner spirit, and he says that those who think they can skip sitting for other practices have not understood what is being offered.

The text also includes his account of his search for a true teacher. It is as much an argument for authenticity as for a method: that the way must be received from someone who has it, and then tested in practice.

Paraphrased lines
Practice and realization are one. Sitting is not a means to awakening, it is awakening taking place.
Even a single period of sitting, upright, expresses the buddhas’ posture and the whole world joins in.

Discernment. The whole treatise is a case for verified transmission and complete practice, set against casual blending and shortcuts.

02ShōjiBirth and Death

Main lessonDo not flee birth and death, and do not cling to them. Let go into the buddha’s way and follow it.

A short text on the problem of birth and death, which is the basic human concern. It is not in Dogen’s own arrangement of the Shobogenzo, but it appears in the traditional Soto editions and has long been read with it. Some scholars question how much of it comes directly from him.

Its main idea is that birth and death, the cycle of ordinary existence, is itself the life of the Buddha. If you try to escape birth and death in order to find the Buddha somewhere else, you lose the Buddha’s life. And if you cling to birth and death as if they were solid and final, you are lost in them. The way is between, or more exactly, in the middle of what is happening.

The text ends with an instruction that has become famous. Throw your body and mind into the house of the Buddha, and let the Buddha act on them. Follow along without effort of body or mind, and without grasping, and you leave birth and death behind and become a buddha. The message is one of trust in practice, not in a plan.

Taken together with the fascicle on total dynamism, it forms a pair about how to face mortality: neither avoidance nor fixation, but taking part fully in what is happening.

Paraphrased lines
Birth and death is itself the buddha’s life.
Do not hate birth and death. Do not cling to them.

Discernment. Dogen refuses both escapism and fixation. The honest posture toward mortality is full participation.

03Yuibutsu yobutsuOnly a Buddha Together with a Buddha

Main lessonRealization is mutual. It is verified by those who have it, and no one confirms themselves alone.

The title is taken from the Lotus Sutra’s statement that only a buddha together with a buddha can fully fathom the true reality of all things. It is a line that Dogen returns to repeatedly, and this fascicle takes it as the main subject.

His claim is that the realization of the dharma cannot be assessed from outside. Someone who has not realized it cannot judge it, and someone who has realized it is not alone. The knowledge is shared and recognized between persons. A buddha knows a buddha, and the transmission is carried in that recognition.

This has implications for how a person should relate to teachers and to their own insight. A student cannot decide for themselves that they have it. A teacher cannot give it to someone who is not ready or willing. Both sides are part of a mutual process that requires time and honesty.

The fascicle also contains a certain humility. If only buddhas fully know buddhas, then those who are still learning should be slow to pass final judgment on others, and quick to accept the check of a teacher who knows more. It is not a doctrine of mystique but of the limits of self-assessment.

It is a good companion to the fascicles on self-verification and face-to-face transmission, and together they make up the core of Dogen’s view about how authenticity is established.

Paraphrased lines
Only a buddha together with a buddha can fully realize the true reality of all dharmas.
No one verifies their own realization alone.

Discernment. The limits of self-assessment are built into the tradition. Authenticity is established between persons, over time.

  • Read one fascicle slowly
